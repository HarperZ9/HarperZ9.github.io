Everything the film says

Next-word prediction

Next-word prediction. One guess, spread over every word.

A language model is first trained on one job: read some words, and say what comes next. Tonight, what that guess looks like, and how it is scored.

A guess is a spread

Take the small model from the attention lesson, give it one word, pylon, and ask what comes next.

It does not answer with one word. It cuts the wheel into a slot for every word, some wider than others: found gets 50 percent of its belief, painted about 9, and the rest is shared out thinner.

We know the rules that wrote these sentences, so we can check it. After pylon, the rules choose found 42 percent of the time. From examples alone, the model got close.

From scores to shares

Inside, the model ends with one score per word. Scores can be any number, even below zero, so they are not yet a share of belief.

Take four words with scores 2, 1, 0 and minus 1. Raise e to each score, which makes every one positive, then divide by their total.

The shares come out 64, 24, 9 and 3 percent. A score one higher means about 2.7 times the share. This step is called the softmax.

The loss is surprise

Then the real next word arrives. Say it was lamp, the word with 64 percent.

The score for the guess is the surprise: minus the log of the share it gave the word that came. Here that is 0.44.

Give the right word half, and the surprise is about 0.69. Give it all of its belief, and the surprise is zero. Give it almost nothing, and the surprise grows without limit. This number is the loss, and training is the work of making it small.

If the model had given the right word every bit of its belief, what would the loss be? (the film pauses here)

The slope of the loss for each score is that word's share, minus one for the word that came. Training steps the other way: the right word's score goes up, and every other score comes down, each by an amount set by its share. We checked that rule against tiny test nudges, and they agree. A version with the minus one left out fails the same check.

Torn between how many?

A loss in logs is hard to picture, so turn it back. Raise e to the average surprise, and you get how many words the guesses are torn between, as if they were all equally likely. That is called perplexity. The same count can be read off a single guess, before any word arrives.

After rerun knocked, the rules allow two words, on or twice, half each: torn between exactly 2. The model's guess there: torn between 2.04.

After tallow found a, the rules allow 10 things, all equally. The model is torn between 10.07, but it leans: it gives kettle 14 percent where the rules give 10.

What context buys

Now score it properly, on 6,144 next words from rows it never trained on, against simpler guessers. The rows are new; the sentences in them are not, because the rules can only make so many.

A blind guess is torn between all 46 words. Counting how often each word appears gets that down to 26.37. Counting which word follows which: 3.60. Which word follows each pair: 2.91.

The attention model: torn between 1.99.

The gap is widest where a word must come back from far earlier, like the cone going on the thing found seven words before. There, counting which word follows which is torn between 7.11 and the model between 1.06. That is what attention is for.

Can any guesser get to torn between one, never surprised at all? (the film pauses here)

The floor

No. Some next words are a coin toss in the rules themselves. Even the rules, scoring their own sentences, are torn between 1.93. That is the floor.

Over 2,000 training steps, the model's loss on those held-out rows fell from 3.84 to 0.69. The floor is 0.66. What is left above it is 0.03.

We can say that only because we wrote the rules. For real text nobody knows exactly where the floor is, so nobody can say how much lower a loss could go.

Goodnight

A guess is a spread of belief over every word. The loss is the surprise when the real word arrives, and training makes the surprise small.

What this lesson leaves out

The held-out rows are new, but the sentences in them all occur somewhere in training: a 46-word grammar can only make so many. Real held-out text is far less forgiving.

We can show the floor only because we wrote the rules; for real text the floor is unknown, so no one can say how much lower a loss could go.
