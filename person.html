Field work, physics, and outside-in engineering

I spent eleven years on a commercial tree crew and worked on the ground for all of it as the rigging specialist. I ran the loads and timing, kept the climber safe when heavy timber let go overhead, and brought limbs down cleanly into tight clearances through silent visual coordination. Every rigging cut is applied physics with immediate physical stakes; over eleven years we maintained an extraordinarily tight safety record.

Tree care was the day job. At night I built software, reversed game engines, traced memory offsets, and wrote shader pipelines to see how real systems behave from the outside. That same outside-in habit drives how I approach AI evaluation and security today: test the live boundary, plant known faults to verify the checker actually catches them, and feed every adversarial finding straight back into stronger defenses.

One public milestone built entirely on my own time is E L D E R . E N B, a lighting and atmospheric rendering preset for Skyrim SE. On Nexus Mods it has crossed one million downloads (1,004,693 total downloads and 179,218 unique accounts as of November 13, 2026). Alongside it, maintainers across 34 external open-source repositories have merged 41 of my pull requests, including DeepEval, Pydantic AI, Datasette, TOMLKit, and Biome.

I operate independently as a Washington sole proprietor. Every repository, release, empirical study, and tool on this site is self-directed engineering work published with reproducible verification paths.

The through-line, craft, and five platforms

My engineering starts from one structural asymmetry: generating plausible text, code, or imagery has become nearly free, while checking whether an output is actually true remains slow and expensive. Whether writing a compiler, debugging a DirectX 12 shader pass, testing an AI agent against planted faults, or measuring how light scatters in dusk and rain, the discipline is identical: separate the proposer from the reference checker, verify the checker against planted failures, and publish null results honestly.

Eleven years in arboriculture shaped how I design software. A tree manages water transport, wind load, compartmentalized decay, and fungal networks without a central manager; a job site stays safe only when scopes match physical limits and anyone on the ground can call a halt without penalty. That attention carries across my broader craft: horticulture and mycology, atmospheric optics and historical color manuals, perfumery (decomposing impressions into volatile top, heart, and base curves), atmospheric drum and bass and Japanese jazz fusion over natural silence, cinema that honors negative space, and mid-century drafting plates paired with precision instruments.

What I stand for

I have a young son who will grow up in a world shaped by agentic AI. Independent verification and public-interest tooling cannot remain optional extras owned solely by the organizations deploying systems at scale.

  • Nothing self-warrantsA model, compiler, or institution should never be the sole judge of its own work.
  • Make checking cheaperLower the cost of verifying claims for ordinary people instead of locking verification behind proprietary walls.
  • Just culture over blameHonest errors lead to learning and a clear path back; only hiding failures corrupts the shared record.
  • Neutral tools, signed essaysPersonal convictions belong in signed first-person writing, while verification and provenance tools apply symmetric rules to every subject, including my own work.
  • Respect for human attentionSoftware should return people to their lives calmer and more capable, never trap them with synthetic urgency or clutter.
