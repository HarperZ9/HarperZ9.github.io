Sourcehood Without a Source: The Arity Gap and the Free Will Debate

Why the free will debate's demand for self-origination was never earned, and what survives when it is dropped.

Zain Dana Harper/ July 2026/ Companion to the research program/ Download PDF

In short. This philosophy paper is about free will, blame and sourcehood, which means being the real author of your own acts. It accepts that every choice is fixed by causes reaching back to before the person was born, and it answers the view that blame is decoration over behavior control: blame is something one person says to another who can answer, and the arity gap names why no one-person description can capture that two-person relation. Start with In plain terms for the idea in everyday words; the numbered sections carry the full argument.

How to read the labels. Notes in parentheses such as “high confidence” say how sure the author is about a source or about one of the paper’s own claims. The References give the same labels in square brackets. “High confidence on the text and the thesis” means the author is sure of what the source says and of its main claim.

Abstract

The free will debate has mostly been run as a search for an origin. It looks for a point where the agent, and not the agent’s past, is responsible for what happens next. Libertarians place that point in an act of will that nothing before it determined. A libertarian holds that free will is real and needs undetermined choices. Hard incompatibilists, following Galen Strawson, say there is no such point, because nothing can be causa sui, the cause of its own existence. A hard incompatibilist denies that free will and real desert exist at all. Desert here means having earned praise or blame and so deserving it. Compatibilists move the point inside the agent’s psychology and hope the move does enough work. For a compatibilist, free will is real and fits with determinism, the view that the state of the world at any moment, together with the laws of nature, fixes every later state.

All three parties share one assumption. Call it the aseity premise. Aseity means self-existence: being the source of oneself and owing one’s being to nothing outside. The aseity premise says that free will, if it exists, must be a kind of self-origination. This paper argues that the premise is a definition slipped in without notice. It was never a finding about what free will requires. It makes the debate impossible to solve from the start.

Drop the premise and the question changes. Sourcehood, being the real author of one’s own acts, is then either impossible or conferred. Conferred sourcehood is inhabited, and it is addressed. Put plainly, it is a standpoint given to you that you occupy and live in, and you do not squeeze it out of yourself.

The paper then names the move it takes to carry the most weight in the whole debate: the arity gap. Arity is the number of argument-places a relation has, the slots for the things it links. A one-place relation needs one thing to fill it. A two-place relation has room for two. P.F. Strawson’s account of the reactive attitudes, everyday responses such as resentment, gratitude and forgiveness, read with care, shows that holding someone responsible is a two-place relation. It runs between an addresser and an addressee. Each stands at a second-personal pole, the position of an “I” speaking to a “you.” The hard incompatibilist wants to reduce that relation to a one-way program of behavior-shaping, a first person acting on a third person.

This paper argues that logical structure blocks the reduction. A one-place vocabulary that fixes a single target, pinning down exactly what a description is about, cannot fix the target of a two-place relation. Chemistry could drop phlogiston, a substance once thought to be released in burning, and keep combustion because oxidation is a one-place property of the burning stuff. The same move fails for holding someone responsible. The second person is the second argument-place of the relation itself, and no predicate, a property of a single thing such as “is flammable,” can be added to a property to stand in for it. The paper grants determinism in full throughout. It withdraws an earlier appeal to chaos theory. It concludes that conferred sourcehood is stronger than libertarianism, because it never took on the debt that libertarianism cannot pay.

In plain terms

Someone borrows money from you and does not pay it back.

You can respond in two ways.

In one, you feel hurt and you blame the person. You say, “You owe me. You wronged me.” You are speaking to them. You expect that they can hear you, answer you, apologize, or make it right. Two people are in this response: you, and the “you” you are speaking to. Each of you can act on the other.

In the other, you stop treating the person as someone to talk to. You decide they are unreliable. You never lend to them again, and you take steps to protect yourself. Here only one person is doing anything. You work on the other person the way you would work on a broken machine, and you adjust how exposed you are to it. The other person never gets to answer you, because their answer is not the point. The point is to manage what they do next.

Now bring in the free will debate. The hard determinist side, which includes the hard incompatibilists named in the abstract below, says the blaming response is a costume worn over the managing one. Underneath, they say, all you do is shape behavior, and the hurt and the blame are only the costume. Take it off, keep the useful management, and you lose nothing. You even gain, because the costume is what lets people be cruel to each other.

This paper answers that the managing vocabulary cannot fully describe the blaming response. The reason lies in its structure. Blame has a second person built into it, a source who can answer back. Management has no place for such a person. Forgiveness shows the gap most clearly. If you forgive the debt, you release a claim, and the other person can even refuse your forgiveness and insist the matter is still open. If you write the person off as a bad bet, nobody is there to refuse anything. The two can look identical from the outside, and what differs is whether a second person is in the picture.

An older knot has to be untied first. Many people think you deserve blame in the full sense only if you made yourself into the sort of person who would skip a debt, and made yourself make yourself, and so on all the way back. The philosopher Galen Strawson states this chain as his Basic Argument. (A different philosopher, P.F. Strawson, gave the two-person account of blame that this paper builds on. The paper cites both.) That demand is impossible. Nothing makes itself out of nothing. So the hard determinist concludes that no one ever deserves anything in that sense. This paper agrees that the demand is impossible. It adds that the demand was the wrong test from the start. You can owe someone an answer because you belong to a community of people who make claims on each other and can respond to those claims. Your standing to be addressed comes from the relationship, so you never have to manufacture it from nothing.

So the paper grants determinism in full. It grants that you are not the ultimate, self-made source of anything. It then shows that this large grant leaves two things in place. One is the two-person structure of holding someone responsible. The other is a real kind of authorship that is given to you and lived in, and that you do not produce out of yourself. The paper calls that given, lived-in authorship conferred sourcehood.

1. The Aseity Premise and Why It Should Not Have Been There

Plainly: every side of the debate quietly demands that a free agent be the source of its own source, and nobody ever earned that demand.

Every major position in the free will debate carries a demand that almost no one states in full. The free agent must be, in some serious sense, the source of the source of its own acts.

Libertarians state this demand openly and try to meet it. They appeal to agent causation, to undetermined self-forming actions (Kane 1996), and to an account of how an undetermined choice still counts as the agent’s choice and does not collapse into noise.

Hard incompatibilists state the demand openly too, and then show that nothing can meet it. Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument leads this camp (1994, Philosophical Studies; high confidence on the text and the thesis). In plain words, it runs: to be responsible for what you do, you must be responsible for how you are, which would require making yourself, which nothing can do. Its six steps are listed in Section 5. Hard incompatibilists conclude that no one ever has true moral responsibility.

Compatibilists try to take the pressure off. They argue that “could have done otherwise,” properly understood, does not require self-origination. Yet most compatibilist accounts, at their deepest point, put back something that behaves like a self-standing, authoritative self. That self stops the backward chain of endorsement. The aseity premise runs under the whole debate, and every position rests on it.

Here is the premise stated bare. An act is free, and the agent is responsible for it in the strong sense, only under one condition. The agent must in the end be the origin of the principles, values and character the act flows from. The agent must also be responsible for being that way, and so on, all the way down. This is Galen Strawson’s own way of setting it out. It is a regress, a chain of “and before that” steps. The chain ends only in something impossible: causa sui, the self-caused self. Nietzsche rightly called this the best self-contradiction that has been conceived so far (Beyond Good and Evil §21; Galen Strawson cites this passage in building the Basic Argument; high confidence on where it comes from). Nothing is causa sui. The chain of causes passes through the agent without stopping, and the agent did not write the starting conditions.

This paper accepts all of that and argues that it answers the wrong question. The argument from premises 1 through 5 of the Basic Argument is sound. It follows that no one is the ultimate, self-originating source of their acts. The Basic Argument reaches no further. Those premises alone settle nothing about three further matters: whether sourcehood of any kind exists, whether responsibility exists, and whether there is free will worth wanting. The argument reaches that further conclusion only by carrying the aseity premise into the conclusion as an unargued definition.

That last step is valid only if responsibility and sourcehood mean ultimate self-origination. They need not mean that. The aseity demand sits packed inside the words “true” and “ultimate” as Galen Strawson uses them, and that packing does all the work in the move from sound premises to the conclusion that nothing matters. Take the demand out and the premises establish only that the will is not causa sui. They establish nothing about whether conferred, inhabited, second-personally constituted sourcehood is real.

The usual deflationary compatibilist move lowers the bar for “free will” and then declares the game won. This paper makes no such move and leaves the definition of free will alone. Its claim is structural: aseity was never the right criterion. Nothing meets the aseity test, and the deeper fault is that it is the wrong test for responsibility. The grounds for that claim come from evidence across traditions and from the arity argument in Section 4.

For now, one point is enough: the aseity demand was always optional. Neither the concept of responsibility, taken by its definition, nor the inner feel of deliberation requires it. Several philosophical traditions that never granted the will aseity show the same thing. In those traditions one can hold agents fully responsible and treat agency as fully real, and sourcehood there is conferred, with no demand that it be originated.

2. Determinism, Granted in Full, Is Orthogonal

Plainly: determinism shows you are not the ultimate source, and this paper grants that. It leaves a separate question untouched: whether the act ran through you. The two questions are orthogonal, which means independent: settling one leaves the other open.

This paper grants the consequence argument (van Inwagen 1983, An Essay on Free Will; high confidence). The argument runs like this. If determinism is true, our acts are the consequences of the laws of nature and the remote past. We had no choice about the laws, and we had no choice about the past. So we have no choice about our acts. The argument is sound given its premises, and this paper does not contest the premises. It takes determinism as given throughout. (Determinism is the view that the state of the world at any moment, together with the laws of nature, fixes every later state.)

So the claim here leaves determinism standing. Determinism is true, and the consequence argument, read correctly, is itself a no-aseity argument. It shows that the agent is not the ultimate source, and that the past and the laws write the future. It does not show, and cannot show, that ultimate self-origination is the only possible sourcehood.

Two things come apart here. The argument removes leeway, the set of alternatives in which the agent could have done otherwise. It leaves sourcehood untouched. Sourcehood asks whether the act that in fact happened flows through the agent as a real source, or bypasses the agent the way an efficient cause, the push that drives a change, passes through inert matter that does nothing itself. Those are different questions. Frankfurt’s cases (1969, Journal of Philosophy; high confidence) are stories in which a hidden controller would have forced the act had the person wavered. The person could not have done otherwise, yet acted on their own. The cases got this distinction right, whatever their final success against the Principle of Alternate Possibilities. That principle says a person is responsible for an act only if they could have done otherwise. For responsibility, what matters is that the act, in the sequence that happened, ran through the agent’s own agency. Whether the agent could have done otherwise is another question. Sourcehood concerns the sequence that happened. What might have happened instead has no bearing on it.

This paper also withdraws, in full, an earlier appeal to chaos theory and indeterminism as any kind of support for free will. The body of work this paper grows from once used the Lorenz attractor and sensitive dependence on initial conditions, the chaos-theory finding that tiny differences in starting conditions grow large, to give the orthogonality thesis “mathematical teeth.” The appeal equivocates: it slides between epistemic unpredictability, which is about what observers can foresee, and constitutive irreducibility, which is about what a system is made of.

A chaotic trajectory can be both determined and irreducible, and irreducibility does not make it a source. Nobody can predict a hurricane’s path from its small-scale starting conditions. That is a fact about observers and the limits of what they can compute, and it says nothing about the hurricane authoring anything.

What the chaos mathematics does legitimately establish is narrower and more modest. It shows one inference to be invalid. The inference runs from “the act is determined” to “the act dissolves into its substrate and can be fully described at the micro-level.” (The substrate is the physical stuff the act is made of.) That is the step from determinism to eliminative reduction, the claim that we can throw away the higher-level description and keep only the lowest-level one. Deterministic systems can be computationally irreducible (Wolfram; moderate confidence on the attribution, high confidence on the phenomenon). Computationally irreducible means there is no shortcut: the only way to learn what the system does is to run it step by step. When that holds, the micro-level description cannot replace the macro-level one, even under full determinism.

This takes away the hard determinist’s reductive premise and adds no positive content to sourcehood. The paper keeps those two things apart throughout: the chaos result clears the space, and the positive content comes from the argument of Sections 4 through 6.

3. Three Traditions That Never Owed the Debt

Plainly: three traditions with no shared metaphysics each held people fully responsible and never demanded that a person be self-made. The demand was optional.

Several traditions reached this structure, ownership without origination, on their own, so it did not begin as a philosopher’s way out of a dilemma built from Western assumptions. Each faced the problem of freedom under total determination, and none granted the aseity premise.

Ash’arī kasb. Classical Ash’arī kalām is a school of Islamic rational theology. It developed a doctrine from al-Ash’arī (died 935) through al-Bāqillānī, al-Juwaynī and al-Ghazālī, and the doctrine holds that God creates (khalq) every act and the human being acquires (iktisāb) it (high confidence on the existence and core of the doctrine; moderate confidence on the finer scholastic disputes over the mechanism of kasb, which the school argued internally). God is the only true cause. The human does not bring the act into being and is not its ontological originator. Yet, where responsibility is concerned, the act is the agent’s. It is the agent’s because of the acquisition: the person whose act it is takes it up from their own standpoint. This is conferred sourcehood in its strict form. It combines total causal dependence with genuine ownership at the locus, the point where the act happens, and it needs no self-origination on the agent’s part. A rival school, the Mu’tazila, pressed for real causal power in the human. The falāsifa, philosophers in the Greek tradition, charged that kasb is a distinction without a difference. The Ash’arī position refuses both. It keeps ownership without origination, and it did so seven centuries before anyone coined the words “compatibilism” and “incompatibilism.”

Yoruba orì. In Yoruba thought, orì is the inner head, the bearer of a person’s destiny (Wande Abimbola; Segun Gbadegesin, African Philosophy, 1991; Barry Hallen; moderate confidence on the philosophical interpretation, which varies by source; high confidence on the broad structure). A person receives it before birth and then lives it out. The destiny is conferred. The lived life is where it is inhabited. Ìwà, character, stays real and answerable within the conferred destiny, and so does effort. Yoruba thought reached this structure wholly outside the Abrahamic frame and without the terms of the Western debate. A shared inheritance does not explain the agreement between Yoruba thought and the Ash’arī doctrine.

The tied camel. A saying of the Prophet, transmitted in al-Tirmidhī, means “tie your camel and then rely on God” (iʿqil baʿīrak wa tawakkal; moderate confidence on the exact chain and grading; high confidence that the saying is canonical and widely transmitted in this sense). The saying counsels full action. It gives no support to passivity, the idea that a decreed world asks you to do nothing. Act with your own hands, to the very end, as if the outcome were entirely up to you, and the decree is at work inside that acting. The acting works with the decree and is how a person inhabits a conferred standpoint inside a determined order.

These three traditions share no metaphysics. Each faced freedom under total determination, and each arrived on its own at ownership without origination. They neither took on the libertarian demand nor dissolved responsibility the way the hard incompatibilist does.

The Western debate took an unusual step and built the aseity demand into the definition of free will. Then, with Galen Strawson and Robert Sapolsky, it found that nothing can meet the demand. It concluded “no free will” where it could have concluded “wrong definition.” The three witnesses show that the definition was always optional. Conferred sourcehood is stronger than libertarianism because it never required the agent to be causa sui, the requirement libertarianism cannot meet.

4. The Arity Gap: The Core Argument

Plainly: holding someone responsible has two people in it, an addresser and an addressee. No one-person description of behavior-shaping can capture the second person.

4.1 P.F. Strawson’s Move, Read Precisely

Plainly: P.F. Strawson showed that holding someone responsible is an address from one person to another and does not follow from any theory about causes.

P.F. Strawson made a bold move in “Freedom and Resentment” (1962, Proceedings of the British Academy; high confidence on the text and the thesis), and most readings still underrate it. The traditional question was whether determinism makes the reactive attitudes unjustified. He declined to answer that question on its own terms. He argued that the reactive attitudes are not conclusions drawn from a prior theory about causation.

The reactive attitudes are our ordinary responses to the quality of another person’s will toward us: resentment, gratitude, indignation, hurt feelings, forgiveness, and the rest. They come built into taking part in shared human life. P.F. Strawson set them against the objective attitude, which views another person as an object to be managed, treated, cured or trained. In the objective attitude you handle the person and do not address them. We can take the objective attitude now and then: toward very young children, toward people in severe psychosis, or in moments of deliberate detachment. But a metaphysical thesis cannot pry loose the commitment to interpersonal relationship that grounds the reactive attitudes.

The standard reading takes this as a naturalized, nearly quietist compatibilism. Quietist here means the view that we should set the metaphysical question aside without answering it. On that reading, responsibility is the practice of holding responsible, and metaphysics cannot revise the practice. That reading is partly right and misses what makes the move structurally interesting. It also makes P.F. Strawson look easy for a hard incompatibilist to refute, because the quietist version seems to change the subject and leave the question open.

Stephen Darwall worked out the precise reading with care (The Second-Person Standpoint, 2006; high confidence). The reactive attitudes encode a second-personal relation. To hold you responsible is to address a demand to you from inside a relationship of mutual accountability. In doing so I presuppose that you have the standing to address me in turn. The “you” in “I hold you responsible” is the second party of an address and names no object in the third person. That is a claim about the logical form of holding someone responsible, and a different claim from the quietist point that practices resist revision.

4.2 The Hard Incompatibilist’s Best Move

Plainly: the hard determinist says blame is a costume over behavior-shaping. Drop the costume, keep the shaping, lose nothing, and gain kindness.

Robert Sapolsky (Determined: A Science of Life Without Free Will, 2023; high confidence on the book’s thesis) and Derk Pereboom (Living Without Free Will, 2001; Free Will, Agency, and Meaning in Life, 2014; high confidence on both) argue as follows. Trace the full causal history of any act, through neurons, hormones, development, culture and evolution. No room is left for desert. So nothing warrants the backward-looking reactive attitudes. (Desert is the idea that a person has earned praise or blame and so deserves it. A backward-looking attitude responds to what was done. A forward-looking one aims at what happens next.)

Their positive proposal is a forward-looking package. Keep the protective functions: quarantine of dangerous people on the public-health model, deterrence, moral formation and reconciliation. Discard basic desert. On this picture, “I hold you responsible” is a folk-psychological wrapper around a signal that shapes behavior. The resentment is the emotional costume on the signal. Take off the costume and nothing of normative value is lost. Something is even gained, because the costume is what licenses cruelty.

This is a serious proposal, and it deserves a serious reply. The reply here does not claim the practice is sacred. It does not claim that metaphysics cannot touch the question. It does not rest on folk psychology being obviously correct. The reply is structural. The reduction fails because it equivocates on arity.

4.3 The Arity Argument, Stated Precisely

Plainly: behavior-shaping is a one-way, one-person operation. Holding someone responsible treats the other as someone who can answer, and a one-place description cannot add that second place back.

Behavior-shaping that looks forward has a first-person to third-person logical form. An agent acts upon a system to change its future outputs. The agent may be me, the state or an institution. The system is you, modeled as a bundle of dispositions that can be changed. The relation is fully specified with no address in it. At no moment do I treat you as a source who could answer me. You have no standing in it to say “you have wronged me” back to me, and your acceptance or refusal of my demand is not the point. In behavior-shaping, your answer is only a lever for the next adjustment.

The reactive attitudes are no emotional decoration on a control signal. Each one registers the other as a source who can be addressed. When I resent you, I am doing more than predicting your future behavior and steering it. I may resent you exactly when steering is hopeless and prediction is certain. I am holding open a relation in which your will toward me is what I respond to.

Forgiveness makes this plain. Forgiving someone is different from ending a deterrence program, and the two come apart. You can deter someone you have not forgiven. You can forgive someone and keep deterring them. To forgive is to release a claim, and the claim presupposed that you were a source against whom a claim could lie in the first place.

So the hard incompatibilist’s plan to replace the reactive attitudes with the forward-looking package reduces nothing. It swaps a two-place relation for a one-place predicate. The relation holds between an addresser and an addressee, each at a second-personal pole. The predicate says: this agent has a profile of dispositions that these interventions can modify. (A predicate is a property attributed to a single thing, such as “is flammable.”) The second person does not sit in the sentence as a predicate added to a third-person property. It is the second argument-place of the relation itself. Remove it and you have removed one of the two relata, the two things the relation holds between. No surface gloss came off something more basic. You are now describing something with a different structure.

This is the arity gap: a one-place vocabulary that fixes a single target cannot fix the target of a two-place relation. (To target-fix a description is to pin down exactly what it is about, with nothing left over.)

4.4 The Phlogiston Objection and Its Failure

Plainly: dropping phlogiston worked because combustion has a one-thing replacement description. Holding someone responsible has none, because its target includes the other person as someone who can answer.

The phlogiston analogy is the most searching form of the hard incompatibilist’s objection. In the objector’s voice it runs like this. I am not simply swapping the reactive attitudes for something else. I am identifying what they were tracking, which is signals that shape behavior. I remove only the wrapper of desert and keep the function. That is exactly how chemistry got rid of phlogiston. (Phlogiston was a substance once thought to be released in burning. The account of oxidation replaced the theory.) Chemists identified what phlogiston-talk was tracking, namely combustion. They replaced the theory and kept the phenomenon. I am doing the same. Your “second-personal remainder” is only a name for your refusal to finish the reduction.

This is the best objection in the literature. It has to be answered on its own terms, without sidestepping.

The answer is that the analogy contains its own refutation. The elimination of phlogiston succeeds because combustion has a target-fixing description, oxidation. That description is available in the third person. It captures everything phlogiston-talk was tracking. Against it, the old theory can be measured and found empty. Oxidation is a one-place property of the burning stuff.

The proposed elimination of the reactive attitudes owes the same thing. It owes a third-personal, forward-looking, target-fixing description of what holding someone responsible was tracking. Against that description, the second-personal “gloss” would have to add nothing. The hard incompatibilist has no such description, and the reason is structural. Current science is not what is missing.

Several phenomena reduce cleanly: map-coloring, combustion, the apparent motion of the sky around a fixed earth. Each is one-place, or already third-personal, and each has a target-fixing description that needs no reference to a second person. The second-personal relation is the one case the move cannot reach. Its target is the addressee as an addressee, the other as a source who can answer. That target is a built-in part of what is being tracked. You cannot strip it off as a theoretical commitment and keep the phenomenon.

The hard incompatibilist has a schema for reduction plus elimination, a general recipe. He has no instance of it for the two-place case. The two-place character of holding someone responsible is no accident, either. It belongs to what holding responsible is.

4.5 The Solipsism Test: Proof of Floating Free

Plainly: imagine a universe with exactly one mind. It has everything a lived standpoint needs, yet nobody in it can hold anyone responsible. So the second person is something extra.

Call the second-personal remainder (R). The claim is that (R) goes beyond anything a one-place account of agency can supply. (R) is more than a restatement of the need for an inhabited standpoint. An assertion will not settle this. It needs a precise demonstration. Here is the test.

Consider a solipsistic universe, one that contains a single mind and so exactly one inhabited standpoint. This universe meets the necessity-of-locus claim in full. There is a standpoint, agency happens there, and that inhabited place is the only place agency could occur. But (R) is false in this universe, because there is no one to address. There is no second pole. No addressee exists whose uptake, the taking-in of an address, is a condition of success for anything. Forgiveness, resentment and the whole range of second-personal relations have no objects there and cannot occur.

A proposition that can be false in a universe where the necessity-of-locus claim is true is not entailed by that claim. It says something strictly extra. So the arity gap is structural, and more than a matter of words. It lies between the one-place necessity of a standpoint and the two-place structure of second-personal address. (R) floats free of the residue.

4.6 Answerability Without Basic Desert

Plainly: showing the relation has two places does not yet show the addressee deserves blame. This part shows that the standing to be addressed comes from the community of address. It does not come from self-origination, so the Basic Argument does not reach it.

Through Section 4.5, the arity argument establishes two things. Holding someone responsible is a two-place relation that cannot be reduced. No one-place, forward-looking vocabulary can target-fix its addressee pole. The argument has not yet settled a further question, and Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument challenges it directly: does either pole of that relation carry basic desert? Basic desert is the warranted, backward-looking attribution of blame or credit, grounded in the agent’s own self-authored character.

The gap is precise. Proving that the relation has two places does not yet prove that the addressee deserves the demand in the sense the regress attacks. Until that gap closes, the Basic Argument still bites. A fully second-personal address could still be aimed at someone who, by the logic of premises 1 through 5, cannot be ultimately responsible for being the kind of person who merits it. This subsection closes the gap. More exactly, it routes around the gap. It separates the normative work the arity argument does from the normative work it has so far borrowed without saying so.

The partition. In recent decades the philosophical literature on moral responsibility has produced a taxonomy. The arity argument can join it and has no need to defeat it. Gary Watson, in “Two Faces of Responsibility” (1996), separates attributability from accountability. Attributability asks whether an act expresses the agent’s own evaluative commitments and is the agent’s own act. Accountability asks whether the agent is properly subject to demands, claims and reactive responses. These come apart. Attributability settles whose act it is. Accountability settles who stands inside the practice of mutual address.

David Shoemaker (2011) refines this into three parts: attributability, answerability and accountability. Answerability concerns the agent’s standing to be called to account, to be asked for reasons, and to be brought into the exchange of justification. Accountability concerns full reactive engagement, including resentment and indignation. The three-part scheme matters here because answerability, as Shoemaker uses it, does not obviously require the agent to be the ultimate, self-originating source of the character the act flowed from. It requires only that the agent can be addressed with a demand for reasons and can respond. Then the second-personal address has the right kind of uptake available to it.

Stephen Darwall’s account of the second-personal standpoint (The Second-Person Standpoint, 2006) supplies the mechanism. Second-personal authority is the standing to address a demand. It does not derive from the addressee’s metaphysical self-origination. The community of address constitutes it. I have the authority to make demands of you because we stand in a relation of mutual accountability. Each of us recognizes the other as a fellow member of the community of people who give reasons. Each is entitled to make claims the other has the standing to answer. The authority is relational and conferred. It rests on no one’s aseity.

This asymmetry is the key point. Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument (1994) targets desert grounded in how one is. It traces that back to how one became that way, and in the end to a self-caused self. Darwall’s second-personal authority does not inherit its normative standing from that chain at all. It earns its standing in the community of address, through mutual recognition among participants who can take up demands. It borrows nothing from the agent’s authorship of their own character.

The demand-to-blame inference. The argument can now go past the partition and take the form of a positive inference. Suppose I address a second-personal demand to you, such as “you wronged me” or “you owe me an account.” The normative standing of that demand does not trace through your causal history to an impossible self-caused origin. It traces through your membership in the community of sources who can be addressed. The demand is warranted because you can receive demands and respond to them. You have the capacity for uptake. You respond to reasons. You have standing inside the community of mutual accountability that P.F. Strawson (1962) described. Whether you originated yourself plays no part.

The Basic Argument’s regress applies to desert grounded in self-origination: true responsibility for what one does requires true responsibility for how one is, and so on back to the impossible causa sui. The regress does not apply to answerability grounded in second-personal standing. That standing does not come from a backward-looking chain through self-authorship. The present structure of address-relations constitutes it. There is no regress to run. No prior fact about the agent’s self-causation justifies the standing. A standing feature of the community of address justifies it, a community both parties take part in.

The inference from second-personal address to warranted blame can now be stated. Take three conditions. You stand in the community of address. You have the capacity to take up demands. You acted from an evaluative orientation that is yours in Watson’s attributability sense. If all three hold, then holding you to account is warranted: resenting, demanding and attributing blame. None of the three conditions requires self-origination. The Basic Argument’s premises 1 through 5 stay sound throughout. They establish that you did not cause yourself. They do not establish that the second-personal demand aimed at you lacks normative standing, because that standing was never borrowed from self-origination.

R.J. Wallace (Responsibility and the Moral Sentiments, 1994) offers a complementary route through the fairness of holding people to expectations. On Wallace’s account, reactive attitudes are fair when the agent has the general capacities for reflective self-control, the ability to grasp moral reasons and be moved by them. Fairness is the normative category that does the work here. It takes its standing from a present, relational fact: the agent has the capacities. It does not take it from a historical fact of self-authorship. The regress does not bite, because Wallace’s condition is synchronic and relational, with nothing diachronic or self-grounding in it. (Synchronic means holding at a single time. Diachronic means holding across time.)

The honest residual bound. This paper defends the partition and the demand-to-blame inference. It does not prove them. Three limits need precise statement.

First, the route through Watson’s attributability and Wallace’s fairness is cleaner for this argument than the route through Shoemaker’s full three-part scheme. Shoemaker explicitly places resentment on the accountability side of his taxonomy. That side carries the backward-looking reactive register, the part most exposed to the desert charge. Suppose the arity argument wants to travel wholly inside Shoemaker’s framework. Then it must accept a limit: the answerability partition alone may not fully shield the accountability face from the Basic Argument. That face covers resentment, indignation and blame in full backward-looking mode. The safer claim is this. Answerability and the attributability conditions together warrant the demand to account and a range of reactive responses. The partition leaves one question open: whether they warrant resentment at the full backward-looking intensity that Strawson’s Section 8 invokes.

Second, the demand-to-blame inference moves from “second-personal standing is constituted in the community of address” to “blame is warranted.” This move is defensible. Blame aimed at an addressable source who acted from their own evaluative orientation, inside a community of mutual accountability, is not an arbitrary imposition. But nobody has shown that this covers everything “warranted” blame requires. The hard incompatibilist will press the point. Grant that the address is structurally appropriate; proportionality and desert in the full sense still require more. The answer is that the arity argument does not need to win that point in full. It needs only to show that the second-personal demand carries normative standing the Basic Argument cannot dissolve. The demand-to-blame inference establishes that.

Third, the argument applies to this paper’s own standing. Second-personal authority is constituted in communities of address and cannot be self-bestowed. So this paper’s claim to have resolved the desert gap is itself open to uptake and refusal by the community of philosophical inquirers. The paper offers the inference to that community. The quality of the argument confers its standing. No authority the paper claims over the Basic Argument by mere assertion confers it. This reflexive application is no weakness. It shows the argument is consistent: the no-aseity structure runs through the argument itself.

High confidence on the partition and the mechanism it relies on. Moderate confidence on the completeness of the demand-to-blame inference as a full reply to the desert charge. The honest bound: the inference is so far unfalsified, and it is not proven unbiteable.

5. The Basic Argument, Granted and Redirected

Plainly: the Basic Argument proves that nothing causes itself, and this paper grants that. The only false step treats that proof as a proof that no sourcehood of any kind exists.

Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument (Philosophical Studies, 1994; also Freedom and Belief, 1986; high confidence) can be stated in six steps. Strawson uses “true” to mark the strong, ultimate kind of responsibility. Section 1 argues that the aseity demand hides in that word.

  1. True moral responsibility for what one does requires true responsibility for how one is: for the mental nature and the principles the act flows from.
  2. True responsibility for how one is requires that one have brought it about that one is that way.
  3. To have brought that about, one must already have been a certain way, and have been responsible for that prior way.
  4. This regresses without end. To stop it, one must be causa sui, ultimately self-originating.
  5. Nothing can be causa sui.
  6. Therefore no one ever has true, ultimate moral responsibility.

This paper agrees with premises 1 through 5 entirely. The aseity-free framework has already denied that anything is self-standing: in existence, in status, in meaning and in agency. Exempting the will alone would give it an exemption nothing else receives. The Basic Argument is the no-aseity thesis applied to the will. Self-causation is aseity. Aseity is impossible. The Basic Argument has the proof of impossibility right. This paper grants van Inwagen’s consequence argument on the same grounds. It too is a no-aseity argument, in another register, and it shows that the agent is not the ultimate source.

The disagreement is precise and confined to one inference. It runs from the true content of step 6, no ultimate, self-originating responsibility, to the conclusion no responsibility and no sourcehood of any kind. That inference is valid only if responsibility and sourcehood mean ultimate self-origination. The aseity demand sits packed inside the words “true” and “ultimate” as Galen Strawson uses them. Take the demand out and premises 1 through 5 establish what they establish: the will is not causa sui. They establish nothing about whether conferred, inhabited, second-personally constituted sourcehood exists. Conferred sourcehood was never a candidate for self-origination. The proof that self-origination is impossible leaves it untouched.

The Basic Argument refutes libertarianism, which did claim self-origination. It refutes Kane’s event-causal libertarianism (The Significance of Free Will, 1996; high confidence). Kane tried to buy back aseity inside a naturalistic frame through undetermined self-forming actions. Grant Kane the indeterminism, and the luck objection still bites (Mele, Free Will and Luck, 2006; high confidence). At the moment it is undetermined, an undetermined choice is no more the agent’s own. If anything it is less the agent’s own, because nothing about the agent settles it.

This is the deep reason aseity is the wrong place to look: self-origination, even if secured, would not give ownership. It would give a coin flip that the agent happens to stand next to. Ownership is conferred and inhabited, and nobody originates it. Kane made a heroic effort to secure aseity, and that effort proved vulnerable to luck. This is perhaps the strongest available evidence that sourcehood was never going to be found in self-origination.

6. Pereboom’s Four Cases and the Structure of Bypass

Plainly: a manipulated puppet differs from an ordinary person in one way. The puppet’s sourcehood was built around address, and the person’s was built through it. A visible manipulator is not what makes the difference.

Pereboom’s four-case argument (Living Without Free Will, 2001; high confidence) is the strongest argument the hard incompatibilist has. It works like this. In Case 1, neuroscientists created Professor Plum moments ago. They manipulate him in real time so that he reasons and acts egoistically, and the process ends in a murder. Plum meets every standard compatibilist condition: reasons-responsiveness, second-order endorsement and guidance control. (Reasons-responsiveness is the capacity to register reasons and act on them. Second-order endorsement is wanting to have the wants one acts on. Guidance control is the agent’s own mechanism steering the act.) Yet, intuitively, Plum is not responsible. He is a puppet, whether or not his inner states meet the compatibilist criteria.

Cases 2, 3 and 4 remove the outside manipulators step by step. Case 2 uses programming done in advance. Case 3 uses a deterministic upbringing engineered to produce these dispositions. Case 4 is ordinary determinism. Pereboom argues that each case is like the one before it in the ways that matter. If Case 1 produces no responsibility, and Case 4 is relevantly similar, then no determined agent is responsible.

The reply here rests on the arity argument. What separates Case 1 from Case 4 lies elsewhere than in local dynamics, and Pereboom is right that those can be matched. The mere presence of visible manipulators does not separate them either. The difference is whether the agent’s sourcehood was constituted through the second-personal community or bypassed it. Manipulation, in the morally relevant sense, is shaping that bypasses the address.

The neuroscientists of Case 1 operate on Plum in the first-to-third mode. They do not address him. They make no demand of him. They do not stand in mutual accountability with him in a way that would make his uptake of their address a condition of success for anything. Ordinary formation runs through the second-personal. People address a child. They hold the child responsible for the quality of its will toward others. They draw the child into the community of reactive attitudes. The child takes up principles as its own in dialogue with other sources (Fischer and Ravizza, Responsibility and Control, 1998; high confidence on this mechanism).

The opponent will press the point. “Through the community of address” and “by bypass,” the opponent says, mark places on a gradient, and the gap between them is one of degree. It is a difference in causal route, and every step from Case 1 to Case 4 differs in causal route. This is the sharpest objection available, and it has a precise answer. A smooth variation in the degree of second-personal constitution fits with a categorical difference at the limit. Responsibility comes in degrees exactly because second-personal constitution comes in degrees. Cults and coercive control are hard cases because they partly bypass the community of address. The theory predicts this and has no reason to be embarrassed by it.

This paper grants that “the cases shade into each other.” The opponent needs something else: “there is no point at which second-personal constitution reaches zero while the dynamics stay matched.” Such a point exists. It is Case 1, authorship from outside in real time, where the community of address contributes nothing to this act’s sourcehood. A continuous function can still take the value zero. A dimmer switch is continuous, and it still has an off position.

The criterion that sorts the cases does not beg the question. It is the arity argument, fixed on independent grounds. Taking up is the relation in which the agent occupies the second pole of an address. The agent has the standing to answer and to refuse, and the agent’s uptake is part of the act’s conditions of success. Installing is the first-to-third operation, in which the agent’s uptake is no condition of success. Linguistics, developmental psychology and the philosophy of indexicals all attest this distinction before any of the parties reach the free will question. (Indexicals are expressions like “I,” “you” and “here,” whose reference depends on who is speaking to whom.)

Apply the distinction to ordinary child development. A child who does not take up the address is merely conditioned. That child has not been formed, and ordinary talk marks the difference. We tell raising a child apart from training a dog by the second-personal uptake condition. Now apply it to Case 1. The manipulators get the murder whether or not Plum answers anything. That asymmetry, whether uptake is a condition of success, is the criterion. It is not drawn from the conclusion it sorts.

7. Compatibilism, the Regress, and What Conferred Sourcehood Adds

Plainly: ordinary compatibilism slips in a small self-made self to stop the backward chain. Conferred sourcehood denies that the chain needs a self-made stopping point at all.

Standard compatibilism appears in Frankfurt’s hierarchical account (1971, Journal of Philosophy; high confidence) and in Fischer and Ravizza’s guidance control. It holds that an act is free when it flows from the self in the right way. A first-order desire is endorsed by a second-order volition, and a reasons-responsive mechanism that the agent owns produces the act. (A first-order desire is a want for something. A second-order volition is a want about which first-order want moves you.) This is real progress. The Frankfurt cases, whatever their standing against the Principle of Alternate Possibilities, place the question correctly in the actual sequence, the sequence of events that happened, and away from counterfactual alternatives.

Ordinary compatibilism still carries a debt it cannot pay. Watson and Velleman press it as the regress of identification (cited in substance as the source of this critique; high confidence on the attribution of the regress pressure). Why is the second-order volition authoritative? Because the agent identifies with it. And the third-order one? At some point the identification must simply settle. That settling looks like a small aseity, a point where the buck stops in the self as an unexplained identifier. Ordinary compatibilism tends to install an authoritative self to halt the regress, and then gives no account to pay for it.

Conferred sourcehood parts ways here, and the parting is its whole substantive contribution to the compatibilism literature. It does not halt the regress in the self. It denies that the regress needs a self-standing end point at all. The buck does not stop in the agent as an uncaused identifier. It stops at a standpoint that is itself conferred. That standpoint is caused, constituted and re-enacted. Its authority is bestowed and inhabited, and nothing about it is self-generated.

Christine Korsgaard (Self-Constitution, 2009; high confidence) captures the structural point. Agency is the continuous activity of constituting oneself as an author. It is no standing property of a self that already exists. The “self” is not a fixed point that the endorsement comes from. The self is the endorsing activity, re-enacted each time. It rests on nothing more self-standing than anything else in a world without aseity. So conferred sourcehood is something other than a version of compatibilism. It fits with compatibilism’s best insights, and it declines compatibilism’s deepest evasion.

8. Forgiveness, Resentment, and the Evidence of the Second Person

Plainly: forgiving someone and writing them off can look identical from outside. Being managed hurts in a different way from being addressed. Both facts reveal a second person that the shaping account cannot capture.

Two more phenomena light up the arity argument from different angles.

Forgiveness. The hard incompatibilist can model forgiveness as a reset of dispositions. The reset restores the expected value of cooperation after someone defects. Keeping up resentment is costly, and ending it signals that the channel for cooperation is open again. This functional story is real and it has substance. But look at what it leaves out. It predicts the behavior and the signals of “I have forgiven you,” and it misses the directed character of forgiveness as an act.

I can reset my dispositions toward you, reopen the channel and stop the loop that keeps my resentment going, all without forgiving you. I can simply stop caring. I can write you off as a hazard that is now contained. I can recalculate on my own, for my own benefit, and never address you at all. The behavior and signals of “I have forgiven you” and of “I have written you off as no longer worth resenting” can be made identical. What differs is whether I released a claim against a source to that source. Forgiveness has an addressee who can refuse it. Dropping a maintenance cost has no addressee to refuse anything.

The asymmetry is on display, and it does not rest on assertion. “I forgive you” requires a “you” who could reply, “I do not accept your forgiveness.” That reply has normative consequences. It keeps the claim alive and registers a standing grievance. “I have stopped maintaining the resentment loop” has no counterpart for any of this. The grammar of the practice shows the second-personal structure.

The objective stance as a demotion in status. P.F. Strawson noted that when someone moves to the objective attitude toward a person, it feels like a demotion in that person’s status. The objective attitude treats them as a system to be managed and stops treating them as a source to be addressed. The hard incompatibilist can explain why this feels bad. On the shaping account, a move from the participant protocol to the management protocol is a loss of status that matters for the fitness of a social primate. Of course it registers as a loss. The prediction matches well.

Notice what it predicts, though. It predicts the valence, that the move feels bad. It does not predict the content, what the hurt is about. The shaping account runs: moved to management, so a loss, so it feels bad. It does not predict that the loss will show up as a withdrawal of standing to answer. The report people give is “you are treating me as something that cannot answer you.” It is not “you have lowered my expected cooperative value.” These are different intentional contents, and people moved to the objective stance report the first. The patient says, “stop managing me and talk to me.” The prisoner’s grievance is “you treat me as if I cannot answer,” and not “you have predicted my behavior.”

The second-personal account predicts that specific content, and it does so independently of the free will question. The shaping account predicts only the valence. It has to treat the content as confabulation, an after-the-fact story the mind tells that does not track the real cause. That content is the remainder. Linguistics, the philosophy of indexicals and developmental psychology’s account of the second person all attest it independently, before anyone reaches the question of free will.

9. The Occasionalism of Agency: A Narrowed Claim

Plainly: the paper withdraws an old, strong claim that authorship can change with no physical change. What remains is smaller: authorship does not reduce to the agent’s state at a single instant, because it depends on relational history.

The body of work this paper is drawn from develops a companion claim, the “occasionalism of agency.” It holds that authorship drains away and returns in ways that do not always track a difference in dynamics. (Occasionalism, in its classical use, is the view that no created thing has real causal power. Here it names the claim that authorship can shift without an underlying dynamical shift.) This paper withdraws that claim as originally stated.

The framework is physicalist: it holds that everything real is physical. Under its own physicalism, every real psychological transition is a real dynamical transition. The addict who watches herself relapse is going through a real dynamical change. So is the dissociated subject who watches herself act. Frankfurt’s unwilling addict (1971; high confidence) is moved by a will she does not endorse, and she is in a different dynamical state from the integrated agent. Internal Family Systems distinguishes being part-led from being Self-led, led by a part of the mind or by the Self. If that distinction tracks something real, it tracks a real difference in network configuration. The original claim, that loss of agency does not always track a dynamical difference, is false. The paper concedes the falsity.

A narrower proposition survives. Ownership of an act is not fixed by the agent’s local synchronic dynamical state, its state at a single instant, and does not reduce to it. Two episodes can match in the agent’s instantaneous dynamical profile and still differ in authorship. They can differ because relational and historical physical facts constitute authorship. The relevant fact is whether the mechanism was taken up through the second-personal community of address or installed by bypass. Such facts do not show up in the instantaneous state.

Ownership supervenes on the total physical facts, so there is no dualism. It does not supervene on the local dynamics, so there is no reduction to dynamics. (To say ownership supervenes on the physical facts is to say there can be no difference in ownership without some difference in those facts.) This is a weaker claim, and the paper states it as the weaker claim. Its only clean witness is the manipulation case. So it depends on the arity argument and does not rest on independent evidence. It is stated at moderate confidence. It depends on the second-personal remainder and does not corroborate it.

The narrowed claim matters in one concrete place. Pereboom’s four-case argument holds the structural and dispositional conditions fixed and varies the etiology, the causal history. On its own terms it cannot also hold fixed the second-personal standing, because second-personal standing is not a structural or dispositional fact. That is the gap the arity argument opens. The narrowed occasionalism claim observes that relational and historical physical facts fill the gap. Nothing supernatural fills it.

10. Conferred Sourcehood: The Verdict

Plainly: drop the aseity demand and you are not left with nothing. You are left with authorship that is given, inhabited and two-place, and no one-place vocabulary can eliminate it.

The argument of this paper can be put together into one structure.

The aseity premise, the demand that free will consist in self-origination, is a smuggled assumption. It is not an analytic necessity, true by the meaning of the words. Dropping it leaves no vacuum. It leaves conferred sourcehood. The standpoint is caused, constituted, addressed and re-enacted. The authorship is inhabited and is not generated from within. The self is, as Korsgaard has it, the continuous activity of constituting an author, and not an object that already exists and whose endorsements can be read off.

Conferred sourcehood is not a deflationary position. It does not lower the bar for what responsibility requires. It questions whether anyone ever described the bar correctly. The three witnesses from other traditions show that ownership without origination is a robust structure, reached independently more than once. It is no evasion. The arity argument shows what the hard incompatibilist’s plan does when it replaces the reactive attitudes with forward-looking management. The plan eliminates, and it reduces nothing. It eliminates the second argument-place of a two-place relation, and no one-place predicate, however finely calibrated, can do that job.

Here is the proposal stated exactly. Holding someone responsible is a two-place relation. Its second relatum is an addressee as an addressee: a source who can answer, against whom a claim can lie, whose refusal of forgiveness keeps the claim alive, and whose uptake of the address is part of what makes the address succeed. No third-personal, forward-looking, one-place description of behavior-shaping captures that second relatum. The eliminativist owes such a description. The structure means it cannot be supplied, because the second argument-place is a relatum and not a predicate to be stripped away. It can only be included or discarded. Discard it and you have not refined holding responsible. You have abolished it and made something else. That something else may be useful. It is not the same thing.

The paper grants determinism in full throughout. It grants the consequence argument, and it grants the Basic Argument through premise 5. Together, determinism and the Basic Argument establish that the agent is not the ultimate, self-originating source of anything. That is true and important. They do not establish, and cannot establish unless the aseity premise is smuggled back in at the conclusion, that there is therefore no sourcehood, no responsibility and no second-personal standing. Conferred sourcehood claims none of the things the hard incompatibilist successfully refutes. It claims only what the arity argument shows no one-place vocabulary can refute. That is the two-place structure of an address, which cannot be reduced. It is the remainder that forgiveness displays and that the solipsism test proves does not reduce to the necessity of a locus, the bare need for a standpoint.

Verdict on the argument. The arity gap is the structural impossibility of target-fixing a two-place relation with a one-place vocabulary. It passes the test for substantive independence. It is falsifiable. The argument fails if someone supplies a complete forward-looking redescription that captures the second-personal differences with nothing left over. Those differences are forgiving while deterring versus deterring without forgiving, releasing a claim versus dropping a cost, and being owed an account versus predicting behavior. Nobody has supplied such a description. The eliminativist has not bitten the bullet. Nobody has proven that the bullet cannot be bitten. The paper keeps that distinction throughout, and it is the honest bound.

The argument would be insincere if it exempted the page it is printed on. Authority, like sourcehood, cannot be self-bestowed. A body of work that declares itself canonical by its own say-so makes the same aseity error the argument was written to expose. Authority is conferred and relational. Like sourcehood, it is constituted in a second-personal community that can answer back and withhold uptake. The argument applies to itself, and it would not be serious otherwise.
