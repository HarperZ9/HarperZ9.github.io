Self-Given Is Not Self-Grounding: Why Perspectival Aseity Dissolves to Seity

Consciousness is known from itself. That fact alone does not show that it owns its own existence.

Zain Dana Harper July 2026 AI-assisted Companion to the research program Download PDF

In short

Several well-known philosophies hold that consciousness is the one thing that exists from itself, and this paper tests the five strongest versions of that claim. It argues that four of them describe something smaller, an ownerless experience known directly from the inside that still depends on outside conditions; the fifth, from the Indian school Advaita Vedānta, claims self-grounding only for an ultimate witnessing awareness, and at that level the question stays open. The difference matters because mixing the two ideas turns a fact about what we know into a claim about what exists.

Key terms

Self-givenness
Being known from itself. An experience is self-given when it shows up for you directly, and no second observer has to confirm it.
Self-grounding
Existing from itself. A thing is self-grounding when its existence comes from nothing else and depends on nothing else.
Aseity
The classical name for self-grounding, from the Latin a se, “from oneself.”
Perspectival aseity
The claim that the first-person point of view, the “I” that has experiences, has aseity.
Seity
What is left when aseity is denied to something that is still not determined from outside the way a stone is. It is an occurrence that is self-given and still depends on conditions. It also has no owner behind it: no lasting, separate self has the experience, just as the “it” in “it is raining” names no one who does the raining.
Empirical level
The everyday level: particular things and experiences as they arise in the world.
Ultimate level
The ground of all arising. Advaita Vedānta places its claim here, at the bare awareness in which all experience appears.
Conferred or conventional order
The ordinary, conditioned world, where each thing receives its existence from conditions outside it. The author develops the idea in Conferred Existence.
Phenomenal, phenomenology
Phenomenal means about how experience feels from the inside. Phenomenology is the study of experience as it is lived, from the first person.

In plain terms

Think of a candle flame. The flame is hot, and nothing outside it adds the heat. No second fire is needed to make it hot. Heat is part of what a flame is. In that sense the heat is “self-given”: it comes with the flame and needs no outside help.

Now ask where the flame comes from. It burns because there is fuel: wax, a wick and air. Take the fuel away and the flame is gone. So the flame does not own its existence. Its existence depends on conditions outside it. In that sense the flame is not “self-grounding.”

A thing can carry its own character, the way a flame carries its heat, and still depend on the world for the fact that it exists at all. Self-givenness and self-grounding are two separate properties. Both feel like a kind of independence, and that is why they are easy to confuse.

Now put your own experience in place of the flame. When you feel or think something, the experience shows up for you directly. No second inner observer has to certify that it is happening. In one narrow way, you cannot be wrong that experiencing is going on right now. That is the self-givenness of consciousness. It is real, and this paper never denies it.

The tempting next step runs like this: my experience is known from itself and cannot be doubted, so my conscious self must be the one thing that grounds its own existence. That step is the mistake this paper is about. It uses the flame’s heat to prove that the flame needs no fuel.

Five major traditions take the step or build the case for it, each in its own way. Descartes, writing in 1641, says “I think, therefore I am” and treats the thinking “I” as a rock-bottom certainty. Galen Strawson, a contemporary philosopher, makes the strongest modern case for a real, concrete subject of experience, the case the step would lean on, though he stops short of taking the step himself. The Indian doctrine of self-luminosity says consciousness lights itself, like a lamp. The German philosopher Fichte, writing in the 1790s, says the I sets itself up, or “posits” itself, absolutely and from nothing prior. Advaita Vedānta points to a witnessing awareness that cannot be denied, and concludes that it must be self-established.

The paper examines each one closely. The first four end the same way: each proves that experience is self-given and stops before proving that the conscious subject owns its existence. Three thinkers, far apart in place and time, made this same correction. Lichtenberg, an eighteenth-century German writer, said the certainty covers only “there is thinking.” Descartes’ argument, the cogito (Latin for “I think”), does not entitle anyone to say “I think.” Kant, in 1781, showed that the bare “I think” attached to all my thoughts does not prove a self-standing soul underneath them. Candrakīrti, a seventh-century Indian Buddhist philosopher, said a lamp does not light itself; it simply burns, and it burns on oil.

Under a fair reading, then, four of the five shrink from aseity, owning your own existence, to seity: a real, self-given happening that still depends on conditions. Seity also has no owner behind it, meaning no lasting, separate self that has the experience.

The fifth tradition is different. Advaita Vedānta agrees that the everyday self is dependent. It claims self-grounding only for the ultimate witness, the bare awareness in which all experience appears. The flame picture does not obviously apply there. It describes particular things arising inside the world, and Advaita’s claim concerns the ground of all arising. The rival Buddhist school, Madhyamaka, replies that even this ultimate awareness is empty of self-grounded existence. Neither side has defeated the other. The paper makes no pretense of settling the matter and sets that one dispute aside as open.

The result is small and stable. In the ordinary, conditioned world, no conscious subject grounds its own existence. Self-givenness does not amount to self-grounding. The question stays open in one place only, the ultimate level, where a serious debate continues and neither side has been defeated.

Abstract

Can consciousness be the one reality that grounds itself, the single thing that owns its existence without receiving it from anything else? Five philosophical traditions make this claim in their strongest published forms. They are Descartes’ cogito, Galen Strawson’s robust phenomenal subject, the Indic doctrine of svaprakāśa (self-luminosity) as developed through Dan Zahavi’s account of pre-reflective self-awareness, Fichte’s self-positing absolute I, and Advaita Vedānta’s self-established Ātman, the self. The paper tests each tradition against one distinction, between self-givenness and self-grounding. Self-givenness is an epistemic property. A thing is self-given when it is known from itself. Doubt of a certain kind cannot touch it. It shows itself by its own light, and its character is built in, owing nothing to relations with other things. Self-grounding is an ontological property. A thing is self-grounding when its very existence is ungenerated and depends on nothing else. A second test uses one picture. Heat is built into a flame, and the flame still depends on fuel. A built-in character does not prove that a thing owns its existence. The paper argues that the first four traditions, taken at full strength, deliver self-givenness and stop short of self-grounding. Read that way, they are correct about something narrower than they claim. Each is an instance of seity: ownerless occurrence, self-given and still dependent. Three well-known critiques reach this result from different starting points, so the result does not rest on any single theory. They are Lichtenberg’s correction of the cogito (from ich denke, I think, to es denkt, there is thinking), Kant’s First Paralogism, his diagnosis of a faulty step from “I think” to a soul, and Candrakīrti’s lamp. The fifth tradition, Advaita Vedānta, forms a real and undefeated standoff at the ultimate level. Any framework that denies aseity has to set that standoff aside without claiming to refute it. The paper ends by stating its exact limit. There is no aseity in the conferred or conventional order, the ordinary, conditioned world in which each thing receives its existence from conditions outside it. Whether the ultimate ground is a self-luminous aseity is the open question between Advaita and Madhyamaka, set aside and left unsettled.

Keywords: aseity, seity, self-givenness, self-luminosity, cogito, Advaita Vedānta, perspectival consciousness

1. Introduction

Plainly: philosophers have long felt a pull to treat consciousness as the one thing that grounds itself. This paper argues that what feels like self-grounding is only self-givenness.

Philosophy of mind and metaphysics have long been tempted to treat consciousness as the one exception to dependence: the single reality that grounds itself and receives its being from nowhere else. The temptation has reasons behind it. First-person experience presents itself with unusual force. From the inside it cannot be doubted the way external objects can. It seems to carry its own warrant and to be, in some sense, its own evidence. The claim runs from Descartes’ foundational cogito ergo sum (I think, therefore I am) through Fichte’s absolute self-positing, and from the Vedāntic sākṣin (the witness) to the pre-reflective self-awareness that contemporary phenomenologists study. Each time it takes a recognizable shape. Consciousness, or the first-person subject, is held to be the self-grounding thing, the one entity whose being hangs on nothing else. The claim reaches past immediacy in knowledge.

This paper calls that claim perspectival aseity. Aseity is a term from classical theology and metaphysics. It names the property of existing a se, Latin for “from oneself.” A thing has aseity when it exists from and through itself, ungenerated, and depends on no other entity for its existence. Perspectival aseity is the claim that the first-person perspective has exactly this property. Traditions give that perspective different names, such as the conscious subject or the knowing center. Under any of those names it is said to exist from and through itself.

The thesis is direct. Examined at its five strongest published points, perspectival aseity delivers seity and falls short of aseity. Seity is a narrower property. It names what is left when we deny aseity to something that is still not determined from outside in the plain way a stone is. Such a thing is self-given. It has no owner, and it occurs. Its existence is received or dependent, even when the dependence is unusual or hard to describe. Examined closely and no longer suppressed, the apparent perspectival aseity turns out to be seity: an ownerless occurrence, self-given and not self-grounded.

Sections 3 through 7 take the five traditions one at a time, each at its strongest, and apply the master distinction and the master image. Section 8 presents the result on two levels. At the empirical, or everyday, level, the apparent perspectival aseity dissolves into seity. One tradition, Advaita Vedānta, produces a real standoff at the ultimate level, and that standoff must be set aside without being refuted. Section 9 states the bound precisely. Section 10 concludes.

A note on method. This paper does not argue that consciousness is unimportant, derivative or reducible to something else. The self-givenness of experience is real and philosophically significant. By the self-givenness of experience the paper means its intrinsic first-person character, its immunity to certain forms of doubt and its non-relational intimacy, a closeness to you that owes nothing to a relation with anything else. The argument here is restricted. Self-givenness, however real, is a different property from self-grounding. Each of the five traditions moves from self-givenness to self-grounding in some form, and that move is not warranted. The distinction matters because merging the two properties produces inflated conclusions about what exists from premises that concern only what we know.

2. The Master Distinction and the Flame

Plainly: self-givenness means being known from itself, and self-grounding means existing from itself. A flame shows that a built-in character does not prove independent existence.

Self-givenness is an epistemic property, a property that concerns knowing. A thing is self-given when it is known or warranted from itself. It needs no outside certification to count as manifest. Its presence cannot be doubted in some characteristic way. Its character is intrinsic: built in and not made up out of relations to other things. Several traditions describe forms of this. The Indic tradition describes self-luminosity, a cognition that reveals its own occurrence and needs no second cognition to reveal it. Sydney Shoemaker gives a version from analytic philosophy, the logic-and-language tradition that dominates English-speaking philosophy: immunity to error through misidentification. It holds that first-person reports about your own mental states cannot go wrong in the particular way third-person reports can. Dan Zahavi, drawing on Husserl and Sartre, describes pre-reflective self-awareness. On his account experience is always already given as mine, and that “mine” sits in the very structure of experiencing. No later act of self-attribution adds it. All of these are real phenomena, and the paper does not dispute them.

Self-grounding is an ontological property, a property that concerns being or existence. A thing is self-grounding when it depends on nothing else for its existence. The scholastic tradition called this causa sui in the most literal sense, cause of itself. Its existence is not conferred by, derived from or sustained by any other entity or condition. This is aseity in the strict sense, and it is a strong claim. Classical theism gives it to God alone. Spinoza’s monism, the view that only one substance exists, gives it to that single substance. Claiming it for the first-person subject, or for consciousness, is a large claim.

The error this paper tracks is the confusion of these two properties. The slide from self-givenness to self-grounding tempts people because self-givenness does look like a kind of independence. Self-given experience needs no outside epistemic warrant. It is not made up of relations. It escapes the uncertainty that attaches to knowledge of the external world. Epistemic independence of that kind is compatible with ontological dependence. A thing can present itself from itself and still depend on outside conditions for its very existence.

The flame image makes this exact. Heat is intrinsic to a flame in a clear sense. The flame is hot through no relation to anything outside it, since heat is part of what being a flame is. You could call the flame’s heat self-given. Nothing imports it from outside, and it is present in the very occurrence of the flame. The flame still depends on fuel for its existence. Take away the fuel and the flame stops existing altogether. So a flame’s heat can be intrinsic while its existence depends on fuel.

The image returns in each of the five traditions as the diagnostic test. Wherever a tradition offers a consciousness whose character is intrinsic, self-luminous or self-given, one question follows. Does that built-in character establish that the thing’s existence depends on nothing? In every case except the last, the answer is no.

3. The Cogito: Self-Givenness Without a Self-Grounding I

Plainly: Descartes’ “I think, therefore I am” proves that thinking is happening and stops there. A self-standing “I” that owns the thinking is a further claim, and the argument leaves it unproved.

Descartes’ cogito ergo sum is the prototype of perspectival aseity. It is the most familiar attempt to derive a self-grounding first person from the structure of doubt. The argument is well known. You cannot doubt that you are doubting without doing some doubting. Doubting is a kind of thinking. Wherever there is thinking, the argument runs, there is an I that thinks. So I am cannot be overturned, and the thinking I stands as the one indubitable reality. The apparent aseity lies in one move, the move from indubitability to existence. The I appears self-given. It also seems to establish its own existence through the very attempt to doubt it away.

The decisive critique of this move came later. Georg Christoph Lichtenberg made it in the late eighteenth century, in the Sudelbücher (the Waste Books). His observation is compact and exact. The cogito licenses es denkt, German for “there is thinking” or “it thinks.” It does not extend to ich denke, “I think.” The indubitable datum is that thinking is occurring now. It does not follow that a subject owns the thinking, a substantial I that persists and stays identical with itself. Descartes needs exactly that further inference, and the cogito does not supply it. The datum is impersonal. It registers an occurrence and no owner of the occurrence. The grammatical subject “I” in cogito may be nothing more than the formal requirement that German and Latin verbs take a subject. On that reading it carries no more ontological weight than the “it” in “it is raining.”

Kant reaches the same point by a different route, in the First Paralogism of the Critique of Pure Reason. A paralogism is a piece of reasoning that looks valid and is not. The First Paralogism, as Kant analyzes it, is the invalid step from the formal unity of apperception to a substantial, simple, numerically identical soul, one that stays one and the same thing over time. Apperception here is the “I think” that must be able to accompany all my representations. Kant’s point is that this “I think” is a logical condition on the unity of experience. It reveals no substance. In his careful phrase, it is a mere representation that accompanies. It shows that my representations are mine in a formal sense: they belong to one unified apperception. It supplies no self-grounding substance that underlies that unity and explains it. The inference to a substance is exactly what Kant calls the paralogism. The argument’s middle term, the word that links its two premises, shifts meaning between them, and the shift gives it the mere appearance of validity.

The strongest reconstructions of the cogito’s epistemic force leave this diagnosis standing. Shoemaker’s immunity to error through misidentification shows that first-person reports cannot go wrong in one specific way, about whose experience is being reported. That is a self-givenness claim, and it carries no self-grounding claim. It shows that the experience is given as mine and stops short of showing a self-grounding “mine” that generates the experience. Thomas Nagel’s insistence on the subjective character of experience makes a related point. There is something it is like to be a bat. This establishes that experience has an intrinsic, perspectival character that cannot be reduced to third-person description. Again, the claim concerns the character of the experience and not the ontological status of the experiencing subject. What is given is the thinking-now, the occurrence-from-here. The cogito does not deliver a subject that grounds that occurrence in its own being.

The cogito, then, vindicates seity and falls short of aseity. The thinking-now is self-given: indubitable, intrinsically perspectival and immune to a certain class of doubt. The es denkt is ownerless and self-given, and its existence depends on conditions that the cogito neither specifies nor excludes.

4. Galen Strawson’s Robust Subject: Intrinsic Character, Dependent Existence

Plainly: Galen Strawson makes the strongest modern case for a real subject of experience, though he stops short of taking the step to self-grounding himself. His case shows that experience has a built-in character. It also shows that the subject arises and perishes, and arising and perishing is dependence.

Galen Strawson makes the strongest contemporary analytic case for a robust phenomenal subject. He has argued at length against eliminativist and illusionist accounts of experience. Eliminativism denies that experience, as ordinarily conceived, exists at all. Illusionism holds that experience only seems to have the features we attribute to it. At least two parts of Strawson’s position bear directly on perspectival aseity.

The first part is a Russellian claim about the intrinsic nature of matter. Bertrand Russell argued in The Analysis of Matter (1927) and Human Knowledge (1948) that physics describes only the structural and relational properties of matter and leaves its intrinsic nature unknown. Strawson follows him and argues that phenomenal experience may constitute or reveal the intrinsic nature of physical reality. On this view the experiential and the physical are one thing: the experiential is what the physical is from the inside, taken intrinsically. The position is serious, and its scope is limited. It establishes that the experiential character of certain physical processes is intrinsic to them: non-relational, and not further analyzable in structural terms. That is exactly the flame’s heat, a built-in character compatible with dependent existence. The Russellian claim establishes nothing about whether the physical substrate is itself self-grounding, the substrate that experience supervenes on (depends on and varies with) or is identical with. Physics describes a world of structured dependencies and causal relations. On this view matter’s intrinsic nature is experiential, and matter, with that experiential nature, is as causally and ontologically dependent as any physical process.

The second part is Strawson’s insistence on real, concrete subjects of experience. In various formulations he calls them SEcts (subjects of experience) or SESMETs (subjects of experience that are mental entities). He wants to resist dissolving the subject into a bundle of experiences with no owner. In his own treatment SESMETs are short-lived: they arise, persist briefly and perish. An entity with aseity, a self-grounding entity, is ungenerated and does not perish. A SESMET that arises and perishes is dependent by that very fact. It comes into being under certain conditions and passes away when those conditions change. Its existence is conditioned and not self-grounded. Strawson insists that subjects are real, concrete entities and refuses to treat them as logical fictions. That insistence is the feature of his view that most respects the reality of the subject, and it is also the feature that reveals the subject as dependent. His subjects are generated and they perish. A se existence would do neither.

Strawson’s own work thus supplies both parts of the flame image for consciousness. Russellian intrinsicality gives the heat: the non-relational, intrinsic character of experience. The SESMET ontology gives the dependence on fuel: the subject arises and perishes under conditions it does not itself provide. The strongest realist subject in analytic philosophy of mind delivers the intrinsic character of a dependent existence. It stops short of aseity.

Strawson explicitly opposes eliminativism, illusionism and deflationary treatments of consciousness, accounts that play down what consciousness is. His work establishes beyond reasonable doubt that experience has a real, intrinsic, non-relational character. The argument of this paper grants that. It disputes only the further step to self-grounding existence. Strawson himself does not take that step, and his own SESMET ontology rules it out.

5. Svaprakāśa and Pre-Reflective Self-Awareness: Epistemic Luminosity Without Ontological Grounding

Plainly: a lamp that lights itself needs no outside certifier, and it still burns on oil. Consciousness that lights itself is in the same position.

The Sanskrit term svaprakāśa means self-luminosity. It names a doctrine held in various forms across several Indian philosophical schools. The doctrine says that consciousness lights itself and needs no outside light. A lamp shows other things by its own light, and no second lamp is needed to make the lamp visible. In the same way, consciousness makes other things known and is itself known in the very act of knowing. The doctrine captures something Western phenomenology reached from a different direction, in the study of pre-reflective self-awareness.

Dan Zahavi develops a phenomenological account of pre-reflective self-awareness in Subjectivity and Selfhood (2005) and elsewhere. He draws on Husserl, Sartre and the wider phenomenological tradition. On this account experience is always already given as mine. That givenness belongs to the pre-reflective structure of experiencing itself. No later act of reflection or self-attribution produces it. Jean-Paul Sartre made a version of this point in the introduction to Being and Nothingness (1943). The pre-reflective cogito is the way consciousness is present to itself, and it comes before the reflective cogito and makes it possible. Experience carries an intrinsic first-person character, a for-me-ness, that nothing outside adds to it.

These claims are well grounded phenomenologically, and the paper grants them in full. Pre-reflective self-awareness is a real and important structure. The for-me-ness of experience is intrinsic and non-relational. In the relevant sense, consciousness is self-luminous. Nothing outside it has to certify its presence.

This is a self-givenness claim. Grant the doctrine its picture, in which the lamp lights itself and other things. Even so, the lamp depends on oil to exist. Candrakīrti, the seventh-century Mādhyamika philosopher, pressed this point against the svaprakāśa doctrine in the Madhyamakāvatāra (Entry into the Middle Way), and he went further. A lamp, he argued, does not light itself. What a lamp does is make things visible. The lamp itself is simply there, burning. To ask what makes its burning real is to ask what conditions sustain it. On Candrakīrti’s argument, the self-luminosity doctrine confuses the lamp’s making other things visible with some kind of self-establishing act. The lamp does not ground its own existence. It burns in dependence on conditions.

Apply this to consciousness. Taken at its strongest, pre-reflective self-awareness, with the for-me-ness of experience and the self-luminous character of cognition, establishes that consciousness needs no outside certifier and no second act of reflection to be given. That is self-givenness. It leaves unproven the further claim that the consciousness so given exists independently of conditions outside it, conditions that are neurological, evolutionary, developmental and causal. The lamp’s light is its own, and its burning depends on oil. Experience has the same shape: an intrinsic for-me-ness and a conditioned occurrence.

Zahavi himself takes care not to overclaim. His argument is phenomenological. It concerns the structure of experience as it is experienced. It is not metaphysical, and it says nothing about the ultimate ontological status of the experiencing subject. The for-me-ness he identifies is an intrinsic phenomenological character. Metaphysical aseity is a different property. To get from Zahavi’s legitimate phenomenological conclusion to a claim of metaphysical aseity takes exactly the step that Candrakīrti’s lamp argument blocks.

6. Fichte’s Self-Positing I: Schlechthin and Its Concealed Dependence

Plainly: Fichte says the I posits itself absolutely. He asserts the word “absolutely” without earning it, and his own system admits that the I depends on a limit it does not supply.

Johann Gottlieb Fichte’s Wissenschaftslehre of 1794–95 is perhaps the most systematic attempt in philosophy to derive the self-grounding of the I from the structure of self-consciousness. Fichte starts from the Tathandlung, the act-fact: the self-positing of the I. To posit something is to set it down as real. The absolute I posits itself, and this self-positing is necessary and unconditioned, the foundation of all reality. Fichte’s adverb is schlechthin, which means absolutely, unconditionally, without remainder. The I posits itself absolutely, with no prior condition. In doing so it posits the very structure within which anything else can appear.

The argument has force because it answers two problems: the Kantian problem of the thing-in-itself, meaning the world as it is apart from how we know it, and the need for a starting point for philosophy that rests on no hypothesis. If the I posits itself in the absolute sense, it would have aseity. Its existence would rest on nothing other than its own act of self-positing.

The critical question is whether schlechthin, the absoluteness of the self-positing, is established or merely assumed. Dieter Henrich’s well-known analysis in “Fichte’s Original Insight” (1966) showed that a pure act of self-positing cannot by itself explain the structure of self-consciousness. Any account that begins with a subject positing itself already presupposes that the subject is itself, and that is exactly what needed explaining. The self-positing is circular, and the circle generates no being from nothing. It presupposes a prior being that the positing merely acknowledges. Ernst Tugendhat pressed a related point in Self-Consciousness and Self-Determination (1979). A consciousness that is “acquainted with itself” must be acquainted with something. So the relation of acquaintance must already be in place before any act of self-positing can occur.

Under this scrutiny, Fichte’s framework delivers a sophisticated account of the structure of self-positing, of what it is for there to be a self-consciousness at all. That is self-givenness made fully explicit. The I is given to itself in an act that no other act precedes and no outside relation constitutes. The adverb schlechthin would carry the argument from self-givenness to aseity, and the argument does not earn it. It is a declaration. Fichte’s own system concedes this at the level of structure. The absolute I requires the Anstoß, the check: the resistance, the not-I that limits the I’s infinite self-positing. It requires the Anstoß as a condition of any determinate empirical consciousness. So the empirical I, the I that anyone can identify with, depends on the Anstoß, a condition it does not provide from itself. The absolute I, meanwhile, becomes a regulative ideal, a guiding idea with no concrete existence. Neither the concrete version nor the ideal version achieves aseity.

In Fichte, the word schlechthin carries the slide from self-givenness to aseity, and it smuggles the conclusion into the premise. Once the adverb goes, or has to be earned, what remains is a precise and important account of how self-consciousness posits itself. The I is self-given, in an act that cannot be reduced further. That act does not ground the existence of the I that performs it. “Positing is occurring” does not license “positing grounds its own occurrence.”

7. Advaita’s Self-Established Ātman: The Genuine Standoff

Plainly: Advaita agrees that the everyday self is dependent. It claims aseity only for the ultimate witness. The Buddhist reply, that even that witness is empty, is serious, and neither side has won.

The fifth form of perspectival aseity is the most serious. It comes from Advaita Vedānta, a non-dual school of Indian philosophy. The Indian philosopher Śaṅkara (eighth to ninth century, traditional dating) states the position in the Brahmasūtrabhāṣya (Commentary on the Brahma Sūtras), and the tradition he founded develops it. Two features set Advaita apart from the four traditions already examined.

The first feature: Advaita places aseity explicitly at the ultimate level. This is the level of turīya (the fourth state, beyond waking, dreaming and deep sleep), of witness-consciousness (sākṣin), and of the self-luminous Ātman that is identical with Brahman. Ātman means the self, and Brahman means ultimate reality. Advaita makes no claim of aseity for the empirical ego (jīva). It treats the empirical ego as mithyā: real as a phenomenon, and illusory at the ultimate level. The empirical ego belongs to the conventional order of vyavahāra. This everyday self arises and passes away. It is composite and conditioned. At the empirical level, then, Advaita agrees entirely with the diagnosis applied to the other four traditions: the ordinary experiencing subject has no aseity. Advaita claims aseity for the ultimate witness-consciousness alone. That is the pure awareness that underlies and witnesses all empirical experience without itself being an object within experience.

The second feature is the distinctive logical structure of Śaṅkara’s argument that the sākṣin is self-established. Witness-consciousness cannot be denied without circularity. Any act of denial is itself an occurrence within consciousness. The consciousness that makes the denial possible cannot be made the object of that denial without starting a regress. The sākṣin is also said to be self-luminous in a stronger sense than the lamp: it is the ground of all illumination, including the lighting of other things. So it cannot be a derived or dependent entity, because then the relation of derivation would itself go unexplained. Śaṅkara moves from two features of witness-consciousness to its self-establishment. It is ineliminable, meaning it cannot be removed from the picture, and non-objectifiable, meaning it cannot be turned into an object of awareness. His reasoning uses a grounding excluded-middle, an either-or with no third option: everything is either other-grounded or self-grounded. What cannot be derived from conditions and cannot be denied must then, on that either-or, be self-grounded.

This argument earns a serious form of self-givenness. The sākṣin is self-luminous in the strongest sense. It is the condition that makes any illumination possible, so nothing else can make it manifest. The ineliminability argument is sound, since any attempt to eliminate or derive witness-consciousness presupposes it. The non-objectifiability is well grounded phenomenologically. You cannot make the witnessing awareness an object of awareness without a further witnessing awareness, and so on.

The argument meets its most serious challenge at one point: the move from non-derivability to self-grounding, which is the grounding excluded-middle. Śaṅkara assumes there are only two options. A thing is either other-grounded, derived from conditions outside it, or self-grounded, existing a se. Given that exhaustive either-or, the ineliminability and non-derivability of the sākṣin are enough to establish its self-grounding.

This excluded middle shuts out a third option, and Madhyamaka philosophy argues for it. Madhyamaka is the Buddhist school of the Middle Way. The third option is ownerless groundlessness, which the tradition calls śūnyatā, emptiness. Nāgārjuna developed the Madhyamaka position, and Candrakīrti carried it forward in the Madhyamakāvatāra. It holds that phenomena are dependently originated (pratītyasamutpanna) in a sense that rules out self-grounding and also rules out other-grounding in any substantial sense. Things arise in dependence on conditions, and no ground, self or other, stands beneath them free of dependence. The chain of dependently arisen phenomena does not bottom out in a self-grounding end point. It is empty of any such end point.

Suppose the Madhyamaka third option is coherent. A substantial body of contemporary scholarship on Nāgārjuna and Candrakīrti argues that it is. Then Śaṅkara’s grounding excluded-middle begs the question. The ineliminability of the sākṣin is compatible with the Madhyamaka conclusion that even the ultimate witnessing awareness is empty of self-grounded existence. On that reading it arises dependently, or, in the limit case of cessation (nirvāṇa), it does not arise at all. On the Madhyamaka reading, the appearance of aseity would then be a deep and stubborn case of svabhāva-graha, grasping at intrinsic existence, which persists even at the ultimate level of witness-consciousness.

The result is a standoff, and it leaves Advaita unrefuted. The Madhyamaka must show that ownerless groundlessness is coherent when applied to the ultimate witnessing awareness itself. Advaita has a powerful response here. If the Madhyamaka denies any self-grounding ground, it owes an account of what sustains dependent arising when the chain has no end point. A chain of dependently arisen phenomena with no ground risks an infinite regress, or an unexplained brute fact of arising. Advaita argues that such a chain cannot be coherent without a self-luminous witness as its condition.

The standoff is genuine and unresolved. Neither side can claim victory in the philosophical literature. Jan Westerhoff’s work on Nāgārjuna and Madhyamaka epistemology gives the best contemporary account of what Madhyamaka can draw on. The Advaita tradition has its own sophisticated defenders. For this paper the key point is structural. The standoff takes place entirely at the ultimate level. The no-aseity thesis, which this paper defends, holds that nothing in the everyday world grounds its own existence. It already brackets the ultimate level, which means it sets that level aside without passing judgment on it. Advaita explicitly agrees that the empirical ego has no aseity. The dispute is whether the ultimate ground of all experiencing is self-established, or whether even that ground is empty of intrinsic existence.

8. The Two-Level Result

Plainly: at the everyday level, all four cases turn out to be seity, and three separate critiques agree on why. Advaita stands undefeated at the ultimate level.

At the empirical level the verdict is decisive, and the critiques converge. The cogito delivers an indubitable occurrence, es denkt, there is thinking. It does not deliver a self-grounding I. Galen Strawson’s robust subject gives a real, intrinsic phenomenal character, the flame’s heat. He places that character in a subject that is generated and perishes, and that is the mark of dependence. The svaprakāśa tradition and Zahavi’s pre-reflective self-awareness yield the intrinsic for-me-ness of experience, a full self-givenness, and the lamp that gives light still depends on oil. Fichte’s self-positing I gives the most systematic account of how self-positing consciousness is structured. The word schlechthin is not earned, and the Anstoß concedes empirical dependence.

Three canonical critiques produce the verdict, each from outside the no-aseity thesis, and they converge independently. They are Lichtenberg’s es denkt (Germany, eighteenth century), Kant’s First Paralogism (Germany, 1781) and Candrakīrti’s lamp argument (India, seventh century). The three arose in different traditions and answered different philosophical problems. They reach the same diagnosis. The apparent self-grounding of the first-person subject is an appearance that the self-givenness of experience generates. The self-givenness is real. The self-grounding is an illicit inference from it. At the empirical level, perspectival aseity dissolves into seity.

The convergence of these three critiques across traditions is significant supporting evidence. By itself it is not decisive. When traditions share no common authority and no common method and still reach the same structural diagnosis, their agreement carries more weight than any argument from a single tradition. The convergence is within one domain, the analysis of consciousness and the first-person standpoint. Agreement reached within a single problem-domain, and still more within a single body of work, falls short of the independent confirmation that cross-domain convergence, agreement reached in a field outside the study of consciousness, would supply. The paper therefore advances the diagnosis at moderate-to-high confidence. The diagnosis is robust across traditions. Its decisive independent corroboration would come, if at all, from convergence reached in a different domain entirely.

At the ultimate level the result is different in kind. The flame image does not dissolve Advaita Vedānta’s self-established sākṣin, because Advaita explicitly makes its claim at the level where the flame image may not apply: the ground of all arising, as opposed to a particular arising within the empirical order. Pointing to the dependence of empirical experiences cannot refute the Advaita argument, because Advaita agrees that empirical experiences are dependent. The argument turns on whether the ultimate witnessing awareness is itself dependently arisen or self-grounded. That dispute with Madhyamaka is genuine and unresolved.

This has two consequences for the overall argument. The no-aseity claim cannot be maintained in the universal form “no aseity anywhere, not even at the ultimate floor.” Advaita is a live and undefeated challenge to that universal claim. The no-aseity claim can be maintained at the empirical and conventional level without qualification. No empirical perspectival aseity is available. Under scrutiny, the apparent self-grounding of the first-person subject dissolves into seity.

9. The Honest Bound

Plainly: the position can claim with confidence that there is no aseity in the everyday order. It must concede an open question: whether the ultimate ground is a self-luminous aseity.

The no-aseity position can claim, and this paper argues the canonical critiques establish, that there is no aseity in the conferred, conventional, or empirical order. The everyday first-person subject has no aseity. This is the subject that psychology describes, that phenomenology analyzes and that ordinary language refers to. Its existence is conditioned by neurological processes, developmental history, ongoing causal relations, and whatever ultimate ground, or absence of ground, lies under all of this. Its self-givenness, the intrinsic, for-me character of its experience, is real and philosophically significant. Self-givenness does not amount to self-grounding. Under careful scrutiny, the five strongest traditions that have argued otherwise deliver only self-givenness.

It must concede that whether the ultimate ground of all experiencing is a self-luminous aseity is an open question, bracketed and unsettled. Advaita Vedānta has argued, with philosophical rigor and a continuous tradition of sophisticated defense, that the ultimate witnessing awareness is self-established and cannot coherently be denied. The Madhyamaka has argued that even this ground is empty of intrinsic existence. Neither has defeated the other. Consider a no-aseity position with universal scope, one that says there is no aseity anywhere, the ultimate metaphysical floor included. It must either engage the Advaita argument on its own terms and defeat it, or bracket the claim. This paper brackets it, because that defeat has not been accomplished.

Bracketing states the epistemic situation as it stands. The paper works by conjecture and refutation: it makes the strongest case it can, presses it against the strongest opposition and reports the verdict, including the part that goes against it. Here the verdict has two parts. The empirical dissolution is decisive (high confidence). The ultimate standoff is genuine: the no-aseity position cannot claim victory there, and Advaita stands undefeated at the floor.

The two-level structure also shows where the argument stands in several live debates in philosophy of mind and metaphysics. It affirms the self-givenness and intrinsic character of experience throughout, so eliminativism and illusionism about consciousness get no support from it. Nor do bundle theory, the view that a self is only a collection of experiences, or no-self views in any naive form. The seity left after perspectival aseity dissolves is a real occurrence: ownerless, a self-given event with no self-grounding owner. The argument also leaves intact the significance of the hard problem of consciousness, the question of why physical processes come with experience at all. The intrinsic, perspectival character of experience, the fact that there is something it is like, is compatible with the self-givenness the argument affirms. The argument presupposes it.

Keith Frankish’s illusionism (2016) holds that phenomenal consciousness, as ordinarily conceived, is an introspective illusion. The paper’s position differs. It treats the for-me-ness of experience as real. It argues that the ontological conclusions some philosophers draw from that for-me-ness are unwarranted.

John Perry’s work on the essential indexical (1979, 2001) bears on the self-givenness side of the distinction from a different angle. An indexical is a word such as “I,” “here” or “now,” whose reference depends on who uses it, where and when. Perry shows that first-person indexical reference cannot be reduced to third-person description, and that captures the intrinsic perspectival character of the first-person standpoint. The irreducibility is a feature of the givenness of the first-person perspective and not of its grounding. Perry’s essential indexical establishes that objective descriptions cannot replace the first-person standpoint. That is a self-givenness result, and it is compatible with the dependent existence of the perspective so given.

10. Conclusion

Plainly: a thing known from itself does not, by that fact, ground itself. This holds everywhere in the everyday order, and the paper names its one open exception.

The question was whether consciousness is the one self-grounding reality: whether the first-person perspective has its existence from and through itself, ungenerated and dependent on nothing. The paper has argued that the five strongest philosophical traditions pressing this claim all deliver, at the empirical level, something real and significant that differs from what they claim. They deliver self-givenness and stop short of self-grounding.

The master distinction is simple, and a great deal follows from it. A thing known from itself is not, by that fact, a thing that grounds itself. The flame’s heat is intrinsic to the flame, and the flame depends on fuel. First-person experience has an intrinsic, non-relational, for-me character. Nothing outside confers that character or has to certify it, and it is real. The occurrence of the experiencing, the arising of the perspective and the existence of the subject all depend on conditions. The self-givenness of experience neither provides those conditions nor excludes them.

Three canonical critiques converge across traditions on this diagnosis. Lichtenberg replaced the cogito’s ich denke with es denkt. Kant’s First Paralogism showed that the formal unity of apperception does not license an inference to a self-grounding substance. Candrakīrti’s lamp argument showed that self-luminosity does not establish independence from conditions. These critiques arose independently, in the German Enlightenment and in seventh-century Mādhyamika Buddhism. Their convergence is the cross-traditional evidence that the dissolution of empirical perspectival aseity is a structural finding about how self-givenness relates to self-grounding, and it holds across traditions.

At the ultimate level the paper concedes an open question. The Advaita Vedānta position, as developed in Śaṅkara’s Brahmasūtrabhāṣya and the tradition it founded, is a real and undefeated philosophical standoff. The self-established sākṣin is the witness-consciousness that cannot be denied without circularity and cannot be derived without a further witnessing. It is a serious argument for ultimate perspectival aseity. The Madhyamaka has contested it and has not demonstrably refuted it. The right response is to bracket it precisely and to keep it in view, even at the cost of a clean universal conclusion. No aseity holds in the conferred and conventional order. Whether the ultimate ground is a self-luminous aseity is the open question between Advaita and Madhyamaka, and the paper leaves it as it stands.

The stable result is that self-givenness does not amount to self-grounding. Examined at its five strongest points, the apparent perspectival aseity is seity: an ownerless occurrence, self-given and not self-grounded. The one exception sits at the ultimate level. There the question stays in the bracket, undefeated and unrefuted, and philosophy of mind and comparative metaphysics have to keep pressing it.
