# Models Propose, Oracles Dispose

## A generated claim is a proposal. Nothing a model produces becomes evidence until something the model does not control has had its chance to say no.

*Zain Dana Harper, July 2026*

## In short

Models crank out drafts and answers for next to nothing now. I don't think any of it should count as evidence until something the model doesn't control has had a fair shot at saying no. I call that something an oracle. When it says no, the claim stops, and the no gets written down on the spot. Why should you care? People cite and build on whatever they trust, so an unchecked claim rides along into every one of those uses. Sections 2 to 4 are how I build that check into my own software. Section 5 is where I stand, including the part where the rule bites me. The code is public at [github.com/HarperZ9/buildlang](https://github.com/HarperZ9/buildlang) if you'd rather check than take my word for it.

## 1. The rule

Four words: models propose, oracles dispose. The oracle is the independent check that decides. To dispose, in the old sense the title borrows, is to settle the outcome, and the disposition is that final call. Everything else I've got is commentary on where those four words draw the line.

A model can draft, translate, summarize and search for me, and I'm glad of the help in my shop. None of it is evidence. By the evidence layer I mean any place where later work trusts a result without checking it again, the stuff that gets cited and built on.

Between a model's output and that layer, something has to stand that can say no, and the proposer can't be the one holding its leash. Somebody skimming for tone doesn't count. A second model doesn't count either, because a second model agreeing is just a second proposal. When the check refuses, the claim stops at the door, and the refusal is written down when it happens.

Yes, the phrasing leans on the old proverb "Man proposes, God disposes." That's on purpose, because the structure is the old one: one party proposes, and another decides. A tireless proposer can be brilliant and right most of the time and still not get the last word. What machines changed is the price. Proposals used to be scarce, so treating a confident one as halfway to a finding was something you could survive. Now they're nearly free, and habits built for scarce proposals fail quietly when proposals show up by the truckload.

I get why people want to skip it. Checking is slow, and a model that's right most of the time feels like enough. But a second check is the whole difference between a layer of evidence and a pile of it. Let one generated result in on its own say-so and everybody downstream inherits a claim nobody ruled on, and it compounds with every reuse. So the rule sits at the door, built into the structure, where it keeps working on the days people forget to be careful. I'm one of those people.

## 2. A receipt that re-derives

Where I hold this line hardest right now is a compiler, the program that turns code into software a computer can run. It compiles buildlang, a programming language whose code is public. A program built with it prints its results and can also emit a receipt: a sealed record of what ran, what came out, and which invariant the run was checked against. Sealed means locked with a digital fingerprint, so any later change to the record shows. An invariant is a condition the run has to satisfy.

A separate program, the verifier, re-derives the result. It works the answer out again from whatever the sealed facts allow and compares. It checks the invariant again. It re-runs the program and compares the exit status, the code a program reports when it finishes, with the sealed one. If they agree, that's a verdict, with the full weight of one.

One record format holds this shape across five kinds of computation: fully deterministic runs, closed-form probability, seeded randomness, sampled estimates, and budgeted search. In plain terms, the same receipt works whether a program gives the same answer every time, works out an exact probability by formula, uses random numbers that can be replayed, estimates an answer from many random trials, or hunts for the best answer it can find on a fixed budget. One shape stretching that far still amazes me. The sampled cases carry their sample counts inside the seal, because an estimate that hides how many samples it rests on can't be checked.

Some facts can't be re-derived exactly, and for those you just say so. Wall-clock time, how long the run took by the clock, is the plain example. The verifier measures it again, then shows the sealed number and the fresh one side by side without forcing them to agree. Calling a second measurement a re-derivation would be the exact overclaim this whole apparatus exists to refuse. Checking this way is slower than trust and more annoying than confidence, and the slowness is the point. A receipt says what was checked and how, nothing stronger, and when a check confirmed nothing, the receipt says that too.
