## 3. A check that can prove it can fail

A check that has never said no is not known to be a check. It might be a working check or a rubber stamp, and from the outside you cannot tell the two apart until the day it matters.

So the negative fixtures, forged inputs the verifier must reject, are a core part of the test suite. For every way a receipt or a chain of receipts could be forged, there is a test that forges it and demands the right refusal by name. A chain here means receipts bound together in a fixed order, so that changing, removing or reordering any one of them breaks the whole. Tamper with one sealed count inside a chained bundle and verification has to fail under the specific name for that tampering, `CHAIN_LINK_UNVERIFIED`, which says the chain link is unverified. A test that gets only a generic error counts as a failure. Reorder the receipts in a chain, drop one, or substitute one, and each sabotage has its own named failure. The ordinary test run exercises each of those names end to end. The verifier also carries a self-test whose whole job is to show, on demand, that the rejections still fire.

Checking systems drift toward saying yes. Deadlines and demos favor a pass, and each false alarm annoys someone into loosening the check, so every pressure on a checking system points the same way. Only deliberately maintained proof that the check still says no pushes back. The named refusal, the less pleasant answer, is the product.
