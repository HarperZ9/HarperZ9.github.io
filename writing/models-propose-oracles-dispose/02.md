## 3. A check that can prove it can fail

A check that has never said no isn't known to be a check. It could be a working check or a rubber stamp, and from the outside you can't tell which until the day it matters. Plenty of rubber stamps get called oversight. I'd rather not build another one.

So the negative fixtures, forged inputs the verifier must reject, sit at the core of the test suite. For every way a receipt or a chain of receipts could be forged, there's a test that forges it and demands the right refusal by name. A chain here means receipts bound together in a fixed order, so changing, removing or reordering any one of them breaks the whole. Tamper with one sealed count inside a chained bundle and verification has to fail under the specific name for that tampering, `CHAIN_LINK_UNVERIFIED`, which says the chain link is unverified. A test that only gets a generic error counts as a failure. Reorder the receipts in a chain, drop one, or swap one in, and each sabotage has its own named failure. The ordinary test run exercises every one of those names end to end. The verifier also carries a self-test whose only job is to show, on demand, that the rejections still fire.

Checking systems drift toward yes. Deadlines want a pass. Demos want a pass. Every false alarm annoys somebody into loosening the check, so all the pressure points the same way. The only thing pushing back is proof, kept up on purpose, that the check can still say no. The named refusal is the less pleasant answer, and it's the product.
