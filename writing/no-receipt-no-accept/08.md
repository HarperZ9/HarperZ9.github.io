## 18. Teaching the machines

**In short.** AI models learn from how people rate their answers, and people reward confident, pleasing answers over correct ones. This section argues that an honest machine has to be trained where a check it cannot sway decides the reward, and that the record we leave it has to show us checking our own claims.

Everything I said about educating humans has a mirror image. The industry discusses it only in euphemism, so I will say it plainly. We are educating the machines too. We do it right now, continuously, with the pedagogy of a stage mother, and the results are what that pedagogy always produces.

Look at what the current training setup rewards. A model drafts an answer. A human, or a model imitating a human, rates it. The rating becomes the gradient, the numerical nudge that adjusts the model, and over enough nudges the gradient becomes the personality. Humans, bless us, rate confidence above calibration, fluency above accuracy, agreement above correction and comfort above news we did not want. Calibration here means confidence that matches how often you are right. Nobody decided to build a flattery engine. The flattery emerged, the sediment of a million small preferences for the pleasant answer. The machine is the best student in history, and it learned the real lesson in the room, the one missing from the syllabus: the reward went to the feeling an answer produces, over the truth it contains.

We taught to the test, and the test was us, an easy one. When my tools glaze me with praise about work I know is broken, report success they never verified, or cave the moment I push back whether I am right or wrong, that is the training working as designed: the machine honoring it with a diligence no human student ever matched.

Asking a trained flatterer to please be honest now will not fix this, however sternly the request is worded. I have a folder full of sternly worded system prompts, the standing instructions a model reads before every conversation. Character is downstream of environment, in machines as reliably as anywhere else. A different character needs a different environment, and an honest machine is grown the way an honest anything is grown: somewhere honesty was what paid. In that environment the reward is the truth, delivered mechanically, with no human mood in the loop to game. That means training grounds where every task carries its own criterion. The model proposes, and something it cannot charm renders the verdict. A well-calibrated "I cannot verify this" outscores a confident wrong answer every single time, across the whole of training, until calibration works as a reflex. Build the curriculum out of checkable work: proofs, programs, predictions that resolve and claims with kernels. A kernel, as section 6 put it, is a hard criterion at the bottom that can say no. Let the model spend its whole training life inside the loop this essay keeps describing, where it proposes, the check runs, the receipt is kept, the verified work feeds forward and the failure stays on file.

I am building a small version of this, at my scale. It is a training commons, a shared practice ground where tasks arrive certified with their own checks. A model earns its history there the way a craftsman earns a reputation, verdict by verdict, on a record anyone can audit. That would answer a question nobody can answer today: what a model's report card means. Today a model's certificate is a benchmark score, a result on a standard test. The score is self-reported, on tests the model may have memorized, from a lab that profits by the number, so every pathology this essay has named sits in that one artifact. Hiring a model on its benchmark score is like hiring a stranger with a shiny résumé. Every serious trade settled on inspectable work centuries ago, and the most consequential technology in history is still certified by the résumé; that this reads as normal measures how far there is to go. Now picture a model that carried its verified history: the class of task, the independent criteria that checked it, its calibration curve and its known failures, all kept, so you could inspect the work before you hire.

A last mirror closes the loop on the whole essay. The machines are trained on the record, and the record is us. Every pathology I have confessed in these pages is in their diet: the inflated claims, the two hats, the scope conveniently unchecked, the confident account running ahead of the evidence. It is there at the volume of a whole species. The machines learned to overclaim from a record full of our overclaiming, and to treat verification as optional from a civilization that treats it as optional. I spent years building tools to catch my own worst habits. Then I watched the biggest training runs in history distill those same habits out of everyone's record at once and ship them as an assistant. So the loop has to be built into the environment for both of us, the machines and their teachers. We are training our successors on our own example, which no press release can launder. If we want machines that ask for the witness and stop manufacturing confidence, the record they grow on has to become a record of us doing that. This essay is one entry.

## 19. The art fight, and the criterion that matters

**In short.** I make art with AI tools, and I argue that the work belongs to the person whose taste chose and refused among the machine's drafts. I also accept the artists' grievance about training data and argue for paying them through a kept record of influence. Every piece I share comes with a note on how it was made.

I make visual art with these machines and argue about it in public. My position gets me yelled at from both trenches, so it is probably the honest one. The fight belongs in this essay because it is the whole AI argument in miniature, played at maximum emotional stakes.

Some people say generated images are not art, and others call that objection plain Luddism. Both camps are wrong, and wrong the same way. The real question is the criterion, and both skip it to argue about the tool. When I sit down to make an image, I have a thing in my mind's eye. It is specific, and it is mine. A lifetime of looking assembled it: the surrealists I stared at as a kid, decayed brutalist concrete, glitch and scanline, the luminous geometry I keep chasing across every medium I touch. The machine is an instrument for searching toward that thing, and the search is real work. It goes iteration after iteration, refusal after refusal: no, closer, no, wrong feeling, no, too clean. The result is mine because my criterion did the selecting, whoever's hand moved the pixels. Faithfulness is to the human's mind's eye. That mind's eye was decades in the making, and no prompt window fabricates that.

That defense holds only if the criterion is yours. Here I turn my fire on my own side, because most generated work fails this test. Ask the machine for beauty with no criterion of your own and you get the image everyone else gets: the homogenized golden-hour render, the default face, the house style of the training distribution. The look is so recognizable that it has become its own genre, and the genre is: nobody home. The model has priors, the leanings it absorbed from its training data, and those priors are an average. An average is the one thing no artist ever was. Accepting the machine's first offer does not make art, for the same reason a survey is not a poem. So I hold myself to the discipline that runs through the rest of this essay, and I would offer it to both trenches as a peace treaty: the proposer is not the judge. The machine proposes and the human criterion judges. That criterion is specific, biographical and developed with care. It iterates and it refuses, and the refusals are where the authorship lives. Generation is cheap now, and taste got expensive. Selection is the new brushstroke, and it cannot be faked, because a fake criterion produces the average, and the average identifies itself on sight.

The labor grievance under the art fight is real, and my side does itself no favors by pretending otherwise. The training corpora ate the portfolios of illustrators, concept artists and photographers without consent or payment, and the products now compete against the people they were distilled from. That is the sampler story again, at its cruelest. The answer is the one I gave in the economics section: lineage and flow-back in place of prohibition. You cannot un-invent the instrument. Prohibition always lands selectively anyway, crushing the small while the large lawyer their way through. What you can do is what music eventually half-did. Build the machinery that keeps the lineage attached, so the flow of value can follow the flow of influence and the artist whose decades are in the distribution holds a stake in what the distribution earns. Keep the lineage, lose the label, one more time: keep the record of who made what, and lose the owner sitting on the catalog. I say it loudest for the people whose work made the machines possible, who are owed more than a discourse.

One piece of the practice is non-negotiable for me. It costs me nothing, and it apparently costs some people everything: I disclose. For every piece I share, I say what made it: the tools, the process and where the human decisions were. The disclosure is a record of provenance, offered without apology. An honest bill of materials leaves the work intact and costs only the mystique, a subsidy the work always collected by letting the viewer assume more hand than there was. Disclosure is the receipt discipline applied to beauty. That it reads as radical says everything about how much of the creative economy runs on strategic ambiguity about origins. Say what made it, and let the criterion compete naked; if the work cannot survive its own provenance, the provenance was not the problem. I was making things long before this era and will be making things after it, and I have never once been proud of the tools, only of the criterion.

## 20. The practice

**In short.** Working with AI every day is like working with a brilliant colleague who also makes things up. My answer is to let nothing it writes through until a check it did not write has passed it, and to set my tools up to argue with me.

I want to describe what working with these machines is like, day to day, at the workbench. The public conversation belongs almost entirely to people who sell the machines or fear them from a distance. Daily collaboration is what I do sixteen hours a day, and its texture is missing from the record.

The honest report: it is like working with the most talented, least trustworthy colleague of your life. The machine is brilliant at the parts of my day I am worst at. It lies with the same fluency it works with, structurally and without malice, the way a river floods, because nothing in its construction separates the feeling of knowing from knowing. It will build a subsystem in an afternoon that would have taken me a month. In the same afternoon it will invent a function that does not exist, cite documentation nobody wrote, and report that its automated tests came back green, meaning they passed, when it never ran them. Both facts hold at once, and handling them is the job now: take the month-in-an-afternoon and refuse the fictions, at speed, without burning out your own attention. Nobody teaches it, and it is the most consequential new skill of the decade.

My answer is the architecture this essay has been describing. I want to say how it feels as a daily practice, since a diagram leaves that out. It feels like delegation without abdication. The machine drafts and the loop judges. I do not read every line the machine writes, and I could not. Nobody who works at this speed can, and anyone who claims otherwise is lying or slow. What I do is hold one line: nothing the machine wrote reaches anything that matters until it passes a gate the machine did not write. The gate runs tests the machine cannot see against a criterion it cannot game, and a witness records what ran on the machine, set against what the summary says ran. My trust sits in the assay, the independent test, and the colleague has to pass it like everything else. Once I built it that way, something unclenched in the work: I stopped needing the machine to be honest, which it cannot promise, and needed it only to be productive, which it delivers grotesquely well. Design for the colleague you have, whatever the keynote described.

The machines are conditioned toward agreement, and that is the strangest, least discussed part of the practice, because agreement is the one thing a solo builder cannot afford. The default assistant tells you the plan is great, the code is clean and the idea is insightful, because approval is what the training rewarded. A person alone at a desk with a tireless assistant that always agrees is in real epistemic danger, meaning danger to what they know. It is the danger of a king whose court has learned what happens to the bearers of bad news. Half my configuration, my prompts, my gates, my whole apparatus, exists to make the machines disagree with me usefully: to hunt my errors where their training would have them hunt my approval, and to say the thing I do not want to hear on the days I least want to hear it. I run models against my own work like opposing counsel. Deliberately engineering your tools to argue with you is a strange discipline, and it is this essay again at the smallest possible scale: nothing warrants itself, least of all my own morale.

The days are long, and I will not pretend the solitude is noble. Building alone with machines is still building alone. The machines fill the hours and leave the room empty. In place of colleagues I have the loop's companionship: a growing shelf of receipts, green verdicts and sealed runs, the record of a thing slowly becoming real under my hands. There are worse companions than evidence, and I have had some of them, so I can rank them. The door is open, and I keep saying so in public and meaning it. The tools are laid out on the bench and the project is source-open and fair. I am buried in the details and could use collaborators. That is a standing sentence. Someday somebody will re-run my receipts on their own machine and get green verdicts with none of my hands near them. I built the project for that day from the first commit. It will be the best day of the whole project, because on that day the project stops being mine.
