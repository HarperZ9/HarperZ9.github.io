My father bonded with his stepbrothers, and they grew close. That world became part of the road that eventually took him to prison in Southern California in the early 1980s. Much of his adolescence and early adulthood happened there and became whatever version of adulthood prison had available.

The prison belonged to a history larger than my father. For much of the history of California men's prisons, people arriving at reception, the intake center where new prisoners are sorted, were officially segregated by racial category. Prisoners also enforced racial rules of their own, commonly described as prison “politics.” Race could become housing, affiliation, threat, protection and debt. It could become a rule a young person was expected to understand before he had any real power to rewrite it.

For a short time, he joined a skinhead prison gang. That carries a stigma, and it should.

I was not there. I cannot reconstruct every pressure he faced, and I will not turn a later scholarly account of California prisons, such as Tonya Lindsey's 2009 study, into a personal alibi. I can say that this mask appeared inside an institution where racial identity was more than an opinion somebody carried privately. Racial identity there was built into the environment and enforced through danger.

The environment helps explain why a mask could be offered as armor, without making the mask neutral.

A skinhead gang is not a harmless costume. People who saw that gang had reason to fear what it represented, and nobody is required to search underneath it for a gentler explanation. I believe my father was young, lost and incarcerated, and that he was trying to protect himself in a place he understood as dangerous. I do not believe racism was the conviction underneath his mask. He wore something that did not fit because he believed it might keep him alive.

The mask still entered the world, and a short chapter can leave a long stigma. A false mask can frighten somebody, and a survival strategy can carry somebody else's terror.

Some people may never forgive him, and they do not owe him forgiveness. The world can stop at the worst thing it learned about a person and stay there.

I have also seen how far he has come. He was never only the mask.

Leaving that mask did not erase the abandonment he carried, or the abandonment he later repeated.

My father had his own pattern of leaving places where he felt unwanted or unwelcome. Because of it, there was a gap of years when I did not see him. I remember learning that he had driven past one of my little league games on the way to use cocaine in the mountains and wander.

That detail stayed because it is almost absurdly specific: the game on one side of the road, disappearance on the other.

I remember being left in strange places with adults whose behavior I did not understand. I absorbed the lesson that a situation could be called normal because the child inside it lacked the vocabulary, the authority or the transportation to contest it.

My stepfather minimized problems. As I experienced it, any shortcoming that touched his pride had to become smaller than it was. When the problem could not be reduced, the person naming it could. It was easier to call me dramatic, confused or ungrateful, or to say I was asking about something that was none of my business, than to let the issue become something we could address.

Money worked the same way. When my mother and stepfather were frightened about money, a question about it got no accounting. Shame turned into anger. Financial illiteracy and spending on alcohol were protected behind a version of *that is none of your business*. A child learns two things from that answer. Resources are dangerous to discuss, and the person who asks for an account can be treated as the problem.

These habits did not begin with us.

The family history I carry includes this: my stepfather's father endured the Native boarding-school system in Klamath County, Oregon. I do not know every link between that institutional violence and the habits that followed. I know the history matters. Systems designed to separate Native children from family, language, culture and community do not leave tidy descendants.

These schools were federal policy at work. Between 1819 and 1969, the Department of the Interior identified 417 federal Indian boarding-school institutions across 37 states or then-territories. Their purpose was forced assimilation. Children were separated from kinship, language, ceremony, land and community authority. The final report of the Interior Department's Federal Indian Boarding School Initiative confirmed at least 973 child deaths and identified at least 74 marked or unmarked burial sites at 65 school locations. Those numbers are only the part of the history that the federal record could count.

In Klamath County, the Klamath Agency Boarding School opened in 1874 on the Klamath Indian Reservation. It stayed in operation until 1927, when students set it on fire.

I do not know whether my stepfather's father attended that exact school, and I will not fill a gap in family testimony with a convenient historical detail. I include the school because it belonged to the policy that surrounded the place and the lineage he came through.

I also do not know what moved every student involved in the fire.

I know the institution did not end as evidence that forced assimilation had succeeded.

When a system attacks kinship and language, descendants can inherit more than a story about something terrible that happened long ago. They may inherit missing vocabulary, interrupted caregiving, shame about where they come from, damaged community authority, distrust of institutions, and survival rules formed under domination. The fracture can enter a family as silence and later be mistaken for personality.

That history is not a voucher that cancels later harm.

People often resist that part of inheritance. We want history to condemn or to absolve, with a clean villain, victim and line of cause and a final moral balance, and real families are harder than that.

A person can be gravely harmed and still harm others, name the system that shaped him correctly and still use it as an alibi, and beg for recognition for years and then refuse it to the people closest to him.

The harms differ in weight. A child carries less responsibility than an adult. A person forced through an assimilation system and a descendant who minimizes a family member's concern are different cases. Context is not equivalence.

Transmission is still real.

I am the result of people who were alienated, shamed, neglected, displaced, intoxicated, frightened, abandoned by mothers, passed over by fathers, imprisoned, and taught distorted versions of virtue, honor, respect, morality, love and survival.

I am also a participant in what happens next.

Victimhood can be a fact. Sometimes it is the most important fact in the room. *Playing the victim* begins when I use the fact that I was harmed as jurisdiction over the people I harm. I have told myself that being silenced made my volume justice, and that being abandoned made my leaving self-protection. Adults once controlled the record, so I decided my version deserved to control it now. And because I could explain the system, I excused myself from answering for my conduct inside it.

That is how the victim can end up wearing the judge's robe.

I have spent my life fighting for my voice to be heard. I did need a voice, and I still do. The correction is learning that my voice enters a room among other voices and never above them.

I did not survive being somebody else's audience so that everyone I love could become mine.

## Three hours of solace

Some of my personal history should remain private.

Keeping it private still leaves room to admit that the architecture was there.

I entered adolescence without much self-respect or a stable identity. I had no coherent understanding of love, sex, friendship, transparency or mental illness, because I had been given contradictory instructions about all of them. Care and control were mixed together. Secrecy was called protection, and adults lied.

My first encounter with anything sexual was abuse. I was five.

I remember forgiving the person who did it before I was old enough to understand forgiveness or grief.

That was not closure.

I do not know exactly what forgiveness meant to me at five. I know I spent years living inside consequences I could not name. I carried that confusion through adolescence. It changed my self-esteem and the way I understood closeness, and it deformed the first map I was given of sex, love, relationships, friendship and transparency.

I have had relationship problems with women, with my family and with myself for most of my life. I do not need to publish every detail to tell the truth about the route.

The abuse was not my fault.

The patterns I later carried into other people's lives are still part of my responsibility.

Both sentences are true.

The house reinforced the distortion.

All I knew was being lied to and deceived while grown-ups painted pretty pictures over a world that was poisonous.

No amount of paint covered the arguments and the fighting.

On school nights I turned my stereo on and left it playing until four in the morning, trying to drown out the yelling, screaming and crying. After the house finally went quiet, I would wake around four, go lie on the couch, and listen to *SportsCenter* until I fell asleep again.

From four until seven felt like three hours of solace.

Then I got up and went to the school bus.

In seventh grade, I fell asleep in first-period history.

History was my favorite subject.

The house came to school with me. The teacher saw a child sleeping through history. My body was still trying to finish the night.

I learned that adults could make the world poisonous and still demand that the child admire the paint, and that sex and love could be tied to secrecy and coercion before I knew what either was meant to be. I also learned that mental illness could be turned into spectacle, accusation or excuse when it needed to be understood, and that transparency was demanded from children and avoided by adults.

Those lessons went deeper than belief.

They became posture, identity, desire, fear and the routes my nervous system reached for later.
