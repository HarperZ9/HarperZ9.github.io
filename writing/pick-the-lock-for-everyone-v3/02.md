## The first apology

I have said *sorry* plenty of times. I have also explained myself until the explanation became the only thing left in the room. I have collapsed under the weight of what I did, called myself a piece of shit with enough conviction that somebody else eventually had to stop being angry and start taking care of me, and then mistaken that reversal for honesty.

I have disappeared into work, substances, code, a new project, a new emergency, a new argument about why the world is arranged badly. Some of those arguments were true, which was part of what made them useful. A true explanation can still be used like a fake passport.

When I was sixteen, I was suicidal, depressed, dissociating, acting out, and swallowing almost everything I did not know how to say. I almost died from drugs, fights, self-criticism, serious illness, and the kind of reckless momentum that can feel strangely calm from inside it. I hurt people physically, mentally, and spiritually. I cheated on tests. I made video game cheats. I lied my way around consequences. I learned to find the loose board in the fence and became proud of how quickly I could get through it.

There was always another reason I was the exception. Another private court where my pain entered as evidence and everybody else's pain was treated like an accusation I had the right to cross-examine.

I know how severe that sounds. I also know how easily severity can become another performance, so let me slow down and say what I mean.

The drinking and the drugs are not the center of this story. Neither is the fact that I avoided things. Those are visible symbols of a deeper pattern: I wanted to confront something in myself, badly, but I had not learned how to do that without turning confrontation into punishment or finding a way to escape it. I demanded transformation from myself immediately, then treated the failure to transform on command as proof that I was beyond repair.

Substances offered a crude imitation of movement. For a few hours the pressure in the room shifted, and I could believe I had shifted with it. The original structure was still there in the morning, usually with more debris around it.

That does not make the substances innocent, and it does not make me their victim in a way that removes my agency. It means they belonged to a longing I did not know how to approach directly. They were one of the ways I kept asking for another life while denying myself the patience required to build one.

The other part of that pattern was something I called independence.

I distrusted authority from a young age. Some of that distrust was taught to me, and some of it was earned. I did not believe institutions had my interests at heart. I did not believe the official route was necessarily the honest one. I wanted to build around the gate.

There is something good in that instinct.

There is also something I did not want to see.

If independence means carving a path while the people who love you carry the cost of the carving, it is not independence. I refused to depend on people honestly, then depended on them to survive the consequences of that refusal. I wanted to be self-made while quietly using their time, money, patience, sleep, and concern as the floor beneath the performance.

Relationships are not transactions. Love is not an invoice. Still, when another person makes a decision with you in mind, they give something up. Maybe it is time. Maybe it is an opportunity, a night's sleep, a quieter month, or a little more faith than they can comfortably afford. Respecting that sacrifice is not transactional. Pretending it does not count can become exploitation.

My thoughts move quickly when I am in motion. Pride, shame, victimhood, resentment, tenderness, hunger, and a fairly predatory view of humanity can all be arguing over the steering wheel before I have decided what I actually believe. I take the fastest neural route available, say the thing with the most voltage, and then spend days doing relationship maintenance and emotional janitorial work.

I explain what I meant. I repair the part that landed wrong. I reassure people that the worst version of the sentence was not the whole of me.

Meanwhile, the original issue is still sitting there.

Self-condemnation is another fast route. I can say, "I am a piece of shit, and I probably will be tomorrow," and there is truth in the fact that one essay does not change my habits by morning. But the sentence can still become a shortcut. It flattens every specific harm into one total identity, then leaves nothing to repair because the case has already been closed. Shame gets to feel profound while doing very little work.

I am not asking for release from judgment. I am not asking the reader to replace consequences with sympathy because I finally found language for the pattern. I want to sit here. Then I want to stand up, walk where I can be seen, and accept that some people may make a mockery of me.

I have spent years trying to control that possibility. I have lied, redirected, disappeared, charmed, become angry, become fragile, or become too busy whenever a room began arriving at a verdict I could not edit.

That is not accountability.

It is fear with an architectural plan.

## The day the Earth Fair ended

There is another childhood dream I have carried for years.

In the dream, my family and I were preparing to visit the Earth Fair, an event my parents had taken me to when I was very young. I wanted desperately to return. When we reached the campground, however, the fair was gone.

There was only a wall and a door.

I walked to the door and opened it.

Beyond it, roughly twelve flights of stairs descended into an industrial factory. The machines below had been built to appear as monsters. As soon as they saw me, they began chasing me back up the stairs and out through the door.

The Earth Fair was closed.

I do not treat dreams as evidence, prophecy, or diagnosis. I treat this one as an image my mind refused to discard. A place I remembered as open, living, communal, and full of possibility had become a wall. The invitation had become a door into a factory. The factory had made machines in the shape of monsters, and being seen by them was enough to trigger pursuit.

Maybe that is too much to ask of a dream. It is still difficult not to recognize the architecture.

We are repeatedly invited toward a fair and delivered to a factory. We are told a system exists for everyone, then discover that the door is controlled by people who benefit from keeping the stairs hidden. We are promised education and receive debt. We are promised connection and receive surveillance. We are promised creative abundance and receive a marketplace in which the platform owns the route to the audience. We are promised democratized intelligence and receive a metered pipe.

The machines do not need to hate the child. They only need to be built so that his presence counts as an error.

That is one reason *pick the lock for everyone* cannot mean finding a private route for myself. If I learn the door, enter the factory, and close it behind me, I have not escaped the architecture.

I have joined its staff.

## A childhood built as somebody else's stage

My childhood often felt like a stage on which other people needed to perform their emergencies.

My mother had emotions that deserved somewhere to exist. The way I experienced our home, however, she often needed the whole room to become that place. Rather than being given somewhere to feel, I learned to become audience, technician, prop, interruption, or witness. I became attentive to the emotional weather because the weather decided what the room was allowed to contain.

That is not the complete truth of my mother.

It is the architecture I remember.

She did not begin with much room either.

My mother never met her own mother. Her mother left when she was born. My maternal grandfather remarried, and the woman he married seemed to carry some kind of disdain toward the bond between father and daughter, perhaps toward women more generally. I cannot know exactly what lived inside that relationship. I know my mother grew up without the kind of recognition a child should be able to expect from the people responsible for loving her.

Later I saw some of that wound return as jealousy and competition. Another woman could become a threat. The fear could turn toward the woman, toward my father, toward my stepfather, or toward the relationship itself. Instead of the concern being addressed, it was usually minimized. The minimization did not settle the fear.

It added fuel.

When hurt people enter relationships with other people in pain, explanation does not make the outcomes predictable.

A fracture does not remain politely at the generation where it began. A child who was not chosen may grow into an adult who reads ordinary distance as abandonment. A daughter can become proof that her mother is loved, then seem like a rival when she becomes separate. A partner can be asked to answer for a parent he never met. A room can contain a missing person for decades after everybody stops saying the name.

None of that is destiny.

It is one way absence recruits later relationships.

I can see how easily I reproduce the same architecture. I can turn pain into an event everybody else must attend. I can talk about being unheard until nobody around me can speak. I can demand recognition in ways that make recognition indistinguishable from surrender. I can make another person's boundary feel like abandonment and then punish them for reminding me they are separate.

A person denied a stage can spend the rest of his life seizing one.

My father inherited another version of the same absence.

His father, my paternal grandfather, remarried a woman who already had six children. He became a stepfather to them while creating distance from his own son. My father watched his father give himself to another household while leaving less and less of himself available to the child who was already his.

The family account I inherited is that the woman was having sex with other men behind his back.

While his father was betraying him, she was betraying his father.

The betrayals were not the same. The sentence names the house my father was learning from. The person who should have chosen him kept turning elsewhere, and the person his father had chosen was turning elsewhere too.
