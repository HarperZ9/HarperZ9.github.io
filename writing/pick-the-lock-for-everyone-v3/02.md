## The first apology

I have said *sorry* plenty of times, and I have explained myself until the explanation was the only thing left in the room. I have collapsed under the weight of what I did and called myself a piece of shit with enough conviction that somebody else eventually had to stop being angry and start taking care of me, and I have mistaken that reversal for honesty.

I have disappeared into work, substances and code, into a new project, a new emergency or a new argument about why the world is arranged badly. Some of those arguments were true, and that was part of what made them useful, because a true explanation can still be used like a fake passport.

When I was sixteen, I was suicidal, depressed and dissociating, which means feeling cut off from my own body and surroundings. I acted out and swallowed almost everything I did not know how to say. I almost died from drugs, fights, self-criticism, serious illness and the kind of reckless momentum that can feel strangely calm from inside. I hurt people physically, mentally and spiritually. I cheated on tests and made video game cheats, and I lied my way around consequences. I learned to find the loose board in the fence, and I was proud of how quickly I could get through it.

I always had another reason to be the exception. I kept a private court where my pain came in as evidence and everybody else's pain came in as an accusation I had the right to cross-examine.

I know how severe that sounds, and I know how easily severity can turn into another performance. So I will slow down and say what I mean.

The drinking and the drugs are not the center of this story. Neither is my habit of avoiding things. Both are visible signs of a deeper pattern. I badly wanted to confront something in myself. I had not learned to do it without turning the confrontation into punishment or finding a way to escape it. I demanded that I change at once, and when I could not change on command I took that as proof that I was beyond repair.

Substances gave me a crude imitation of movement. For a few hours the pressure in the room shifted, and I could believe I had shifted with it. In the morning the original structure was still there, usually with more debris around it.

None of this makes the substances innocent, or makes me their victim in a way that removes my agency. They belonged to a longing I did not know how to approach directly. Through them I kept asking for another life while I denied myself the patience it takes to build one.

The other part of that pattern was something I called independence.

I distrusted authority from a young age. Some of that distrust was taught to me, and some of it was earned. I did not believe that institutions had my interests at heart or that the official route was necessarily the honest one. I wanted to build around the gate.

That instinct has something good in it, along with something I did not want to see.

When the people who love you pay for the path you carve, the word independence stops fitting. I refused to depend on people honestly, and then I depended on them to survive the consequences of that refusal. I wanted to be self-made, and all the while I quietly used their time, money, patience, sleep and concern as the floor under the performance.

I do not mean that relationships are transactions or that love is an invoice. Still, when another person makes a decision with you in mind, they give something up. Maybe it is time. Maybe it is an opportunity, a night's sleep, a quieter month, or a little more faith than they can comfortably afford. You can honor that sacrifice without keeping score, and pretending it does not count can become exploitation.

My thoughts move quickly when I am in motion. Pride, shame, victimhood, resentment, tenderness, hunger and a fairly predatory view of humanity can all be fighting over the steering wheel before I have decided what I believe. I take the fastest neural route available and say the thing with the most voltage. Then I spend days on relationship maintenance and emotional janitorial work.

I explain what I meant and fix the part that landed wrong. I reassure people that the worst version of the sentence was not the whole of me.

Meanwhile, the original issue is still sitting there.

Self-condemnation is another fast route. I can say, "I am a piece of shit, and I probably will be tomorrow," and there is truth in that, because one essay does not change my habits by morning. The sentence can still become a shortcut. It flattens every specific harm into one total identity and closes the case. Nothing is left to repair, and the shame feels deep while it does very little work.

I am not asking for release from judgment, and I do not want the reader's sympathy to stand in for consequences just because I finally found language for the pattern. I want to sit here first. Then I want to stand up, walk where I can be seen, and accept that some people may make a mockery of me.

I have spent years trying to control that possibility. Whenever a room has begun to arrive at a verdict I could not edit, I have lied, redirected, disappeared or charmed, or I have turned angry, fragile or too busy. All of that is fear with an architectural plan, and none of it is accountability.

## The day the Earth Fair ended

There is another childhood dream I have carried for years.

In the dream, my family and I were getting ready to visit the Earth Fair, an event my parents had taken me to when I was very young. I wanted desperately to go back. When we reached the campground, the fair was gone.

There was only a wall and a door.

I walked to the door and opened it.

Beyond it, roughly twelve flights of stairs went down into an industrial factory. The machines below had been built to look like monsters. As soon as they saw me, they chased me back up the stairs and out through the door.

The Earth Fair was closed.

I do not read dreams as evidence, prophecy or diagnosis. I hold this one as an image my mind refused to discard. A place I remembered as open, living, communal and full of possibility had become a wall. The invitation had become a door into a factory. The factory had made machines in the shape of monsters, and being seen by them was enough to set off the chase.

Maybe that asks too much of a dream. It is still hard not to recognize the architecture.

Again and again we are invited toward a fair and delivered to a factory. We hear that a system exists for everyone, then find that the door is controlled by people who benefit from keeping the stairs hidden. Education arrives as debt, connection as surveillance, and creative abundance as a marketplace where the platform owns the route to the audience. The promise of AI for everyone, sometimes called democratized intelligence, arrives as a metered pipe that sells access by the unit.

The machines chase the child whether or not they hate him, because they were built so that his presence counts as an error.

That is one reason *pick the lock for everyone*, the title of this essay, cannot mean a private route for me. It means a way through that other people can use too. If I learn the door, enter the factory and close the door behind me, I have joined the architecture's staff.

## A childhood built as somebody else's stage

My childhood often felt like a stage on which other people needed to perform their emergencies.

My mother had emotions that deserved somewhere to exist. As I experienced our home, she often needed the whole room to become that place. My own feelings had nowhere to go, so I learned to be audience, technician, prop, interruption or witness. I watched the emotional weather closely, because the weather decided what the room was allowed to hold.

This is the architecture I remember. It is only part of the truth about my mother.

She did not begin with much room either.

My mother never met her own mother, who left when she was born. My maternal grandfather remarried. His new wife seemed to carry some kind of disdain toward the bond between father and daughter, and perhaps toward women more generally. I cannot know exactly what lived inside that relationship. I know my mother grew up without the recognition a child should be able to expect from the people responsible for loving her.

Later I saw some of that wound come back as jealousy and competition. Another woman could become a threat. The fear could turn toward the woman, toward my father, toward my stepfather, or toward the relationship itself. Usually the concern was minimized and left unaddressed, and the minimizing added fuel to the fear.

When hurt people form relationships with other people in pain, no explanation makes the outcome predictable.

A fracture does not stay politely in the generation where it began. A child who was not chosen may grow into an adult who reads ordinary distance as abandonment. A daughter can become proof that her mother is loved, and then look like a rival once she becomes her own person. Sometimes a partner is asked to answer for a parent he never met. And a room can hold a missing person for decades after everybody stops saying the name.

This is one way an absence recruits later relationships, and none of it is destiny.

A person denied a stage can spend the rest of his life seizing one, and I can see how easily I rebuild the same architecture. I can turn pain into an event everybody else must attend, or talk about being unheard until nobody around me can speak. My demand for recognition can grow until recognizing me looks the same as surrendering to me. Another person's boundary can feel like abandonment to me, and then I can punish them for reminding me that they are separate.

My father inherited another version of the same absence.

His father, my paternal grandfather, remarried a woman who already had six children. He became a stepfather to them and put distance between himself and his own son. My father watched his father give himself to another household and leave less and less of himself for the child who was already his.

The family account I inherited is that the woman was having sex with other men behind my grandfather's back.

While my grandfather was betraying my father, she was betraying my grandfather.

The two betrayals were different. Together they describe the house my father was learning from. The person who should have chosen him kept turning elsewhere, and the person his father had chosen was turning elsewhere too.
