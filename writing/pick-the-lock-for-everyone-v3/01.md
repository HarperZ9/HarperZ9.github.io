# Pick the Lock for Everyone

## An apology to the Mad-Happy Scientist, an argument about inheritance, forgiveness, and accountable creation, and a question about what survives us

*Zain Dana Harper, July 2026*

> **Content note:** This essay discusses childhood sexual abuse, childhood neglect, parental substance use, domestic conflict, incarceration, brief affiliation with a racist prison gang, historical enslavement and forced family separation, Native boarding-school history, serious illness, suicidal depression, dissociation, violence, misogyny, shame, and harm to other people. It does not describe methods. The family history is presented as first-person memory and inherited account, not as a complete adjudication of other people's lives.

## The sentence that would not come out

One of the oldest dreams I remember begins at a park.

My family is gone. I do not know where they went or how to find them. Another family is in the park, and I run toward them because they look like people who might help. I try to explain that I am lost.

I open my mouth.

Nothing comes out.

I have spent most of my life trying to finish that sentence.

That is true. It is also not innocent. A person who spends a life fighting to be heard can start to treat every room as the hearing he was denied. He can mistake having a voice for having the floor and make his pain the price of everybody else's attention, and he can work so hard to prove that he exists that the people around him begin to disappear.

I know because I have done it.

Before I could explain any of this, books gave me side doors.

Shel Silverstein did not write toward me as though childhood were a waiting room for adulthood. His poems were mischievous, dark and impossible, and they let a child suspect that the official map was incomplete. In *Where the Sidewalk Ends*, he writes:

> “For the children, they mark, and the children, they know.”

I knew that sentence before I understood what I knew. Children notice the boundary because adults have not yet finished teaching them that the pavement is reality and that whatever grows past it is irresponsible. The end of the sidewalk can be danger. It can also be the place where an inherited route stops pretending to be the whole earth.

Silverstein trusted the child with edges.

*The Giving Tree* gave me another kind of edge. I never read it as a clean lesson about generosity. I saw affection, hunger, use, delight, depletion and a stump in the same relationship. I could feel love in the giving. I could also feel something unsettling in the way the giving went on until almost nothing recognizable was left.

Was the tree fulfilled, or consumed? And the boy: loved, entitled, desperate, ordinary, or all of those things at different ages?

The book left the moral account open, the way a children's book sometimes survives by refusing to close it, and that is part of why it stayed with me.

I had already seen adults call depletion love. Later I became capable of taking another person's time, patience, attention or belief until they felt reduced, and then of treating their exhaustion as evidence that they did not love me enough. I learned the habit elsewhere. Silverstein gave me an image I would argue with for years.

Jack Prelutsky gave language knees and elbows. He had studied music and still sang, and you can hear the training in his words. In his poems, rhythm was the way meaning moved. An impossible creature could be funny enough to get into a child's head, and a sentence was free to jump around like the creature it described, with no report to file.

Dr. Seuss made constraint look like motion. The Beginner Books were built to help children read on their own through simple words, visual clues, rhyme and repetition, and through an almost unreasonable confidence that a limit could turn into play. The vocabulary did not have to be enormous for the room to feel enormous.

That may be one reason I later loved code, where a rule can be an instrument as well as a cage.

Beverly Cleary gave ordinary children the dignity of an inner weather. In her books a child did not need a catastrophe before embarrassment, boredom, resentment, curiosity or a sense of injustice was allowed to count. A misunderstanding with an adult could matter. A parent's job, a bill, a sister, a teacher or a bad afternoon could change the whole climate without first becoming a grand symbol. Her children were people living through the day, never props in an adult's lesson.

P. D. Eastman's *Are You My Mother?* gave me a baby bird who starts searching before he even knows what the person he needs is supposed to look like. He asks the wrong animals and the wrong machines the right question.

How much of adult life is that question in another costume?

Are you my mother?

Will you recognize me, tell me I belong, become the person I lost before I knew what losing meant?

We ask it of partners, employers, institutions, audiences, ideologies, communities and machines. We ask a job to parent the part of us that never felt useful, and a lover to prove that no one will leave again. Then we ask recognition to turn into love, and we get angry when applause cannot hold us.

A child denied recognition can spend decades confusing recognition with rescue. I read like someone trying to find another climate, and never like a student preparing to answer questions.

For a while I wanted to be an entomologist, then a marine biologist. I wanted the small worlds under leaves and the enormous worlds under water. At another point my ambition was to become a geographical atlas and an encyclopedia. Neither one is a profession, and it was still the ambition. Consulting the map was never enough for me. I wanted to grow large enough to hold where everything went.

I read and read and read, because knowledge seemed to offer what the adults around me could not give me consistently: a world where a question could lead somewhere other than shame.

What book gave you another climate?

What story, game, field, song, instrument, machine or map let some part of you exist without first being cross-examined?

Maybe you no longer remember the title.

Your body may still remember the door.

Music became another language before I was ready for language.

Billy Strayhorn's *A Flower Is a Lovesome Thing* gave delicacy enough room to be serious. Strayhorn composed, arranged and wrote for Duke Ellington's orchestra for three decades. Their musical languages grew so closely together that scholars have sometimes had trouble deciding where one ended and the other began. He lived openly as a gay Black man in an era that made that openness dangerous. He contributed enormously from a position often treated as background, and while he was alive he did not receive public credit equal to the work.

I do not use that history to reduce the music to biography. The piece reached me before the biography did, and what reached me was permission to be delicate without becoming trivial, the way a flower can be serious without armor.

Being underrecognized does not make work more beautiful, though it does make the question of who gets heard harder to ignore. A person can help create the language of an era and still stand partly in somebody else's shadow. The work can survive that imbalance, and the imbalance stays unjust.

Funkadelic's *Maggot Brain* gave longing somewhere to live without explanation. George Clinton opens the track with eight words:

> “Mother Earth is pregnant for the third time.”

Then Eddie Hazel's guitar comes in. In the account Clinton gave later, he asked Hazel to imagine the grief of his mother's death and to let what was inside him move through the instrument. Hazel recorded the long solo in one take.

The guitar metabolizes grief without explaining it. It circles, tears, pleads, pulls back and returns, and it keeps finding more feeling inside the same few materials. I heard a nervous system that refused to stay trapped in prose. The virtuosity never registered with me as a credential.

Later, after wading through the dubstep era, I found drum 'n' bass. It sounded like the future had learned to run. It also sounded like the speed at which my thoughts, ideas and synapses moved from point to point.

I learned later that the future I heard had ancestors. Jungle and drum 'n' bass belong to a living Black British history shaped by Caribbean sound-system culture, breakbeats, reggae, dub, soul, hip-hop, rave, technology, migration and community. A sound system is a crew with its own mobile speaker rig, playing records at street parties and dances. A breakbeat is a few bars of drumming lifted from an older record and looped into a new track. The music carried old recordings into new pressure. It cut, repeated, sped up and rearranged inherited material.

The breakbeat is a memory reorganized into motion.

That idea mattered to the philosophy I built much later. Ancestry is never pure, and nothing new arrives free of influence. Something new can grow out of inherited material without lying about the material that made it possible.

What music finished a sentence for you?

What sound gave your body somewhere to put what your house, school, church, job or family could not receive?

I have moved through genres the way I moved through books, through highs, lows and intense mids, looking for a form that could hold what ordinary conversation could not.

None of those works rescued me, and art does not have to. They gave me rooms where an experience could exist before it had won an argument.

Art can keep a human possibility alive until someone finds language for it. An apology for me, a check on a scientific claim, restored trust and repair for a person I hurt all need a source outside art.

Somewhere in those rooms was the child I used to imagine becoming. I called him the Mad-Happy Scientist.

He was not thinking about prestige. He wanted research, graphics, code, biology, mathematics, physics, strange instruments and the feeling of asking a question nobody had answered yet. He wanted to take the broken thing apart to see whether the failure had a shape, to be wrong in an interesting direction, and to laugh when something finally moved.

The first sentence I need to finish for him is unflattering.

I have never apologized.

Not in a way that counted.
