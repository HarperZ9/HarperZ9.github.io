I did not choose my initial nervous system, my parents, their parents, the schools and substances and songs and silences already waiting for me, the economy around the household, the neighborhood, the available language for manhood, or the moment in history when I became old enough to interpret any of it. My mother did not choose to begin life without her mother or author every condition that later made attention and loyalty feel scarce. My father did not invent the abandonment he later repeated, or the prison world in which a dangerous mask could seem safer than an undefended face. My stepfather did not create the boarding-school system whose damage entered the family before him.

If responsibility requires being the original author of every cause that formed us, responsibility is impossible.

That conclusion can sound compassionate. It can also become an excuse to turn people into weather.

I do not want that.

A storm cannot answer me. A person can.

To hold someone responsible is not to claim they created themselves from nothing. It is to address them as a source inside the world, a person through whom an action passed, who can recognize what happened, answer for it, participate in repair, and change what happens next. Sourcehood can be real without being self-origination.

I am not the source of the source of me.

I am still one of the sources of what leaves me.

That is a harder freedom than the fantasy of complete independence. It gives me no pristine self outside history, but it also gives history no final ownership of my hands.

The causal chain runs through hormones, neurons, parents, institutions, money, language, culture, and chance. It still runs through me. I inhabit the point where those conditions become an action another person has to live with.

That is where answerability enters.

The system caused much. Each of us still plays a part, but not the same part and not under the same power. The language of *we all play the victim* becomes dangerous when it flattens a child and a parent, the colonized and the institution, the person harmed and the person currently deciding whether to continue the harm. Shared humanity does not mean equal responsibility.

What it means is that nobody arrives self-made, including the person I most want to condemn.

That recognition is the beginning of forgiveness for me.

Not the end of judgment.

## Forgiveness without deletion

Forgiveness has been used to pressure people into silence, reunion, politeness, restored access, and spiritual performance. I am not interested in that version.

Nobody owes forgiveness to the person who hurt them.

Forgiveness is not exoneration. It is not a finding that the act was acceptable, unavoidable, mutual, or too old to matter. It is not forgetting. It is not trust. It is not reconciliation. It is not an invitation back into the house. It does not cancel restitution, consequence, distance, anger, grief, or the right to say no.

Forgiveness is not deleting the receipt.

For me, forgiveness is the refusal to let the injury become the only author left.

I can forgive my father without calling the empty bleachers full. I can see the boy whose father turned toward six other children while moving away from him. I can see the young man who entered a Southern California prison and wore a skinhead mask that did not fit because he believed it might keep him alive. I do not have to call the mask harmless, and I do not get to demand that anyone else forgive it. I can say that the symbol entered the world, that people had reason to fear it, and that he was responsible for wearing it. I can also say the mask was not the whole person, that he moved beyond it, and that the worst chapter a person survives does not have to become the only chapter anybody is permitted to read. I can still say I was the child at the game, he was the adult, and the road he chose had a consequence.

I can forgive my mother without saying I had enough room to feel. I can see the child who never met her mother, the daughter whose bond with her father may have been treated as a threat, and the woman who learned to experience other women through competition and possible loss. I can understand why minimization made the fear louder. I can also say that her fear could take the room, and that a child should not have to become the permanent audience for a parent's uncontained life.

I can forgive my stepfather without agreeing that the problem was small. I can understand how pride protects a person who learned that exposure brings humiliation. I can honor the lineage of a Native boarding-school survivor without converting that suffering into a pardon for every harm that followed.

I can forgive the person who abused me without pretending I received closure, without calling what happened mutual, and without reopening the door. I had already tried to forgive before I knew what the word meant. Adult forgiveness cannot be demanded from that child retroactively. The act remains wrong. The person remains responsible. My refusal to organize the rest of my life around hatred does not revise the charge.

To honor an ancestor is not to certify every act.

It is to see a person as more than the worst thing they transmitted. It is to remember the conditions that narrowed their choices without pretending those conditions removed every choice. It is to carry forward what kept life alive and refuse what life should no longer require.

Some of our ancestors survived through silence. Some through obedience. Some through rage. Some through alcohol. Some through leaving before they could be left. Some through making themselves indispensable. Some through becoming hard enough that nobody could reach the wound again.

Those strategies may be part of why we are here.

That does not mean our children should need them.

Honoring the dead can mean ending the job an adaptation was hired to do.

I want my children to inherit names, stories, humor, music, recipes, curiosity, endurance, and the knowledge that people before them made it through conditions designed to erase them. I do not want them to inherit every unfinished alarm. I do not want them to confuse love with depletion, secrecy with dignity, money with shame, masculinity with possession, vigilance with wisdom, or disappearance with peace.

Forgiveness can face backward, but its work is for the future.

The same is true of self-forgiveness.

Self-forgiveness is not a private acquittal. If I forgive myself by deciding my intentions were pure, my trauma was sufficient explanation, or my shame has already punished me enough, I have built another private court.

Real self-forgiveness has to preserve the charge.

I did this.

It affected you.

You do not owe me reassurance.

I may not be able to return what was taken.

I am still responsible for what can be repaired and for the conditions I create around the pattern now.

Then self-forgiveness refuses one additional claim: that because I did harm, harm is the only honest future available to me.

The positions can be flipped more ways than any clean story allows.

You may be waiting for the world to issue a certificate saying you are redeemable.

It may never come.

The people you hurt may never see the person you become. The institution may retain the old label. The search result may preserve the worst chapter. Somebody may refuse the context because the context cannot undo what they had to survive.

Self-forgiveness cannot depend on winning the appeal.

It has to begin without overruling the people who still say no.

In one scene, you are the child passed over.

In another, you are the adult driving past.

In one place, the mask is how you survive.

In another, you are the person frightened by what the mask means.

I do not know whether everybody eventually does something that makes them feel irredeemable.

I know some of us do.

The world may never forgive the thing. The people harmed may never forgive it. A person may carry a stigma long after the danger, belief, or desperation that produced the act is gone. None of that creates an entitlement to a cleaner record.

But if the only verdict I permit is *irredeemable*, permanent self-hatred becomes another excuse not to change. I can become loyal to the worst version of myself because at least the role is known.

At some point, we still have to pick up what remains and keep going.

Not because the past disappeared.

Because the future is still receiving what we do next.

Without that refusal, shame becomes loyalty to the wound. I keep repeating the pattern because repetition feels like the only sentence severe enough to prove I understand it. I destroy myself and call the destruction moral clarity.

Self-destruction removes the person who owes the work.

Forgiveness leaves the person alive and removes the fantasy that annihilation is repair.

It does not make the work smaller.

It makes the work possible.

## The river

At sixteen I experienced acute renal failure and spent time in the children's intensive care unit at Randall Children's Hospital. During that hospitalization, I had a dream in which I experienced what I imagined death might be.

I was floating on a sturdy raft in a river. The river was healthy in size, but not so wide, deep, or fast that it frightened me. The water relieved the pain inside my body and organs. The raft drifted gently to the shore, and I began climbing a hill covered in trees.

Near the top I saw two brontosauruses playing tag, or doing something close enough to tag that the name did not matter.

I felt happy.

The pain had subsided, and I was able to witness other creatures experiencing a kind of uncomplicated joy I remembered having felt at points in my own life.

I do not claim I died. I was sick, frightened, medicated, and dreaming. I also do not need to reduce the dream to a clinical explanation before admitting that it stayed with me.

What I recognize now is that the dream did not make disappearance beautiful.

It made relief beautiful.

Those are not the same thing.

When the red button appears to promise an end to contradiction, pain, accountability, or shame, it often lies about what is being requested. The desire may not be for the self to end. It may be for the pain to stop, for the pursuit to stop, for the performance to stop, for the body to become bearable, or for the person to reach a shore where nobody is demanding another explanation.

The river gives me a counterimage.
