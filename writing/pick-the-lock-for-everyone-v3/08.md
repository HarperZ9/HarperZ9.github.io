At the same time, do not make failure the only data the family keeps. A system trained only on error becomes distorted in another way. Children should see repair succeed too: an adult who pauses, comes back, apologizes without falling apart, accepts a no, changes a plan, tells the truth about money, asks for help without making the helper responsible for everything, and keeps a promise after the strong feeling has passed.

Verified experience should include the good things that happened.

The flywheel has limits, and its own thesis states an honest ceiling. Recirculating experience can make that experience more useful. It cannot create coverage of situations the system never met. A model cannot learn a domain it has never seen by replaying what it already has.

Neither can a family.

Sometimes the new experience has to come from outside the family line: another culture, a teacher, a therapist, a friend, a partner, a book, a community, a spiritual practice, a medication, a boundary, an institution that works differently, or a child who asks a question nobody in the family learned how to answer.

That outside experience is new coverage. It widens what the family has seen and contaminates nothing.

The mistake is believing healing has to be built only from materials already inside the wound.

## The robe and the farm

Kahlil Gibran gave me one of the images that holds this argument together.

In his 1912 novel *The Broken Wings*, a bishop forces Selma Karamy into marriage with his nephew for the sake of her father's wealth. Her defeat has nothing to do with how well she argues. No fair hearing exists for her. The bishop controls the standard, benefits from the decision, and sits above any real appeal. His robe makes persuasion unnecessary. Power has dressed itself up as propriety. Everyone around it understands what is happening, and they still behave as though proper process took place.

That structure is the cage. A system wears the robe when the judge writes the standard, profits from the verdict, and stops the people living under it from checking the decision again. The costume changes while the structure can stay the same.

A university can define merit, sell access to the definition, and point to the credential as proof the definition worked. A publisher can let prestige stand in for reasoning that readers can inspect. A benchmark, a standard test used to rank performance, can keep handing out authority even when its tests cannot reject a wrong answer. A technology company can define safety, originality, capability, or acceptable use while keeping the evidence needed to challenge those definitions inside the company.

A platform can swallow culture, own the route to the audience, generate substitutes for the people whose work fed it, and set the price of being seen. It can still present itself as a neutral marketplace.

Creative communities can wear the robe too. I do not say that to dismiss the real harm being done to artists. A human-only practice can matter. The process can be part of what the work means. Consent, labor, identity, chosen limits, and tradition matter. A community is allowed to say, "This space is for people working under these conditions."

The problem starts when pedigree gets smuggled in under the name of ethics. Familiarity starts to pass for authenticity, difficulty for virtue, and membership for merit. The old gate feels human because people spent years learning how to survive inside it.

I wore the robe whenever I decided my intention mattered more than someone else's experience. I wrote the standard and benefited from the verdict. I made appeal expensive through anger, absence, charm, collapse, threats, or a sudden show of being too broken to answer a plain question. Hating the robe did not stop me from putting it on.

That matters because I have been anti-authoritarian, opposed to rule by unchecked power, for most of my life. I know the tidy version of that story: I distrusted power, found the weak point, and built alternatives for the people kept outside.

Sometimes that is true.

In another version, I hated being ruled and did not notice how often I was trying to become impossible to rule.

Pink Floyd gave me the sound of that contradiction before I had words for it. Their album *Animals* shaped much of my early criticism and wonder. I heard it before I knew what class analysis was, and before I had useful words for institutions or economic power.

I understood the animals anyway: dogs that keep running until the chase replaces the purpose, pigs that turn appetite into moral authority, sheep that move with the flock because leaving feels more dangerous right now than wherever the flock is headed.

The record sounded industrial and biological at the same time. It had concrete, machinery, isolation, instinct, and hierarchy in it, and huge human structures acting as though they were simply nature.

Then Orwell gave me the farm. *Animal Farm* taught me a distinction I have had to learn again and again: removing the owner does not necessarily remove ownership. A revolution can change the slogans, the names on the wall, and the people in the farmhouse. The grammar of the relationship underneath can survive all of it. The rebel can inherit the robe, and the animal who learned to spot the farmer can still want the farmer's chair.

I took that in as political criticism long before I understood it as a warning about myself.

At first the animals were always other people. In my reading the dogs were executives, enforcers, climbers, and anyone willing to bite for a place near power; the pigs were politicians, preachers, billionaires, administrators, and founders who turned coordination into ownership; and the sheep were the people who followed, repeated, and went along. That reading conveniently left my own category empty.

It took me longer to admit that all three animals can live in the same person, sometimes in the same afternoon.

I have been the dog, chasing prestige, recognition, relief, capability, money, and the hope of becoming impressive enough to quiet the private argument about whether I deserved to exist. I have kept chasing goals after the chase began hurting the people walking beside me. I have mistaken movement for direction and intensity for sincerity.

I have been the pig. I have criticized hypocrisy while protecting my own. I have talked about liberation while expecting other people to absorb the cost of my comfort and my instability. I have treated seeing clearly as a kind of entitlement, as though seeing the cage excused me from examining what I was doing inside it.

I have been the sheep. I have followed inherited ideas about masculinity, success, substances, survival, resentment, and independence. I have called my behavior self-directed because it pushed against an authority, even when that authority still set my direction by giving me something to oppose.

These are roles, patterns, and capacities under incentives, and none of them is a species: no dog is born to hunt forever, no pig is corrupt by nature, and a sheep is no lesser kind of person.

Persistence can turn into predation when the target replaces the reason for running. When the person coordinating a system starts to believe everything passing through it belongs to them, stewardship can become ownership. Trust and learning from others can slide into submission once the group loses the right to question where it is going.

Criticism means little if it cannot turn around and look at me.

I want the conditions to change, and a better bishop, a kinder pig, or a founder so persuasive that nobody asks for an appeal would not change them. The standard should not belong entirely to the person it judges or the person who profits from it. The record should survive embarrassment. The people affected should have somewhere to stand when they disagree. The work should be able to condemn the worker.


## The bundle breaks

These contradictions predate artificial intelligence, which made them move faster and harder to hide.

For a long time, difficult work was tied to the road the worker took to get there. A solved problem implied study. A polished illustration implied practice. Working code implied some contact with the machinery underneath it. The contribution and the life story of the person who made it traveled together so closely that we built institutions around the bundle.

Credentials, interviews, seniority, portfolios, peer review, code review, guilds, and reputation became stand-ins. They let people judge work without inspecting all of it, because full inspection was expensive.

Then the bundle split open.

A person can now produce work that looks expert without traveling the expected road. The surface no longer tells you how much understanding, labor, intention, borrowed structure, or machine help sits underneath it.

I feel the freedom in that.

I missed the official road into research. For years I treated that miss as a ruling on whether I was allowed to discover anything. These tools gave me a way back toward the questions. They can translate a paper, explain the same idea until one version lands, help someone outside an institution learn that the question they keep circling already has a name, and let one person attempt work that once needed a department and a budget.

Pedigree has shut out capable people for reasons that had little to do with what they could contribute: money, disability, geography, race, language, family obligations, neurotype (how a person's brain happens to work), knowing how institutions work, social manner, timing, and the thousand accidents that decide who gets a clean runway.

I will not pretend that this opening means nothing, or that it arrived clean.

Work that looks expert is cheap now, and expertise still costs what it did. Generating work got faster while checking it stayed costly, so the cost moved downstream: software maintainers read the pull requests, the code changes people submit; teachers work out whether a student understands the code; editors sort the plausible from the real; researchers rebuild the proof; and clients and audiences find out which polished surface had nothing underneath it.

I call that review debt. Somebody pays it, even when the bill stays out of sight.

Creative workers pay a different version. Their craft was more than a badge. It paid rent, built identity and community, and gave shape to years of practice. When a client can ask for ten plausible images in an afternoon, the illustrator gets a lower rate, a shorter deadline, or no call at all, and none of it arrives as an abstract seminar about technological progress.

You can tell that person that difficulty was never identical to quality, and it is true.
