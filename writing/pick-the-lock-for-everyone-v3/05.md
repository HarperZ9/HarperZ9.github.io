Civilizations behave this way too. They preserve some names and erase others. They call an adaptation tradition, a conquest order, a wound destiny, and a profitable arrangement nature. They build archives around what the powerful needed remembered and unconformities around what they needed forgotten. Then descendants inherit the story and mistake inheritance for evidence that the arrangement was inevitable.

Humanity is not one household, and the language of family should not sentimentalize conquest, slavery, genocide, colonialism, caste, war, or incarceration. Institutions can organize violence at a scale no private relationship can reproduce.

After emancipation, formerly enslaved people placed newspaper advertisements searching for children, parents, siblings, spouses, and other relatives separated through sale and the domestic slave trade. Some searches continued decades after the original separation. The National Archives-supported *Last Seen* project preserves thousands of those notices.

Those notices are not metaphors for my family.

They are evidence of what happens when a system claims authority over kinship itself.

The system treated a person as property and a relationship as something that could be sold apart. The survivors kept publishing the relation back into the world.

Have you seen my mother?

Have you seen my child?

Do you know the name they use now?

The words are simple because the need beneath them is not.

I think of the baby bird asking, *Are you my mother?* I have to keep the scale honest. A children's book is not the history of slavery. The connection does not make the harms comparable. It shows that recognition, name, return, and the right to belong are not childish needs. They are among the first structures power attacks when it wants people easier to control.

A system can fracture a family and then leave descendants to explain the fracture as personality.

But people still learn the institution in rooms.

At tables.

In schools.

Through jokes.

Through who is believed.

Through whose pain becomes history and whose pain becomes attitude.

You did not choose the first language the world used to explain itself to you.

You are still responsible for what that language lets you do once you can hear it.

## The unconformity

I wanted to become an atlas because maps made absence visible.

Geology goes further.

Geology is the archive after the author has lost the right of reply.

A rock cannot explain what it meant. A layer cannot tell us it was misunderstood. The record has to be read from composition, position, fracture, pressure, contact, and what remains on either side of what is missing.

In an ordinary sequence of sedimentary layers, the older material lies below and the younger material accumulates above it. Geologists call that superposition. The idea feels intuitive enough to become metaphor immediately: childhood underneath adolescence, one generation underneath the next, history stacking itself until the latest surface forgets how much weight it rests upon.

But a rock record is not a perfect stack.

It erodes.

It folds.

It faults.

It is buried, lifted, heated, weathered, dissolved, and exposed again. A layer may never be deposited. A layer may be deposited and later removed. A surface can place much younger rock directly on something ancient, with millions or hundreds of millions of years absent between them.

That absence has a name.

An unconformity.

At the Grand Canyon, more time is missing from the exposed record than is preserved. In places, a single contact stands across more than a billion years of unrecorded time. The gap is not a blank that tells us nothing. Geologists examine the layers above and below it, the angle of contact, the erosion surface, nearby formations, and comparable records elsewhere. Missing time still leaves conditions around the missingness.

Families have unconformities.

My mother's mother leaves at birth.

My father disappears for years.

A child is told something is none of his business.

A boarding-school record ends where family testimony becomes silence.

An abuse occurs before the child has enough language to build a continuous account.

A household paints over the fighting and presents the finished wall as the original surface.

The years are not empty because the record is incomplete.

The gap is part of the record.

That does not give me permission to invent whatever event would make the later layer easiest to explain. This matters. The urge to fill an unconformity is the same urge that turns a family story into mythology. We see an effect, select the cause that serves our current case, and call inference memory.

Responsible interpretation names the gap.

It looks at what survived around it.

It keeps uncertainty where uncertainty belongs.

Sediment becomes rock through lithification. Loose grains are compacted, spaces are filled with mineral cement, and what once moved separately becomes a solid formation.

Families lithify stories too.

A frightening event becomes a rule.

The rule is repeated until nobody remembers the event.

The repetition is cemented by shame, reward, silence, money, gender, religion, survival, or the simple exhaustion of arguing.

Eventually somebody says, “That is just how our family is,” as though bedrock arrived without process.

What feels permanent may be compacted repetition.

That does not make it easy to break.

It makes the process visible.

Metamorphic rock begins as another rock and changes under heat and pressure. Minerals recrystallize. Structures stretch, twist, fold, and align in the direction pressure was applied. The original material is not simply erased. It becomes another arrangement with the history of force inside its structure.

A person is not a rock.

Pressure is not automatically ennobling. Trauma does not turn people into diamonds, and suffering is not a kiln that guarantees wisdom. Sometimes pressure breaks. Sometimes it deforms. Sometimes it changes a system in ways that increase strength in one direction and fragility in another.

Still, the geological image helps me understand why a person can be genuinely transformed without becoming unrelated to what came before.

Healing is not a return to unpressured material.

There is no original version waiting untouched beneath the event.

There is another arrangement.

There may be new strength, new cleavage, new beauty, and new vulnerability. The direction of the pressure may remain visible. That is not failure. It is history inside form.

Faults offer another lesson. When rock moves, a layer can be displaced so that matching material appears separated across a break. A geologist can sometimes correlate the layers and estimate how far the world moved.

Relationships have displaced layers too.

Two people may remember the same year from opposite sides of a fault. One remembers the argument. The other remembers the months of pressure before it. One remembers leaving. The other remembers every earlier moment that made the departure feel inevitable. The records may align without becoming identical.

The goal is not to press them into one approved story.

It is to recognize the displacement.

Then there are fossils.

The fantasy of deep time can make it sound as though every body eventually becomes stone. That is not true. Only a small percentage of organisms become fossils. Rapid burial and durable structures improve the odds. Most life decomposes, is consumed, dissolves, disperses, or enters cycles that preserve no recognizable body.

We are not guaranteed a fossil.

One day there may be millions of tons of mud, stone, roots, construction, ocean sediment, or some material I cannot imagine above the places where we stood. What is now my body may become soil, water, atmosphere, mineral, another organism, or matter distributed beyond any shape that could still be called me.

The matter changes custody.

I do not continue as a rock merely because some of my atoms may enter one.

The world continues using material.

Fossils also include traces rather than bodies: a footprint, a burrow, a trackway, evidence that something lived and acted in an environment even when none of the organism itself remains.

That is closer to legacy.

When we die, most of us will not leave a body the future can read. We will leave altered conditions.

A child who no longer flinches at a question about money.

A child who does.

A bridge maintained.

A river poisoned.

A tool another person can inspect.

A debt moved onto people who never agreed to it.

A story preserved.

A language revived.

A door opened.

A door made to look open while the lock moved somewhere less visible.

The rock does not preserve intention.

It preserves effect.

People are more than geology because living people can testify, contest, forgive, refuse, and change what happens next. But death removes our final editorial privilege. We will not be present to explain that our intentions were better, that the harm was complicated, that the system made us do it, or that the monument should be interpreted generously.

Our explanations end.

Effects keep moving.

We are not the beginning of the record.

We are not the end.

We are a temporary arrangement of matter capable, briefly, of choosing what kind of trace enters the next layer.

What will your trace make easier?

What will it make harder?

What will somebody infer about the world you believed should exist?

## Sourcehood without a source

The geological analogy ends where answerability begins.

A rock can bear the direction of pressure. It cannot apologize for the avalanche. A formation can preserve a fault. It cannot choose whether to continue the displacement.

A person can.

This is where the philosophy underneath my work meets the family story.

None of us is *causa sui*. None of us is the cause of ourselves.
