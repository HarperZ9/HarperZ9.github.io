Civilizations rename their past the way families do. They keep some names and erase others, and they call an adaptation tradition, a conquest order, a wound destiny, and a profitable arrangement nature. Archives get built around what the powerful needed remembered, and gaps get built around what the powerful needed forgotten. Descendants inherit the story. Then they mistake the inheritance for evidence that the arrangement had to happen.

Humanity is not one household. The language of family should not sentimentalize conquest, slavery, genocide, colonialism, caste, war, or incarceration. Institutions can organize violence at a scale no private relationship can reproduce.

After emancipation, formerly enslaved people placed newspaper advertisements searching for children, parents, siblings, spouses, and other relatives. Sale and the domestic slave trade had separated them. Some searches continued decades after the original separation. The *Last Seen* project, supported by the National Archives, preserves thousands of those notices.

Those notices are evidence of what happens when a system claims authority over kinship itself, and I will not use them as metaphors for my family.

The system treated a person as property. It treated a relationship as something it could sell apart. The survivors kept printing the relationship back into the world.

Have you seen my mother?

Have you seen my child?

Do you know the name they use now?

The words are simple because the need under them is not.

I think of the baby bird asking, *Are you my mother?* I have to keep the scale honest here. A children's book is not the history of slavery, and the connection does not make the harms comparable. The connection shows that recognition, a name, return, and the right to belong are grave needs, and calling them childish misreads them. They are among the first structures power attacks when it wants people easier to control.

A system can break a family apart and then leave the descendants to explain the break as personality.

People still learn that system's habits in ordinary places: at the table, in school, in jokes, in who gets believed, and in whose pain becomes history while someone else's gets called attitude.

You did not choose the first language the world used to explain itself to you. Once you can hear it, you are still responsible for what it lets you do.

## The unconformity

As a child I wanted to become an atlas, because maps made absence visible.

Geology goes further. It is the archive left after whoever made the record can no longer answer back.

A rock cannot explain what it meant. A layer cannot tell us we misread it. A geologist reads the record from what the rock is made of, where it sits, how it fractured, what pressure it took, where it touches other rock, and what remains on either side of the missing part.

In an ordinary sequence of sedimentary layers, the older material lies at the bottom and the younger material builds up on top. Geologists call that superposition. The idea turns into a metaphor almost at once. Childhood sits under adolescence. One generation sits under the next. History stacks up until the newest surface forgets how much weight it rests on.

A rock record is an imperfect stack. It erodes, folds, and cracks along faults. Rock gets buried, lifted, heated, weathered, dissolved, and exposed again. Some layers never form. Some form and later wear away. Much younger rock can end up lying directly on something ancient, with millions or hundreds of millions of years missing between them.

That missing time has a name. Geologists call it an unconformity.

At the Grand Canyon, the exposed rock is missing more time than it preserves. In places, a single contact between two layers spans more than a billion years of unrecorded time. The gap still tells us something. Geologists study the layers above and below it, the angle where they meet, the eroded surface, nearby formations, and similar records elsewhere. Missing time leaves evidence around its edges.

Families have unconformities.

My mother's mother leaves at birth. My father disappears for years. A child is told something is none of his business. A boarding-school record ends where family testimony falls silent. An abuse happens before the child has the words to build a continuous account. A household paints over the fighting and presents the finished wall as the original surface.

The years in the gap still happened, and the gap belongs to the record.

That does not give me permission to invent whatever event would make the later layer easiest to explain. The urge to fill an unconformity is the same urge that turns a family story into mythology. We see an effect, pick the cause that helps our current argument, and call the inference memory.

Responsible interpretation names the gap and studies what survived around it. The uncertainty stays where it belongs.

Sediment becomes rock through a process called lithification. The loose grains are pressed together, mineral cement fills the spaces between them, and grains that once moved separately become one solid formation.

Families harden stories in the same way. A frightening event becomes a rule. People repeat the rule until nobody remembers the event. Shame, reward, silence, money, gender, religion, survival, or plain exhaustion with arguing sets the repetition in place.

Eventually somebody says, “That is just how our family is,” as though the rock had formed with no process at all.

Something that feels permanent may be repetition pressed hard over time. Seeing that shows how it formed, even though the knowledge does not make it easy to break.

Metamorphic rock starts as a different rock and changes under heat and pressure. Its minerals form new crystals. Its structures stretch, twist, fold, and line up in the direction the pressure pushed. The original material is not simply erased. It takes a new arrangement, with the history of the force inside its structure.

A person is not a rock.

Pressure does not automatically make anyone better. Trauma does not turn people into diamonds, and suffering is no kiln that guarantees wisdom. Pressure can sometimes break a person or bend them out of shape, and at other times it leaves someone stronger in one direction and more fragile in another.

The geological picture still helps me see how a person can be transformed and stay connected to what came before.

Healing returns nobody to material that never felt pressure, and no original version waits untouched beneath the event. The new arrangement may hold new strength, planes where it can split, beauty, and vulnerability. The direction of the pressure may stay visible. That is history held inside a shape, and it is no failure.

Faults teach something else. When rock moves along a fault, a layer can be shifted so that matching pieces end up on opposite sides of a break. A geologist can sometimes match the layers across the break and estimate how far the ground moved.

Relationships have shifted layers too.

Two people may remember the same year from opposite sides of a fault. One remembers the argument. The other remembers the months of pressure before it. One remembers leaving. The other remembers every earlier moment that made leaving feel inevitable. The two records may line up without being identical.

The goal is to recognize the displacement. Pressing the two records into one approved story is no part of that goal.

Then there are fossils.

The fantasy of deep time, the billions of years of the Earth's history, can make it sound as though every body eventually turns to stone. That is false. Only a small percentage of living things become fossils. Quick burial and hard body parts improve the odds. Most life decays, gets eaten, dissolves, scatters, or enters cycles that keep no recognizable body.

Nobody is guaranteed a fossil.

One day millions of tons of mud, stone, roots, buildings, ocean sediment, or some material I cannot imagine may lie over the places where we stood. What is now my body may become soil, water, air, mineral, another living thing, or matter scattered beyond any shape that could still be called me.

The matter moves on into other forms. Some of my atoms may end up in a rock, and I will not live on as that rock. The world keeps using the material.

Some fossils are traces with no body in them: a footprint, a burrow, a trackway. They show that something lived and acted in a place even when none of the creature itself remains.

Legacy works more like a trace.

When we die, most of us will leave no body the future can read. We will leave changed conditions:

- a child who no longer flinches at a question about money
- a child who does
- a bridge kept in repair
- a river poisoned
- a tool another person can inspect
- a debt pushed onto people who never agreed to it
- a story kept
- a language brought back
- a door opened
- a door made to look open while the lock moved somewhere harder to see

Rock keeps only the effect.

People are more than geology. Living people can testify, dispute, forgive, refuse, and change what happens next. Death takes away our last chance to edit. We will not be there to say our intentions were better, that the harm was complicated, that the system made us do it, or that the monument deserves a generous reading. Our explanations stop when we do, and the effects keep moving.

We are a temporary arrangement of matter somewhere in the middle of the record, and for a short time we can choose what kind of trace goes into the next layer.

What will your trace make easier?

What will it make harder?

What will somebody infer about the world you believed should exist?

## Sourcehood without a source

By sourcehood I mean being a real source of what you do, even though you did not make yourself.

The geological comparison stops at the point where a person has to answer for what they did.

A rock can carry the direction of pressure, and a formation can preserve a fault. Only a person can apologize for the avalanche or choose whether the shifting continues.

Here the philosophy under the software I build meets the family story.

None of us is *causa sui*, a Latin phrase for something that caused itself.
