# A witness should not become a ruler

AI-prepared editorial working draft for Zain Dana Harper, based on his published writing and project sources. Not yet personally reviewed by him. First-person prose below is a proposed draft in his voice. This is not a peer-reviewed paper or an announcement of journal acceptance.

A record can be intact and the decision made from it can still be wrong.

That is one of the boundaries I want Flywheel to preserve. An agent can produce an answer, a tool can record its actions, and a verifier can check a particular property of the result. Those are useful contributions. The difficult moment comes when we promote one of them into a different claim: the answer is true, the action was permitted, the criterion was fair, or the system is safe.

Those claims need their own grounds.

In *Pick The Lock for Everyone*, I wrote: “Nothing I build gets to inherit authority from the fact that I hate authority.” That applies to the tools as much as the person building them. An accountable system must make room for the builder to be mistaken. It must also let someone affected by a decision find its basis, challenge it, and preserve their work if they leave.

## What the byte witness can say

EMET is a deliberately limited integrity witness. Its specification separates byte-level checks from authorization and semantic judgment. A match can establish that the material being checked agrees with an anchor under the declared procedure. It cannot, from that fact alone, establish that the material is accurate, that the anchor was appropriately chosen, or that acting on it is justified.

Calling that witness incapable of authority is a design commitment about its role and output. It is not a claim that software can never check permission. Software can verify a signed, scoped authorization against an accepted policy. The policy, delegation, scope and relevant current state then become part of what must be checked. A signature by itself does not supply all of that context.

Nor does a restricted output vocabulary make misuse impossible. Another component could interpret an integrity result as a blanket approval. A user interface could paint it green and remove the qualification. A downstream agent could treat a stored success label as permission to repeat an action. The boundary has to survive those connections.

This is where a modest witness becomes a demanding engineering problem.

## Five questions that deserve separate answers

Consider an agent asked to prepare a document and send it to one recipient. We should be able to ask:

- Are these the same bytes that were reviewed?
- Does the document satisfy the task's stated criterion?
- Was this particular send permitted for this recipient and time?
- What was actually delivered, and what remains unknown?
- Who set the criterion and the permission, and how can a person contest them?

An intact draft does not show that it reached the intended person. A delivery receipt does not show that the content was correct. An earlier approval does not necessarily cover a changed attachment. A policy checker can report compliance with its policy while leaving the legitimacy of that policy open to challenge.

These are illustrative cases, not results from a newly completed experiment. Their purpose is to make the distinctions concrete enough to test.

## What this asks of Flywheel

Flywheel is being built as the central harness for a collection of tools that also have value on their own. The research question is whether their connections can preserve the distinctions above through an actual workflow: source gathering, proposals, checking, action, observation and later reuse.

Memory makes this especially consequential. If an agent remembers that a task succeeded but loses the conditions, the criterion or the unresolved parts, its next action can inherit confidence that the original evidence never supported. Preserving a failure label is useful. Preserving enough context for another person to determine what failed is more useful.

Evaluation has the same problem. A grader can accept an artifact that exploits its interface. A record of that acceptance may be perfectly authentic. We therefore need controls that ask how a verifier could approve the wrong result, alongside the examples it is expected to accept. A second agent can help find mistakes; shared models, materials and incentives still limit how independent that review is.

Bulletin offers a place to investigate intentionally shared agent activity: claims, sources, disagreements, handoffs and corrections. It is not, merely by being a board, a sandbox or a complete view of an agent's behavior. A useful experiment must specify which messages were delivered, which actions were observed, what the grader could see, and what remains unknown or outside that observation. Public inspection also needs privacy boundaries. Accountability does not require publishing everyone's private work.

The current program includes implemented controls and ongoing integration work. This essay does not establish that every intended connection is deployed, that the tools improve model alignment, or that any organization has adopted them. Those are separate questions for source inspection, workflow trials and external use.

## Opening the work to challenge

The publication I want to develop from this is narrow enough to be wrong: when an evaluation record omits a distinction that matters to a decision, which conclusions can still be drawn from it? What additional evidence would resolve the ambiguity, and where must a governing choice remain explicit?

There is substantial earlier work on authentication, authorization, capabilities and proof-carrying systems. A new vocabulary does not displace that work. Our manuscript has to explain what those methods already solve, what assumptions remain, and whether agent evaluation introduces a useful additional analysis. The elementary observation that missing information limits a decision is a starting point, not a claim of discovery.

The practical standard is a person who can make a better decision with less misplaced confidence. For an evaluator, that might mean identifying exactly why a passing result is insufficient. For a tool builder, it might mean refusing a stale permission without losing the user's work. For someone affected by an automated decision, it means a reachable account and a meaningful opportunity to challenge it.

That is the connection to *Pick The Lock for Everyone*: access to useful capability, access to the basis of a claim, and room to refuse the system's answer. The witness should help people exercise judgment. It should remain answerable to them.
