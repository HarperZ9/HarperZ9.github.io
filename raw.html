The native renderer

The Playground's renderer, live at the size of your window. The engine's own ambient occlusion, reflections and anti-aliasing run on its own scenes, beside a path-traced reference, and the page measures what each pass gets wrong while you watch.

The native renderer is the part of The Playground that renders frames in C++ on Direct3D 12, Vulkan and WebGPU, formerly released as raw-native. It also draws each scene on a CPU reference path, checks its fast screen-space ambient occlusion against a ray-traced reference, and writes a JSON certificate saying whether the shortcut held.

It needs a browser with WebGPU. Without it, the pictures below are frames from the same page, and the certificate check further down still runs on your CPU.

Render it here

Pick a scene. It opens full-window and draws every frame on your GPU: the engine's GTAO, SSR and TAA passes, sun and sky, light in the air, and a tilted lens. Hold the camera still and two references gather behind it, a ray-traced one for occlusion and mirrors and a path-traced one for the whole frame. Each pass in the panel says how far the frame is from the reference with that pass on and with it off.

The engine's test scene: a ground plane and a box, the scene its certificates are written for.

With scripts and WebGPU on, each picture opens the renderer full-window. A scene downloads only when you press.

Drag to look. W A S D or the arrows move, Q and E sink and rise, the wheel goes forward, Shift is faster. The panel at the right sets the scene, the camera, what is shown, each pass, the sun, the air, the lens, the display transform and the pixel count. Esc, or Back to the page, leaves.

The pictures are the renderer's own canvas, drawn on an RTX 4090 at 1600 x 900 from the code and scenes this page loads.

Every row is the same frame with one pass turned on or off, measured against the same path-traced reference. Four passes bring the frame closer. The sky light makes it slightly worse, because its ambient cube has no idea the hall has a roof; the reference does.

GTAO against ray-traced occlusion within the same 1.2 m: RMSE 0.064, inside the engine's 0.12 tolerance; with no occlusion at all, 0.150. SSR against the traced mirror over 343,234 pixels: 0.107; the sky probe alone: 0.210.

On the same card the real-time passes took 4.0 ms of GPU time a frame at 1280 x 800, 13.1 ms at 2560 x 1440 and 37.1 ms at 3840 x 2160, where the engine's GTAO took 14.9 ms and its SSR 9.8 ms. The references run only while the camera holds still: at 4K one sample of each costs another 70 ms.

What runs, and whose it is

The engine's

  • GTAO, mirror SSR and the TAA resolve: the engine's WGSL (post.wgsl), the GPU forms of the passes its CPU reference checks.
  • Every ray: the engine's watertight triangle test and its BVH walk (passes.wgsl), on the engine's node layout.
  • The display transform: the engine's colour pipeline (colour.wgsl) with its ACES 2.0 tables, or AgX, PBR Neutral or clip.
  • The sky: the engine's Preetham clear sky and sun, ported line for line from sky.cpp.
  • The scenes: raw-hall and raw-hero, the engine's owned assets, byte for byte as its exporter writes them.

Written for this page

  • The G-buffer: the browser's rasterizer draws it. The engine's own raster pass walks every triangle per pixel, which is too slow to move through 586,424 triangles at window size.
  • The sun and sky shading, the light in the air, the tilt-shift blur, the tree build and both references are written for this page.
  • Transmission is not refracted on either side: the hero's visor is drawn as dark glass.

Each engine file is carried unchanged from superframe at commit cac34166 and checked against its SHA-256 before it compiles; each scene is checked against the hash the engine commits for it. A file that does not match stops the page.

In the engine, not yet in your browser

The engine draws more than this page can carry yet. These are its own frames, with the record each came from.

960 x 540 frames from the CPU stage of the engine's tilt-shift lens, evidence/tiltshift at superframe commit c1f85d50. The GPU chain costs about 0.86 ms at 1080p on an RTX 4090 (Direct3D 12 median; Vulkan about 0.85 ms). Two of its checks are recorded misses: out of focus the ink does not fall off as far as the bound asks (1.32 against at most 0.5), and the art scene's measure is 0.474 against at least 0.5.

Also in the engine and not on this page: ReSTIR direct lighting over many lights (unbiased against the exact tracer on two scenes, its records in evidence/restir-di), the material model with clearcoat, sheen, anisotropy, iridescence and transmission (checked against a float64 reference), and the Direct3D 12 and Vulkan paths.

Run it now.

  
See how it works, step by step: both occlusion estimators, the reconcile against the tolerance and the verifier, animated with values from the repository.

  
Download the binary for your system from the 0.8.0 release, check it against the published hashes, and point it at an empty folder. A 256 by 256 frame takes about a quarter of a second. The release is still named raw-native. It ships the CPU, Direct3D 12 and WebGPU builds; the Vulkan path is in The Playground’s source and was added after 0.8.0.

The certificate

  
A certificate is a JSON file with a claim, a verdict, the oracle that judged it and the numbers behind the verdict. The verdict is verified, refuted or unverifiable. A channel with no data is written as null.

  
You can check one in two ways. From 0.3.0 the run writes its ambient occlusion as 32-bit floats beside a coverage mask, and raw_native_cli verify recomputes the error from those files alone. Or run the same command yourself, because the output is deterministic: the default frame and its certificate hash the same with MSVC 19.50 on Windows, with GCC 13.3 on Ubuntu and from the WebAssembly build, locally and on GitHub’s build runners. The renderer also measures exactly how many bytes the frame needs, renders again inside that budget, and refuses any allocation past it. A second certificate records whether the budget held.

Scope and speed

  
    
    
  • There is one built-in test scene and no model loader yet.
    • 
    
  • The ray-traced reference uses 64 samples per pixel, so it shows visible grain.
    • 
    
  • The screen-space method is a simple one, which is why most views come back refuted.
    • 
    
  • The default tolerance is 0.12 RMSE. --tolerance changes it, and the certificate records the value used.
    • 
    
  • A full render with the ray-traced reference takes about 0.26 seconds at 256 by 256 and 1 second at 512 by 512 on one thread of an Intel Core i7-13700KF, natively or in WebAssembly. Times on this page depend on your machine.
    • 
    
  • On an NVIDIA RTX 4090 in Chrome, the GPU draws a 512 by 512 frame with every channel in about 14 milliseconds, and all 16 GPU certificates in the release evidence say verified. The CPU reference that checks each GPU frame still takes its full time, so a GPU render on this page is not faster end to end.
    • 
    
  • Without WebGPU this page runs the single-threaded CPU build. The threaded WebAssembly build ran about eight times faster on the same machine, but browsers allow it only on pages sent with cross-origin isolation headers, and GitHub Pages cannot send them.
    • 
    
  • Matching hashes were checked on x86-64 with two compilers and the WebAssembly build. Other processors and compilers are untested.
    • 
  






  
Where it came from.

  
The name comes from RAW, a Direct3D 11 effects platform I wrote for Skyrim SE: a proxy library that took over a closed game’s frame and added its own lighting passes mid-picture. That taught me how a frame is built and how quickly an effect can look right while being wrong. The native renderer keeps the lesson and drops the game. It owns its whole frame, and every frame carries a check against a reference. The Skyrim code stays public. Work on it is paused, and I plan to return to it.






  
Read the source.

  
C++23, built from the standard library and its own headers. The rasterizer, both ambient occlusion methods, the reconcile step, the certificate writer, the verifier and the bounded memory arena each have their own tests, 32 in all. From 0.3.0 the license is FSL-1.1-MIT, and each version becomes MIT two years after its release. Version 0.2.0 stays MIT, as released.
