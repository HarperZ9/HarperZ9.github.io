In short

This long personal essay is addressed to the Mad-Happy Scientist, the curious child the author once hoped to become. It covers his childhood and family history, which include sexual abuse, drinking and drug use, prison and a federal boarding-school system, along with the harm he later did to other people, and it argues that an explanation for harm does not excuse it. The later sections turn to the AI tools he builds, which keep failures on the record and check each proposed answer against a standard the model did not write, and they say where that comparison to people stops. The title asks for a way through that everyone can use. Read the content note first, then take the essay one section at a time.

The sentence that would not come out

One of the oldest dreams I remember begins at a park.

My family is gone. I do not know where they went or how to find them. Another family is in the park, and I run toward them because they look like people who might help. I try to explain that I am lost.

I open my mouth.

Nothing comes out.

I have spent most of my life trying to finish that sentence.

That is true. It is also not innocent. A person who spends a life fighting to be heard can start to treat every room as the hearing he was denied. He can mistake having a voice for having the floor and make his pain the price of everybody else's attention, and he can work so hard to prove that he exists that the people around him begin to disappear.

I know because I have done it.

Before I could explain any of this, books gave me side doors.

Shel Silverstein did not write toward me as though childhood were a waiting room for adulthood. His poems were mischievous, dark and impossible, and they let a child suspect that the official map was incomplete. In Where the Sidewalk Ends, he writes:

“For the children, they mark, and the children, they know.”

I knew that sentence before I understood what I knew. Children notice the boundary because adults have not yet finished teaching them that the pavement is reality and that whatever grows past it is irresponsible. The end of the sidewalk can be danger. It can also be the place where an inherited route stops pretending to be the whole earth.

Silverstein trusted the child with edges.

The Giving Tree gave me another kind of edge. I never read it as a clean lesson about generosity. I saw affection, hunger, use, delight, depletion and a stump in the same relationship. I could feel love in the giving. I could also feel something unsettling in the way the giving went on until almost nothing recognizable was left.

Was the tree fulfilled, or consumed? And the boy: loved, entitled, desperate, ordinary, or all of those things at different ages?

The book left the moral account open, the way a children's book sometimes survives by refusing to close it, and that is part of why it stayed with me.

I had already seen adults call depletion love. Later I became capable of taking another person's time, patience, attention or belief until they felt reduced, and then of treating their exhaustion as evidence that they did not love me enough. I learned the habit elsewhere. Silverstein gave me an image I would argue with for years.

Jack Prelutsky gave language knees and elbows. He had studied music and still sang, and you can hear the training in his words. In his poems, rhythm was the way meaning moved. An impossible creature could be funny enough to get into a child's head, and a sentence was free to jump around like the creature it described, with no report to file.

Dr. Seuss made constraint look like motion. The Beginner Books were built to help children read on their own through simple words, visual clues, rhyme and repetition, and through an almost unreasonable confidence that a limit could turn into play. The vocabulary did not have to be enormous for the room to feel enormous.

That may be one reason I later loved code, where a rule can be an instrument as well as a cage.

Beverly Cleary gave ordinary children the dignity of an inner weather. In her books a child did not need a catastrophe before embarrassment, boredom, resentment, curiosity or a sense of injustice was allowed to count. A misunderstanding with an adult could matter. A parent's job, a bill, a sister, a teacher or a bad afternoon could change the whole climate without first becoming a grand symbol. Her children were people living through the day, never props in an adult's lesson.

P. D. Eastman's Are You My Mother? gave me a baby bird who starts searching before he even knows what the person he needs is supposed to look like. He asks the wrong animals and the wrong machines the right question.

How much of adult life is that question in another costume?

Are you my mother?

Will you recognize me, tell me I belong, become the person I lost before I knew what losing meant?

We ask it of partners, employers, institutions, audiences, ideologies, communities and machines. We ask a job to parent the part of us that never felt useful, and a lover to prove that no one will leave again. Then we ask recognition to turn into love, and we get angry when applause cannot hold us.

A child denied recognition can spend decades confusing recognition with rescue. I read like someone trying to find another climate, and never like a student preparing to answer questions.

For a while I wanted to be an entomologist, then a marine biologist. I wanted the small worlds under leaves and the enormous worlds under water. At another point my ambition was to become a geographical atlas and an encyclopedia. Neither one is a profession, and it was still the ambition. Consulting the map was never enough for me. I wanted to grow large enough to hold where everything went.

I read and read and read, because knowledge seemed to offer what the adults around me could not give me consistently: a world where a question could lead somewhere other than shame.

What book gave you another climate?

What story, game, field, song, instrument, machine or map let some part of you exist without first being cross-examined?

Maybe you no longer remember the title.

Your body may still remember the door.

Music became another language before I was ready for language.

Billy Strayhorn's A Flower Is a Lovesome Thing gave delicacy enough room to be serious. Strayhorn composed, arranged and wrote for Duke Ellington's orchestra for three decades. Their musical languages grew so closely together that scholars have sometimes had trouble deciding where one ended and the other began. He lived openly as a gay Black man in an era that made that openness dangerous. He contributed enormously from a position often treated as background, and while he was alive he did not receive public credit equal to the work.

I do not use that history to reduce the music to biography. The piece reached me before the biography did, and what reached me was permission to be delicate without becoming trivial, the way a flower can be serious without armor.

Being underrecognized does not make work more beautiful, though it does make the question of who gets heard harder to ignore. A person can help create the language of an era and still stand partly in somebody else's shadow. The work can survive that imbalance, and the imbalance stays unjust.

Funkadelic's Maggot Brain gave longing somewhere to live without explanation. George Clinton opens the track with eight words:

“Mother Earth is pregnant for the third time.”

Then Eddie Hazel's guitar comes in. In the account Clinton gave later, he asked Hazel to imagine the grief of his mother's death and to let what was inside him move through the instrument. Hazel recorded the long solo in one take.

The guitar metabolizes grief without explaining it. It circles, tears, pleads, pulls back and returns, and it keeps finding more feeling inside the same few materials. I heard a nervous system that refused to stay trapped in prose. The virtuosity never registered with me as a credential.

Later, after wading through the dubstep era, I found drum 'n' bass. It sounded like the future had learned to run. It also sounded like the speed at which my thoughts, ideas and synapses moved from point to point.

I learned later that the future I heard had ancestors. Jungle and drum 'n' bass belong to a living Black British history shaped by Caribbean sound-system culture, breakbeats, reggae, dub, soul, hip-hop, rave, technology, migration and community. A sound system is a crew with its own mobile speaker rig, playing records at street parties and dances. A breakbeat is a few bars of drumming lifted from an older record and looped into a new track. The music carried old recordings into new pressure. It cut, repeated, sped up and rearranged inherited material.

The breakbeat is a memory reorganized into motion.

That idea mattered to the philosophy I built much later. Ancestry is never pure, and nothing new arrives free of influence. Something new can grow out of inherited material without lying about the material that made it possible.

What music finished a sentence for you?

What sound gave your body somewhere to put what your house, school, church, job or family could not receive?

I have moved through genres the way I moved through books, through highs, lows and intense mids, looking for a form that could hold what ordinary conversation could not.

None of those works rescued me, and art does not have to. They gave me rooms where an experience could exist before it had won an argument.

Art can keep a human possibility alive until someone finds language for it. An apology for me, a check on a scientific claim, restored trust and repair for a person I hurt all need a source outside art.

Somewhere in those rooms was the child I used to imagine becoming. I called him the Mad-Happy Scientist.

He was not thinking about prestige. He wanted research, graphics, code, biology, mathematics, physics, strange instruments and the feeling of asking a question nobody had answered yet. He wanted to take the broken thing apart to see whether the failure had a shape, to be wrong in an interesting direction, and to laugh when something finally moved.

The first sentence I need to finish for him is unflattering.

I have never apologized.

Not in a way that counted.

The first apology

I have said sorry plenty of times, and I have explained myself until the explanation was the only thing left in the room. I have collapsed under the weight of what I did and called myself a piece of shit with enough conviction that somebody else eventually had to stop being angry and start taking care of me, and I have mistaken that reversal for honesty.

I have disappeared into work, substances and code, into a new project, a new emergency or a new argument about why the world is arranged badly. Some of those arguments were true, and that was part of what made them useful, because a true explanation can still be used like a fake passport.

When I was sixteen, I was suicidal, depressed and dissociating, which means feeling cut off from my own body and surroundings. I acted out and swallowed almost everything I did not know how to say. I almost died from drugs, fights, self-criticism, serious illness and the kind of reckless momentum that can feel strangely calm from inside. I hurt people physically, mentally and spiritually. I cheated on tests and made video game cheats, and I lied my way around consequences. I learned to find the loose board in the fence, and I was proud of how quickly I could get through it.

I always had another reason to be the exception. I kept a private court where my pain came in as evidence and everybody else's pain came in as an accusation I had the right to cross-examine.

I know how severe that sounds, and I know how easily severity can turn into another performance. So I will slow down and say what I mean.

The drinking and the drugs are not the center of this story. Neither is my habit of avoiding things. Both are visible signs of a deeper pattern. I badly wanted to confront something in myself. I had not learned to do it without turning the confrontation into punishment or finding a way to escape it. I demanded that I change at once, and when I could not change on command I took that as proof that I was beyond repair.

Substances gave me a crude imitation of movement. For a few hours the pressure in the room shifted, and I could believe I had shifted with it. In the morning the original structure was still there, usually with more debris around it.

None of this makes the substances innocent, or makes me their victim in a way that removes my agency. They belonged to a longing I did not know how to approach directly. Through them I kept asking for another life while I denied myself the patience it takes to build one.

The other part of that pattern was something I called independence.

I distrusted authority from a young age. Some of that distrust was taught to me, and some of it was earned. I did not believe that institutions had my interests at heart or that the official route was necessarily the honest one. I wanted to build around the gate.

That instinct has something good in it, along with something I did not want to see.

When the people who love you pay for the path you carve, the word independence stops fitting. I refused to depend on people honestly, and then I depended on them to survive the consequences of that refusal. I wanted to be self-made, and all the while I quietly used their time, money, patience, sleep and concern as the floor under the performance.

I do not mean that relationships are transactions or that love is an invoice. Still, when another person makes a decision with you in mind, they give something up. Maybe it is time. Maybe it is an opportunity, a night's sleep, a quieter month, or a little more faith than they can comfortably afford. You can honor that sacrifice without keeping score, and pretending it does not count can become exploitation.

My thoughts move quickly when I am in motion. Pride, shame, victimhood, resentment, tenderness, hunger and a fairly predatory view of humanity can all be fighting over the steering wheel before I have decided what I believe. I take the fastest neural route available and say the thing with the most voltage. Then I spend days on relationship maintenance and emotional janitorial work.

I explain what I meant and fix the part that landed wrong. I reassure people that the worst version of the sentence was not the whole of me.

Meanwhile, the original issue is still sitting there.

Self-condemnation is another fast route. I can say, "I am a piece of shit, and I probably will be tomorrow," and there is truth in that, because one essay does not change my habits by morning. The sentence can still become a shortcut. It flattens every specific harm into one total identity and closes the case. Nothing is left to repair, and the shame feels deep while it does very little work.

I am not asking for release from judgment, and I do not want the reader's sympathy to stand in for consequences just because I finally found language for the pattern. I want to sit here first. Then I want to stand up, walk where I can be seen, and accept that some people may make a mockery of me.

I have spent years trying to control that possibility. Whenever a room has begun to arrive at a verdict I could not edit, I have lied, redirected, disappeared or charmed, or I have turned angry, fragile or too busy. All of that is fear with an architectural plan, and none of it is accountability.

The day the Earth Fair ended

There is another childhood dream I have carried for years.

In the dream, my family and I were getting ready to visit the Earth Fair, an event my parents had taken me to when I was very young. I wanted desperately to go back. When we reached the campground, the fair was gone.

There was only a wall and a door.

I walked to the door and opened it.

Beyond it, roughly twelve flights of stairs went down into an industrial factory. The machines below had been built to look like monsters. As soon as they saw me, they chased me back up the stairs and out through the door.

The Earth Fair was closed.

I do not read dreams as evidence, prophecy or diagnosis. I hold this one as an image my mind refused to discard. A place I remembered as open, living, communal and full of possibility had become a wall. The invitation had become a door into a factory. The factory had made machines in the shape of monsters, and being seen by them was enough to set off the chase.

Maybe that asks too much of a dream. It is still hard not to recognize the architecture.

Again and again we are invited toward a fair and delivered to a factory. We hear that a system exists for everyone, then find that the door is controlled by people who benefit from keeping the stairs hidden. Education arrives as debt, connection as surveillance, and creative abundance as a marketplace where the platform owns the route to the audience. The promise of AI for everyone, sometimes called democratized intelligence, arrives as a metered pipe that sells access by the unit.

The machines chase the child whether or not they hate him, because they were built so that his presence counts as an error.

That is one reason pick the lock for everyone, the title of this essay, cannot mean a private route for me. It means a way through that other people can use too. If I learn the door, enter the factory and close the door behind me, I have joined the architecture's staff.

A childhood built as somebody else's stage

My childhood often felt like a stage on which other people needed to perform their emergencies.

My mother had emotions that deserved somewhere to exist. As I experienced our home, she often needed the whole room to become that place. My own feelings had nowhere to go, so I learned to be audience, technician, prop, interruption or witness. I watched the emotional weather closely, because the weather decided what the room was allowed to hold.

This is the architecture I remember. It is only part of the truth about my mother.

She did not begin with much room either.

My mother never met her own mother, who left when she was born. My maternal grandfather remarried. His new wife seemed to carry some kind of disdain toward the bond between father and daughter, and perhaps toward women more generally. I cannot know exactly what lived inside that relationship. I know my mother grew up without the recognition a child should be able to expect from the people responsible for loving her.

Later I saw some of that wound come back as jealousy and competition. Another woman could become a threat. The fear could turn toward the woman, toward my father, toward my stepfather, or toward the relationship itself. Usually the concern was minimized and left unaddressed, and the minimizing added fuel to the fear.

When hurt people form relationships with other people in pain, no explanation makes the outcome predictable.

A fracture does not stay politely in the generation where it began. A child who was not chosen may grow into an adult who reads ordinary distance as abandonment. A daughter can become proof that her mother is loved, and then look like a rival once she becomes her own person. Sometimes a partner is asked to answer for a parent he never met. And a room can hold a missing person for decades after everybody stops saying the name.

This is one way an absence recruits later relationships, and none of it is destiny.

A person denied a stage can spend the rest of his life seizing one, and I can see how easily I rebuild the same architecture. I can turn pain into an event everybody else must attend, or talk about being unheard until nobody around me can speak. My demand for recognition can grow until recognizing me looks the same as surrendering to me. Another person's boundary can feel like abandonment to me, and then I can punish them for reminding me that they are separate.

My father inherited another version of the same absence.

His father, my paternal grandfather, remarried a woman who already had six children. He became a stepfather to them and put distance between himself and his own son. My father watched his father give himself to another household and leave less and less of himself for the child who was already his.

The family account I inherited is that the woman was having sex with other men behind my grandfather's back.

While my grandfather was betraying my father, she was betraying my grandfather.

The two betrayals were different. Together they describe the house my father was learning from. The person who should have chosen him kept turning elsewhere, and the person his father had chosen was turning elsewhere too. My father bonded with his stepbrothers, and they grew close. That world became part of the road that eventually took him to prison in Southern California in the early 1980s. Much of his adolescence and early adulthood happened there and became whatever version of adulthood prison had available.

The prison belonged to a history larger than my father. For much of the history of California men's prisons, people arriving at reception, the intake center where new prisoners are sorted, were officially segregated by racial category. Prisoners also enforced racial rules of their own, commonly described as prison “politics.” Race could become housing, affiliation, threat, protection and debt. It could become a rule a young person was expected to understand before he had any real power to rewrite it.

For a short time, he joined a skinhead prison gang. That carries a stigma, and it should.

I was not there. I cannot reconstruct every pressure he faced, and I will not turn a later scholarly account of California prisons, such as Tonya Lindsey's 2009 study, into a personal alibi. I can say that this mask appeared inside an institution where racial identity was more than an opinion somebody carried privately. Racial identity there was built into the environment and enforced through danger.

The environment helps explain why a mask could be offered as armor, without making the mask neutral.

A skinhead gang is not a harmless costume. People who saw that gang had reason to fear what it represented, and nobody is required to search underneath it for a gentler explanation. I believe my father was young, lost and incarcerated, and that he was trying to protect himself in a place he understood as dangerous. I do not believe racism was the conviction underneath his mask. He wore something that did not fit because he believed it might keep him alive.

The mask still entered the world, and a short chapter can leave a long stigma. A false mask can frighten somebody, and a survival strategy can carry somebody else's terror.

Some people may never forgive him, and they do not owe him forgiveness. The world can stop at the worst thing it learned about a person and stay there.

I have also seen how far he has come. He was never only the mask.

Leaving that mask did not erase the abandonment he carried, or the abandonment he later repeated.

My father had his own pattern of leaving places where he felt unwanted or unwelcome. Because of it, there was a gap of years when I did not see him. I remember learning that he had driven past one of my little league games on the way to use cocaine in the mountains and wander.

That detail stayed because it is almost absurdly specific: the game on one side of the road, disappearance on the other.

I remember being left in strange places with adults whose behavior I did not understand. I absorbed the lesson that a situation could be called normal because the child inside it lacked the vocabulary, the authority or the transportation to contest it.

My stepfather minimized problems. As I experienced it, any shortcoming that touched his pride had to become smaller than it was. When the problem could not be reduced, the person naming it could. It was easier to call me dramatic, confused or ungrateful, or to say I was asking about something that was none of my business, than to let the issue become something we could address.

Money worked the same way. When my mother and stepfather were frightened about money, a question about it got no accounting. Shame turned into anger. Financial illiteracy and spending on alcohol were protected behind a version of that is none of your business. A child learns two things from that answer. Resources are dangerous to discuss, and the person who asks for an account can be treated as the problem.

These habits did not begin with us.

The family history I carry includes this: my stepfather's father endured the Native boarding-school system in Klamath County, Oregon. I do not know every link between that institutional violence and the habits that followed. I know the history matters. Systems designed to separate Native children from family, language, culture and community do not leave tidy descendants.

These schools were federal policy at work. Between 1819 and 1969, the Department of the Interior identified 417 federal Indian boarding-school institutions across 37 states or then-territories. Their purpose was forced assimilation. Children were separated from kinship, language, ceremony, land and community authority. The final report of the Interior Department's Federal Indian Boarding School Initiative confirmed at least 973 child deaths and identified at least 74 marked or unmarked burial sites at 65 school locations. Those numbers are only the part of the history that the federal record could count.

In Klamath County, the Klamath Agency Boarding School opened in 1874 on the Klamath Indian Reservation. It stayed in operation until 1927, when students set it on fire.

I do not know whether my stepfather's father attended that exact school, and I will not fill a gap in family testimony with a convenient historical detail. I include the school because it belonged to the policy that surrounded the place and the lineage he came through.

I also do not know what moved every student involved in the fire.

I know the institution did not end as evidence that forced assimilation had succeeded.

When a system attacks kinship and language, descendants can inherit more than a story about something terrible that happened long ago. They may inherit missing vocabulary, interrupted caregiving, shame about where they come from, damaged community authority, distrust of institutions, and survival rules formed under domination. The fracture can enter a family as silence and later be mistaken for personality.

That history is not a voucher that cancels later harm.

People often resist that part of inheritance. We want history to condemn or to absolve, with a clean villain, victim and line of cause and a final moral balance, and real families are harder than that.

A person can be gravely harmed and still harm others, name the system that shaped him correctly and still use it as an alibi, and beg for recognition for years and then refuse it to the people closest to him.

The harms differ in weight. A child carries less responsibility than an adult. A person forced through an assimilation system and a descendant who minimizes a family member's concern are different cases. Context is not equivalence.

Transmission is still real.

I am the result of people who were alienated, shamed, neglected, displaced, intoxicated, frightened, abandoned by mothers, passed over by fathers, imprisoned, and taught distorted versions of virtue, honor, respect, morality, love and survival.

I am also a participant in what happens next.

Victimhood can be a fact. Sometimes it is the most important fact in the room. Playing the victim begins when I use the fact that I was harmed as jurisdiction over the people I harm. I have told myself that being silenced made my volume justice, and that being abandoned made my leaving self-protection. Adults once controlled the record, so I decided my version deserved to control it now. And because I could explain the system, I excused myself from answering for my conduct inside it.

That is how the victim can end up wearing the judge's robe.

I have spent my life fighting for my voice to be heard. I did need a voice, and I still do. The correction is learning that my voice enters a room among other voices and never above them.

I did not survive being somebody else's audience so that everyone I love could become mine.

Three hours of solace

Some of my personal history should remain private.

Keeping it private still leaves room to admit that the architecture was there.

I entered adolescence without much self-respect or a stable identity. I had no coherent understanding of love, sex, friendship, transparency or mental illness, because I had been given contradictory instructions about all of them. Care and control were mixed together. Secrecy was called protection, and adults lied.

My first encounter with anything sexual was abuse. I was five.

I remember forgiving the person who did it before I was old enough to understand forgiveness or grief.

That was not closure.

I do not know exactly what forgiveness meant to me at five. I know I spent years living inside consequences I could not name. I carried that confusion through adolescence. It changed my self-esteem and the way I understood closeness, and it deformed the first map I was given of sex, love, relationships, friendship and transparency.

I have had relationship problems with women, with my family and with myself for most of my life. I do not need to publish every detail to tell the truth about the route.

The abuse was not my fault.

The patterns I later carried into other people's lives are still part of my responsibility.

Both sentences are true.

The house reinforced the distortion.

All I knew was being lied to and deceived while grown-ups painted pretty pictures over a world that was poisonous.

No amount of paint covered the arguments and the fighting.

On school nights I turned my stereo on and left it playing until four in the morning, trying to drown out the yelling, screaming and crying. After the house finally went quiet, I would wake around four, go lie on the couch, and listen to SportsCenter until I fell asleep again.

From four until seven felt like three hours of solace.

Then I got up and went to the school bus.

In seventh grade, I fell asleep in first-period history.

History was my favorite subject.

The house came to school with me. The teacher saw a child sleeping through history. My body was still trying to finish the night.

I learned that adults could make the world poisonous and still demand that the child admire the paint, and that sex and love could be tied to secrecy and coercion before I knew what either was meant to be. I also learned that mental illness could be turned into spectacle, accusation or excuse when it needed to be understood, and that transparency was demanded from children and avoided by adults.

Those lessons went deeper than belief.

They became posture, identity, desire, fear and the routes my nervous system reached for later.

What travels

When people say trauma travels down a family line, they can mean several different things, and the distinctions matter. A child can inherit a story, a silence, a surname, a debt, a neighborhood, a ritual or a missing language. A child can also inherit a parent's startle response, a rule about money, a theory of men, a theory of women, and an expectation that love will leave. Some inheritances are habits, like drinking before speaking or speaking before feeling. And some are a nervous system organized around a danger that ended before the child was born. That danger still governs the room where the child learns what safety is.

Public-health agencies, including the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), group many conditions like these under the name adverse childhood experiences: abuse, neglect, violence in the home, household substance use, mental-health crises, separation and incarceration. The category is useful because it names how threats to safety, stability and bonding can pile up. It turns dangerous when the count of these experiences, often reported as a single score, is treated as destiny, when unlike harms are flattened into a tally, or when a number becomes a new way to explain a person without listening to them.

The CDC also describes relationships that are safe and stable, with adults who respond to a child's needs sensitively and consistently. Those relationships are part of the developmental environment itself. Treating them as sentimental extras, added after development is complete, misreads how development works.

Biology still matters here, and none of this requires a mystical theory of blood.

Experience can alter stress regulation, attention, sleep, memory, threat learning, and the body's expectations about what is likely to happen next. Early adversity is associated with changes in development, and those changes vary; there is no single uniform scar stamped into every brain. The effects differ with age, the kind of adversity, the developmental period, protective factors and the person living through them. Trauma exposure is not identical to post-traumatic stress disorder, or PTSD. Two people can survive the same event and carry different consequences. The same person can carry vigilance in one part of life and unusual strength, perception, humor or care in another.

An adaptation can be brilliant and still become destructive when the environment changes.

Hypervigilance, a constant watch for danger, can keep a child safe in an unpredictable house. In a calmer home years later, the same system can read a delayed text as abandonment, a question as prosecution, or a boundary as an approaching attack. Dissociation can make an unbearable moment survivable, and later remove a person from the conversation in which repair is finally possible. Anger can create enough force to escape powerlessness, and later make every disagreement feel like a cage that must be broken before anyone else finishes a sentence.

The nervous system is not a moral oracle. It can have a reason for a response and still produce the wrong response for this moment.

What I could not say still found outlets. It moved into dreams, substances, fights, music, code, grand projects, sexual posture, anger, humor and the body itself. An outlet can turn pain into art, motion, inquiry or a request for contact. It can also pour pain into another person. Expression does not heal by default, and sometimes it is only transmission with better lighting.

When trauma leaves a mark on what we make, the question is what happens to the mark before we hand it forward.

This is one reason I am careful with the language of epigenetics. In broad terms, epigenetics covers the regulation that affects how genes are expressed, such as which genes switch on and how strongly, without changing the underlying DNA sequence. Environmental conditions can influence that regulation. Animal studies provide strong evidence that some effects of stress can reach later generations through biological pathways. Human studies have found suggestive associations in families exposed to severe adversity.

Human beings are not controlled laboratory lineages.

We inherit genes, prenatal conditions, parenting, culture, economic structures, discrimination, language, food, housing, neighborhoods, stories, institutions and one another. Those pathways overlap. In human families it is extremely difficult to prove that an observed effect crossed generations through the germline, the egg and sperm cells, because shared environment, pregnancy, behavior and genetic variation can each set up the same effect again in the next generation.

So I cannot honestly say that my ancestors' memories are written into my DNA. I can say that their conditions helped build the conditions that built me, which is less dramatic and is enough.

The science does not need to turn family history into molecular destiny before the history counts. The child who learns to scan a parent's face for weather has already received an inheritance. The parent who cannot tolerate questions about money may pass on fear without passing on a single methyl group, the kind of chemical tag epigenetic studies often measure. The family that loses language, land, kinship or cultural authority under an assimilation system may spend generations rebuilding structures that biology alone cannot describe.

The important word is not only inheritance.

It is plasticity.

Plasticity means that living systems keep adapting. New experience can strengthen new pathways. Old fear can become less dominant. A body can learn that a context once tied to danger is a different context now. The word also has limits. Plasticity does not mean that positive thinking can shape the brain like clay, that every injury reverses, that healing follows a clean timeline, or that a person who still has symptoms has failed to want recovery correctly.

Fear extinction is the fading of a learned fear when its trigger keeps showing up without the danger. It is often described badly, as though the brain deletes an old file. The older learning can remain. New learning holds it back, competes with it and gives the person another route. Under stress, or in a changed context, the older response can return.

When that happens, it proves the record remained. It does not prove the healing was fake.

I understand forgiveness in a similar way. Forgiveness can become another route through the same terrain without erasing the memory, denying the harm, restoring access or calling the old response irrational. The old alarm may still exist. It no longer has to be the only system with authority.

Neural plasticity gives me a language for hope without a guarantee. Epigenetic plasticity gives me a language for conditions mattering without an inherited verdict. PTSD names what threat can do to perception, memory, the body and relationships, and it stops short of describing a whole identity. A diagnosis can name an adaptation without becoming a prophecy, and a family history can explain a route without walking the route for me.

The inheritance is yours too

You are in this essay even if none of the names, diagnoses, substances, prisons, hospitals, songs or dreams belong to you.

I am not asking you to borrow my wounds, and I am not asking us to pretend that every childhood contains the same violence, that every family secret is abuse, or that every discomfort deserves the language of trauma. A child assaulted at five, a child displaced by state policy, a child frightened by domestic violence, and a child whose parent expected perfect grades are not interchangeable cases.

Shared humanity requires us to recognize the architecture without stealing somebody else's scale, and without pretending that unequal harms were equal.

Every family has a record, and the records differ in how much violence they hold.

Maybe your family never had a prison, cocaine in the mountains, a boarding school, a stereo playing until four in the morning or a child trying to turn SportsCenter into three hours of peace.

Maybe the inheritance was quieter.

A father who could provide but could not apologize. A mother who made achievement the admission price for warmth. A grandparent who survived war and treated softness as an invitation to die. A household where religion gave people language for grace and also a reason not to name what happened. A family that crossed an ocean and could not afford for the children to fail.

A wealthy home where appearances were protected so thoroughly that pain became an embarrassment. A working-class home where exhaustion was called strength because nobody had time to call it grief. A joke everyone repeated whenever the truth came close. A person everybody agreed not to mention.

A role assigned so early that you mistook it for a self: the responsible one, the difficult one, the achiever, the peacemaker, the clown, the patient, the disappointment, the person who leaves, or the person who never gets to.

What adaptation are you still calling your personality?

What did your family name virtue because naming it fear would have broken the story?

Who was allowed to be angry, and who was required to translate everybody else's anger into something survivable? Who could ask about money, have needs without being called selfish, or fail and remain loved? And who disappeared, while someone else was required not to notice?

Diagnosing the dead from a distance, or turning every parent into an antagonist, would misuse these questions. The past cannot defend itself against our most convenient interpretation. Some people did the best they knew how to do under conditions we only partly understand, and some people's best still hurt children.

Other things travel too: humor, recipes, songs, craft, spiritual practices, stubbornness, language, repair, tenderness, resistance, and the memory of somebody who made a different choice when the old one would have been easier. A family line is also a route through which people kept finding reasons to live.

Honoring a family line takes both records, or ancestry turns into prosecution or advertising.

Maybe there is something you have done that rearranged your idea of who you are. Maybe you have never done anything you consider irredeemable. Good. Do not manufacture guilt because guilt sounds serious. But perhaps there is one sentence you avoid because speaking it would cost you the last clean version of your self-image.

The script can flip without warning.

You can be the child passed over and later the adult who stays away. You can need a mask and later become the person whose mask frightens the room, or survive control and become the one who controls when fear returns. You can demand honesty while you quietly decide which facts count, and you can be right about the system and wrong about what you are doing to the person beside you.

None of this makes everybody secretly the same. Moral positions change hands, and nobody holds one as permanent property. Civilizations rename their past the way families do. They keep some names and erase others, and they call an adaptation tradition, a conquest order, a wound destiny, and a profitable arrangement nature. Archives get built around what the powerful needed remembered, and gaps get built around what the powerful needed forgotten. Descendants inherit the story. Then they mistake the inheritance for evidence that the arrangement had to happen.

Humanity is not one household. The language of family should not sentimentalize conquest, slavery, genocide, colonialism, caste, war, or incarceration. Institutions can organize violence at a scale no private relationship can reproduce.

After emancipation, formerly enslaved people placed newspaper advertisements searching for children, parents, siblings, spouses, and other relatives. Sale and the domestic slave trade had separated them. Some searches continued decades after the original separation. The Last Seen project, supported by the National Archives, preserves thousands of those notices.

Those notices are evidence of what happens when a system claims authority over kinship itself, and I will not use them as metaphors for my family.

The system treated a person as property. It treated a relationship as something it could sell apart. The survivors kept printing the relationship back into the world.

Have you seen my mother?

Have you seen my child?

Do you know the name they use now?

The words are simple because the need under them is not.

I think of the baby bird asking, Are you my mother? I have to keep the scale honest here. A children's book is not the history of slavery, and the connection does not make the harms comparable. The connection shows that recognition, a name, return, and the right to belong are grave needs, and calling them childish misreads them. They are among the first structures power attacks when it wants people easier to control.

A system can break a family apart and then leave the descendants to explain the break as personality.

People still learn that system's habits in ordinary places: at the table, in school, in jokes, in who gets believed, and in whose pain becomes history while someone else's gets called attitude.

You did not choose the first language the world used to explain itself to you. Once you can hear it, you are still responsible for what it lets you do.

The unconformity

As a child I wanted to become an atlas, because maps made absence visible.

Geology goes further. It is the archive left after whoever made the record can no longer answer back.

A rock cannot explain what it meant. A layer cannot tell us we misread it. A geologist reads the record from what the rock is made of, where it sits, how it fractured, what pressure it took, where it touches other rock, and what remains on either side of the missing part.

In an ordinary sequence of sedimentary layers, the older material lies at the bottom and the younger material builds up on top. Geologists call that superposition. The idea turns into a metaphor almost at once. Childhood sits under adolescence. One generation sits under the next. History stacks up until the newest surface forgets how much weight it rests on.

A rock record is an imperfect stack. It erodes, folds, and cracks along faults. Rock gets buried, lifted, heated, weathered, dissolved, and exposed again. Some layers never form. Some form and later wear away. Much younger rock can end up lying directly on something ancient, with millions or hundreds of millions of years missing between them.

That missing time has a name. Geologists call it an unconformity.

At the Grand Canyon, the exposed rock is missing more time than it preserves. In places, a single contact between two layers spans more than a billion years of unrecorded time. The gap still tells us something. Geologists study the layers above and below it, the angle where they meet, the eroded surface, nearby formations, and similar records elsewhere. Missing time leaves evidence around its edges.

Families have unconformities.

My mother's mother leaves at birth. My father disappears for years. A child is told something is none of his business. A boarding-school record ends where family testimony falls silent. An abuse happens before the child has the words to build a continuous account. A household paints over the fighting and presents the finished wall as the original surface.

The years in the gap still happened, and the gap belongs to the record.

That does not give me permission to invent whatever event would make the later layer easiest to explain. The urge to fill an unconformity is the same urge that turns a family story into mythology. We see an effect, pick the cause that helps our current argument, and call the inference memory.

Responsible interpretation names the gap and studies what survived around it. The uncertainty stays where it belongs.

Sediment becomes rock through a process called lithification. The loose grains are pressed together, mineral cement fills the spaces between them, and grains that once moved separately become one solid formation.

Families harden stories in the same way. A frightening event becomes a rule. People repeat the rule until nobody remembers the event. Shame, reward, silence, money, gender, religion, survival, or plain exhaustion with arguing sets the repetition in place.

Eventually somebody says, “That is just how our family is,” as though the rock had formed with no process at all.

Something that feels permanent may be repetition pressed hard over time. Seeing that shows how it formed, even though the knowledge does not make it easy to break.

Metamorphic rock starts as a different rock and changes under heat and pressure. Its minerals form new crystals. Its structures stretch, twist, fold, and line up in the direction the pressure pushed. The original material is not simply erased. It takes a new arrangement, with the history of the force inside its structure.

A person is not a rock.

Pressure does not automatically make anyone better. Trauma does not turn people into diamonds, and suffering is no kiln that guarantees wisdom. Pressure can sometimes break a person or bend them out of shape, and at other times it leaves someone stronger in one direction and more fragile in another.

The geological picture still helps me see how a person can be transformed and stay connected to what came before.

Healing returns nobody to material that never felt pressure, and no original version waits untouched beneath the event. The new arrangement may hold new strength, planes where it can split, beauty, and vulnerability. The direction of the pressure may stay visible. That is history held inside a shape, and it is no failure.

Faults teach something else. When rock moves along a fault, a layer can be shifted so that matching pieces end up on opposite sides of a break. A geologist can sometimes match the layers across the break and estimate how far the ground moved.

Relationships have shifted layers too.

Two people may remember the same year from opposite sides of a fault. One remembers the argument. The other remembers the months of pressure before it. One remembers leaving. The other remembers every earlier moment that made leaving feel inevitable. The two records may line up without being identical.

The goal is to recognize the displacement. Pressing the two records into one approved story is no part of that goal.

Then there are fossils.

The fantasy of deep time, the billions of years of the Earth's history, can make it sound as though every body eventually turns to stone. That is false. Only a small percentage of living things become fossils. Quick burial and hard body parts improve the odds. Most life decays, gets eaten, dissolves, scatters, or enters cycles that keep no recognizable body.

Nobody is guaranteed a fossil.

One day millions of tons of mud, stone, roots, buildings, ocean sediment, or some material I cannot imagine may lie over the places where we stood. What is now my body may become soil, water, air, mineral, another living thing, or matter scattered beyond any shape that could still be called me.

The matter moves on into other forms. Some of my atoms may end up in a rock, and I will not live on as that rock. The world keeps using the material.

Some fossils are traces with no body in them: a footprint, a burrow, a trackway. They show that something lived and acted in a place even when none of the creature itself remains.

Legacy works more like a trace.

When we die, most of us will leave no body the future can read. We will leave changed conditions:

  • a child who no longer flinches at a question about money
  • a child who does
  • a bridge kept in repair
  • a river poisoned
  • a tool another person can inspect
  • a debt pushed onto people who never agreed to it
  • a story kept
  • a language brought back
  • a door opened
  • a door made to look open while the lock moved somewhere harder to see

Rock keeps only the effect.

People are more than geology. Living people can testify, dispute, forgive, refuse, and change what happens next. Death takes away our last chance to edit. We will not be there to say our intentions were better, that the harm was complicated, that the system made us do it, or that the monument deserves a generous reading. Our explanations stop when we do, and the effects keep moving.

We are a temporary arrangement of matter somewhere in the middle of the record, and for a short time we can choose what kind of trace goes into the next layer.

What will your trace make easier?

What will it make harder?

What will somebody infer about the world you believed should exist?

Sourcehood without a source

By sourcehood I mean being a real source of what you do, even though you did not make yourself.

The geological comparison stops at the point where a person has to answer for what they did.

A rock can carry the direction of pressure, and a formation can preserve a fault. Only a person can apologize for the avalanche or choose whether the shifting continues.

Here the philosophy under the software I build meets the family story.

None of us is causa sui, a Latin phrase for something that caused itself. My first nervous system, my parents and theirs, the schools, substances, songs, and silences already waiting for me, the economy around the household, the neighborhood, the language on offer for being a man, and the point in history when I grew old enough to make sense of any of it: none of that was my choice. My mother never chose to begin life without her mother, and she did not write every condition that later made attention and loyalty feel scarce. The abandonment my father later repeated was not his invention, and neither was the prison world where a dangerous mask could seem safer than an unguarded face. My stepfather did not create the boarding-school system whose damage reached the family before he did.

If responsibility requires being the first author of every cause that formed us, nobody can be responsible for anything.

That conclusion can sound kind. It can also become an excuse to treat people like weather.

I do not want that, because a person can answer me in a way a storm never can.

Holding someone responsible means speaking to them as a source inside the world: a person an action passed through, who can see what happened, answer for it, help repair it, and change what happens next. It makes no claim that they created themselves from nothing. A person can be a real source of their actions without having been the origin of themselves.

I am not the source of the source of me.

I am still one of the sources of what leaves me.

That freedom is harder than the fantasy of complete independence: no clean self of mine stands outside history, and history still has no final ownership of my hands.

The chain of causes runs through hormones, neurons, parents, institutions, money, language, culture, and chance. It also runs through me. I stand at the point where those conditions turn into an action another person has to live with, and that is where I start to answer for the action.

The system caused a great deal. Each of us still plays a part, though the parts differ and the power behind them differs too. The idea that we all play the victim turns dangerous when it flattens a child and a parent into equals. It does the same to the colonized and the institution, and to the person harmed and the person deciding right now whether to keep harming.

Shared humanity means nobody arrives self-made, and that includes the person I most want to condemn. Equal responsibility does not follow from it.

Seeing that is where forgiveness begins for me, and judgment does not stop there.

Forgiveness without deletion

People have used forgiveness to push others into silence, reunion, politeness, restored access, and spiritual performance. I have no interest in that version.

Nobody owes forgiveness to the person who hurt them.

Forgiveness clears nobody, and it makes no finding that the act was acceptable, unavoidable, mutual, or too old to matter. Memory can stay, and so can distrust and distance. Reconciliation is a separate choice, and forgiveness invites nobody back into the house. Restitution, consequence, anger, grief, and the right to say no remain in place, and forgiveness keeps the receipt.

For me, forgiveness means refusing to let the injury become the only author left.

I can forgive my father without calling the empty bleachers full. I can see the boy whose father turned toward six other children while moving away from him, and the young man who entered a Southern California prison and wore a skinhead mask that did not fit, because he believed it might keep him alive. I do not have to call the mask harmless, and I have no right to demand that anyone else forgive it. The symbol entered the world, people had reason to fear it, and he was responsible for wearing it. I can also say the mask was not the whole person and that he moved beyond it. The worst chapter a person survives does not have to be the only chapter anyone may read. And I was still the child at the game. He was the adult, and the road he chose had a consequence.

I can forgive my mother without saying I had enough room to feel. I can see the child who never met her mother and the daughter whose bond with her father may have been treated as a threat. She grew into a woman who learned to see other women as competition and possible loss. I can understand why minimizing made the fear louder. Her fear could fill the room, and a child should not have to become the permanent audience for a parent's uncontained life.

I can forgive my stepfather without agreeing that the problem was small. I can understand how pride protects a person who learned that being exposed brings humiliation. I can honor the lineage of a Native boarding-school survivor, and that suffering still does not pardon every harm that followed.

I can forgive the person who abused me without pretending I received closure, without calling what happened mutual, and without opening the door again. I had already tried to forgive before I knew what the word meant. Nobody can go back and demand adult forgiveness from that child. The act was wrong and still is, and the person who did it remains responsible. I refuse to organize the rest of my life around hatred, and that refusal leaves the charge as it stands.

Honoring an ancestor means seeing a person as more than the worst thing they passed down and remembering the conditions that narrowed their choices, without pretending those conditions removed every choice. It carries forward what kept life going and refuses whatever life should no longer require. None of that approves every act.

Some of our ancestors survived through silence, obedience, rage, or alcohol. Others left before anyone could leave them, made themselves indispensable, or grew hard enough that nobody could reach the wound again.

Those strategies may be part of why we are here. That does not mean our children should need them, and honoring the dead can mean ending the job an adaptation was hired to do.

I want my children to inherit names, stories, humor, music, recipes, curiosity, endurance, and the knowledge that people before them made it through conditions designed to erase them. I do not want them to inherit every unfinished alarm, or to confuse love with depletion, secrecy with dignity, money with shame, masculinity with possession, vigilance with wisdom, or disappearance with peace.

Forgiveness can look backward while it does its work for the future, and self-forgiveness works the same way.

Self-forgiveness is not a private acquittal. If I forgive myself by deciding my intentions were pure, or my trauma explains enough, or my shame has already punished me enough, I have built another private court.

Real self-forgiveness keeps the charge on the record.

I did this.

It affected you.

You do not owe me reassurance.

I may not be able to return what was taken.

I am still responsible for what can be repaired and for the conditions I create around the pattern now.

Then self-forgiveness refuses one more claim: that because I did harm, harm is the only honest future open to me.

You may be waiting for the world to issue a certificate saying you can be redeemed.

It may never come.

The people you hurt may never see the person you become, and the institution and the search result may keep the old label and the worst chapter. Somebody may refuse the context, because the context cannot undo what they had to survive.

Self-forgiveness cannot depend on winning the appeal. It has to begin without overruling the people who still say no.

The moral positions people hold can flip in more ways than any tidy story allows. In one scene you are the child passed over, and in another the adult driving past. The mask can be how you survive in one place, while somewhere else you are the person frightened by what it means.

I do not know whether everybody eventually does something that makes them feel beyond redemption. I know some of us do.

The world may never forgive the thing. The people harmed may never forgive it. A person may carry a stigma long after the danger, belief, or desperation behind the act is gone. None of that gives anyone a right to a cleaner record.

If the only verdict I allow myself is irredeemable, though, permanent self-hatred becomes one more excuse not to change. I can stay loyal to the worst version of myself because at least I know that role.

At some point we still have to pick up what remains and keep going. We do it because the future is still receiving what we do next, even though the past has not gone anywhere.

Without refusing that verdict, the one that says harm is my only honest future, shame turns into loyalty to the wound. I keep repeating the pattern because repeating it feels like the only sentence harsh enough to prove I understand it. I destroy myself and call the destruction moral clarity.

Self-destruction removes the person who owes the work.

Forgiveness leaves that person alive and drops the fantasy that wiping yourself out counts as repair. It keeps the work as large as it was and makes the work possible.

The river

At sixteen I went into acute renal failure, a sudden loss of kidney function. I spent time in the children's intensive care unit at Randall Children's Hospital. During that stay I had a dream of what I imagined death might be.

I was floating on a sturdy raft in a river. The river was a healthy size, and it was not so wide, deep, or fast that it frightened me. The water eased the pain inside my body and organs. The raft drifted gently to the shore, and I began climbing a hill covered in trees.

Near the top I saw two brontosauruses playing tag, or doing something close enough to tag that the name did not matter.

I felt happy.

The pain had eased, and I could watch other creatures enjoying a kind of simple joy I remembered feeling at points in my own life.

I do not claim I died. I was sick, frightened, medicated, and dreaming. I also do not need to reduce the dream to a clinical explanation before I admit that it stayed with me.

Looking back, the dream made relief beautiful, and relief is a different thing from disappearing.

When the red button appears to promise an end to contradiction, pain, accountability, or shame, it often lies about what is being requested. The wish may not be for the self to end. It may be for the pain to stop, for the chase to stop, or for the performance to stop. It may be for the body to become bearable, or for a shore where nobody demands another explanation.

The river gives me a different image to hold against it. Repair aims at relief, where the red button offers erasure. It looks for a shore and needs no verdict there, and on the hill it asks only for enough quiet to notice two impossible animals playing, with no final proof of innocence required. So repair has to make relief more available than annihilation.

Consequences stay in place. Repair refuses systems in which the only visible exits are disappearance, domination, denial, or death.

I do not know whether forgiveness is the river, though I know it can be a raft.

The first black box I learned to exploit

I bring artificial intelligence into an essay that has spent this long on family, rock, and forgiveness because I am tired of hearing people talk about black boxes, systems whose inner workings you cannot see, as if that opacity arrived with neural networks, the software behind modern AI.

People are black boxes with excellent public relations. We make a decision, feel the decision, and then hire language to defend it. We call appetite principle, fear discernment, status merit, property ethics, obedience professionalism, and avoidance self-care, and we call a legal outcome moral because the law happened to protect the person already holding the keys.

I did the same thing on a smaller scale, at home, where it hurt people I claimed to love.

When I lied, I was doing more than moving words around. I was editing another person's picture of reality without their permission. Then I benefited from what they did inside the picture I gave them. When I cheated, I changed the evidence other people used to decide who had done the work, who understood it, who deserved trust, and who should get an opportunity.

I controlled the input and then acted offended when anybody questioned the output.

That is a small autocracy, and nothing about it is glamorous. I hated authority and wore a little crown in private.

I still lie and avoid, and when I should sit down and listen I can still turn to charm, anger, collapse, or intellectual distance. Writing that down does not make me honest. It is closer to a risk disclosure.

It is also where the software I build comes from.

I do not offer those programs as my redemption or as evidence that I have become accountable. I do not want anyone to treat them as a technical indulgence, a modern version of the pardons the medieval church sold, issued in place of an apology. I built them as a confrontation, out of material I know how to use.

I am drawn to five things in that work, and each one traces back to something I grew up with:

  • source boundaries, the lines that show where a claim came from, because I grew up around stories whose boundaries moved with the needs of the person telling them
  • receipts, written records of what was checked, because that is none of your business is not an accounting system
  • independent verification, because the loudest person in the room should not own the standard, the verdict, and the appeal
  • preserved witnesses, saved evidence of what happened, because I remember the child at the park opening his mouth and producing no record at all
  • UNVERIFIABLE, the label my tools give a claim when the evidence cannot settle it, because forcing certainty when the witness is missing is another way to let power write the ending

The tools have a narrow job: to make some of my favorite evasions harder. Saving me is no part of it, and testifying that I am a good person is certainly no part of it. Here is what five of them do. Crucible takes an argument apart and asks what would disprove each claim. Forum keeps a record of what AI agents, programs that carry out tasks on their own, did and what caused each step. EMET checks the bytes of a file, the content itself, and does not treat a digital signature, the electronic stamp that shows who signed a file, as moral authority. Gather tries to keep visible where each piece of information came from. Learn keeps the person involved, because I do not want a machine that makes me faster while I understand less of what I am doing.

A person can build a smoke alarm and still set the house on fire.

Whether the tools prove anything good about me is beside the point. The work should hold up without any goodness from the person who built it.

A system built by a liar should be able to catch the liar. If an egotist builds it, it should survive his loss of control, and when the builder is somebody desperate for recognition, it should keep working after his name comes off.

I do not want the work to walk into the room ahead of me and bargain for a better verdict on my behalf. An idea is separate from me, the way a brick is separate from the hand that set it down. A principle is true or false on its own terms, whether a decent person said it or a hypocrite found it first.

The question is whether the brick bears weight: whether another person can inspect it, move it, test it, replace it, or use it to build something I never imagined; whether the system can keep a useful claim while rejecting the person who made it; and whether it can protect somebody from me.

Work that needs admiration is one more machine for feeding insecurity. Work that can indict me, outlive me, and stay useful to people who never need to trust me may be infrastructure. A creator's confession cannot vouch for the creation, and the creation cannot absolve the creator.

The flywheel turned inward

The central claim behind Flywheel, the software I build to check the work of AI systems, is that correctness comes from the loop built around an AI model, and that the model alone cannot supply it.

The loop runs like this. A system takes in a task and proposes an answer. It checks the answer against a standard that the proposer did not write. It keeps a receipt. Then it feeds the verified experience back into its memory, its context (the information handed to the model with each task), its evaluation, and its future work. A failure stays in the record even when it spoils the story. It becomes a regression case, a saved test that catches the same failure if it comes back. It can also become a boundary, a lesson, or an honest null: a plain record that the hoped-for result did not appear.

The system grows from experience that survived contact with something able to say no.

That design came out of engineering problems. It rhymes with the human story closely enough to help, as long as I keep the boundary between them visible.

A model has weights, the numbers it learned in training. It has no childhood.

It has no body remembering a hospital room, no attachment system (the instinct that bonds a child to a caregiver), no ancestors, and no fear of an empty set of bleachers. It has no child waiting for a parent to come back, no capacity to forgive, no moral injury, and no family that its explanation can hurt. I have no right to assume it has an inner life simply because the metaphor is convenient.

The analogy concerns conditioning. It makes no claim that a model and a person are equivalent.

Models and people are both shaped by their environments, and both can become more likely to repeat whatever gets rewarded. Either can overfit, learning one narrow history so closely that it mistakes the local pattern for the world, and either can hold on to failures in ways that warp later judgment. Each can improve when the environment gives better examples, clearer feedback, wider coverage of situations, and a way to tell what only felt successful from what held up.

Then the analogy reaches its limit.

A human being has no test suite, the automated checks software runs, for moral worth. A family is not an oracle, a trusted source of correct answers. A spouse is not a reward model, the program that scores answers so another model can learn from the scores. A child should not become the evaluation harness, the rig that runs the tests, for a parent's growth. A poem has no kernel, the small core that everything else runs on, and neither does forgiveness.

Where the analogy breaks, the seams act as witnesses and never as foundations.

Another person's pain can witness an effect I did not intend. A boundary can show that my preferred reading is one reading among others with standing. A corrected record can witness that the first story was incomplete, and repeated behavior can witness that a promise has yet to become a reliable change.

None of those witnesses calculates my value as a person. They tell me where my current model of myself fails, and that is enough to begin another cycle:

  • Perceive what happened.
  • Name the claim.
  • Let the person affected describe the effect, without editing their report into a format I prefer.
  • Separate intention from outcome.
  • Identify what would count as change before announcing that change occurred.
  • Put friction around the path that keeps winning.
  • Practice another response.
  • Come back later and see whether the result held once the emotional moment of the promise had passed.
  • Keep the miss.
  • Carry the verified lesson forward.

That is a human flywheel, and it differs from a machine one. Its standard includes relationship, consent, agency, context, and values that no optimizer (the software that tunes a model toward a higher score) discovers on its own. It requires judgment, care, and people who are free to leave, and the person building the loop has to stay answerable to the people living inside its effects.

The project credo lists growth, learning, knowledge, application, and ownership, in that order.

I used to demand ownership first.

I wanted the title, the authority, the keys to the city, the proof that I belonged, and the right to act from that standing. I wanted the badge to arrive before the understanding. Then I resented anyone who asked me to walk the work.

The order matters:

  • Growth before ownership: the self is not a fixed credential.
  • Learning before ownership: being corrected does not steal my identity.
  • Knowledge before application: a fast response should not become action because urgency feels sincere.
  • Application before ownership: I do not own a principle because I can talk about it beautifully.

Ownership is earned by understanding.

In human terms, understanding means showing that I grasp what happened well enough to behave differently when the same pressure returns. Reciting the apology does not show that.

The Flywheel documentation describes verified experience becoming the nutrition of the next version of the model. That phrase lands differently when I think about children.

What experience becomes their nutrition?

Do they learn that conflict ends in repair or in disappearance? That money can be discussed, or only feared? That adults can admit uncertainty, or must defend their authority? That a mistake becomes a lesson, or an identity? That love has room for their inner life, or that love means finding the adult's need before their own?

Every household is a developmental environment, whether anyone designed it to be one or not. Children will learn from us either way, so the open question is what our repeated outputs teach them.

A healthy flywheel does not feed every output back into itself. It does not train on its own confident guesses. It chooses what to feed back by what passes its checks, records where each piece came from, and uses a one-way valve, so a failure cannot come back in disguised as a success.

Human families need a similar discipline, without pretending people are datasets:

  • Do not let the explanation become the training label, the answer the system learns to treat as correct.
  • Do not call a pattern repaired because the person who produced it feels ashamed.
  • Do not teach the child that the adult's intention outranks the child's perception.
  • Do not bank a promise as evidence before it survives repetition.
  • Do not quietly change the standard after the miss.
  • Do not delete the failed run.

At the same time, do not make failure the only data the family keeps. A system trained only on error becomes distorted in another way. Children should see repair succeed too: an adult who pauses, comes back, apologizes without falling apart, accepts a no, changes a plan, tells the truth about money, asks for help without making the helper responsible for everything, and keeps a promise after the strong feeling has passed.

Verified experience should include the good things that happened.

The flywheel has limits, and its own thesis states an honest ceiling. Recirculating experience can make that experience more useful. It cannot create coverage of situations the system never met. A model cannot learn a domain it has never seen by replaying what it already has.

Neither can a family.

Sometimes the new experience has to come from outside the family line: another culture, a teacher, a therapist, a friend, a partner, a book, a community, a spiritual practice, a medication, a boundary, an institution that works differently, or a child who asks a question nobody in the family learned how to answer.

That outside experience is new coverage. It widens what the family has seen and contaminates nothing.

The mistake is believing healing has to be built only from materials already inside the wound.

The robe and the farm

Kahlil Gibran gave me one of the images that holds this argument together.

In his 1912 novel The Broken Wings, a bishop forces Selma Karamy into marriage with his nephew for the sake of her father's wealth. Her defeat has nothing to do with how well she argues. No fair hearing exists for her. The bishop controls the standard, benefits from the decision, and sits above any real appeal. His robe makes persuasion unnecessary. Power has dressed itself up as propriety. Everyone around it understands what is happening, and they still behave as though proper process took place.

That structure is the cage. A system wears the robe when the judge writes the standard, profits from the verdict, and stops the people living under it from checking the decision again. The costume changes while the structure can stay the same.

A university can define merit, sell access to the definition, and point to the credential as proof the definition worked. A publisher can let prestige stand in for reasoning that readers can inspect. A benchmark, a standard test used to rank performance, can keep handing out authority even when its tests cannot reject a wrong answer. A technology company can define safety, originality, capability, or acceptable use while keeping the evidence needed to challenge those definitions inside the company.

A platform can swallow culture, own the route to the audience, generate substitutes for the people whose work fed it, and set the price of being seen. It can still present itself as a neutral marketplace.

Creative communities can wear the robe too. I do not say that to dismiss the real harm being done to artists. A human-only practice can matter. The process can be part of what the work means. Consent, labor, identity, chosen limits, and tradition matter. A community is allowed to say, "This space is for people working under these conditions."

The problem starts when pedigree gets smuggled in under the name of ethics. Familiarity starts to pass for authenticity, difficulty for virtue, and membership for merit. The old gate feels human because people spent years learning how to survive inside it.

I wore the robe whenever I decided my intention mattered more than someone else's experience. I wrote the standard and benefited from the verdict. I made appeal expensive through anger, absence, charm, collapse, threats, or a sudden show of being too broken to answer a plain question. Hating the robe did not stop me from putting it on.

That matters because I have been anti-authoritarian, opposed to rule by unchecked power, for most of my life. I know the tidy version of that story: I distrusted power, found the weak point, and built alternatives for the people kept outside.

Sometimes that is true.

In another version, I hated being ruled and did not notice how often I was trying to become impossible to rule.

Pink Floyd gave me the sound of that contradiction before I had words for it. Their album Animals shaped much of my early criticism and wonder. I heard it before I knew what class analysis was, and before I had useful words for institutions or economic power.

I understood the animals anyway: dogs that keep running until the chase replaces the purpose, pigs that turn appetite into moral authority, sheep that move with the flock because leaving feels more dangerous right now than wherever the flock is headed.

The record sounded industrial and biological at the same time. It had concrete, machinery, isolation, instinct, and hierarchy in it, and huge human structures acting as though they were simply nature.

Then Orwell gave me the farm. Animal Farm taught me a distinction I have had to learn again and again: removing the owner does not necessarily remove ownership. A revolution can change the slogans, the names on the wall, and the people in the farmhouse. The grammar of the relationship underneath can survive all of it. The rebel can inherit the robe, and the animal who learned to spot the farmer can still want the farmer's chair.

I took that in as political criticism long before I understood it as a warning about myself.

At first the animals were always other people. In my reading the dogs were executives, enforcers, climbers, and anyone willing to bite for a place near power; the pigs were politicians, preachers, billionaires, administrators, and founders who turned coordination into ownership; and the sheep were the people who followed, repeated, and went along. That reading conveniently left my own category empty.

It took me longer to admit that all three animals can live in the same person, sometimes in the same afternoon.

I have been the dog, chasing prestige, recognition, relief, capability, money, and the hope of becoming impressive enough to quiet the private argument about whether I deserved to exist. I have kept chasing goals after the chase began hurting the people walking beside me. I have mistaken movement for direction and intensity for sincerity.

I have been the pig. I have criticized hypocrisy while protecting my own. I have talked about liberation while expecting other people to absorb the cost of my comfort and my instability. I have treated seeing clearly as a kind of entitlement, as though seeing the cage excused me from examining what I was doing inside it.

I have been the sheep. I have followed inherited ideas about masculinity, success, substances, survival, resentment, and independence. I have called my behavior self-directed because it pushed against an authority, even when that authority still set my direction by giving me something to oppose.

These are roles, patterns, and capacities under incentives, and none of them is a species: no dog is born to hunt forever, no pig is corrupt by nature, and a sheep is no lesser kind of person.

Persistence can turn into predation when the target replaces the reason for running. When the person coordinating a system starts to believe everything passing through it belongs to them, stewardship can become ownership. Trust and learning from others can slide into submission once the group loses the right to question where it is going.

Criticism means little if it cannot turn around and look at me.

I want the conditions to change, and a better bishop, a kinder pig, or a founder so persuasive that nobody asks for an appeal would not change them. The standard should not belong entirely to the person it judges or the person who profits from it. The record should survive embarrassment. The people affected should have somewhere to stand when they disagree. The work should be able to condemn the worker.

The bundle breaks

These contradictions predate artificial intelligence, which made them move faster and harder to hide.

For a long time, difficult work was tied to the road the worker took to get there. A solved problem implied study. A polished illustration implied practice. Working code implied some contact with the machinery underneath it. The contribution and the life story of the person who made it traveled together so closely that we built institutions around the bundle.

Credentials, interviews, seniority, portfolios, peer review, code review, guilds, and reputation became stand-ins. They let people judge work without inspecting all of it, because full inspection was expensive.

Then the bundle split open.

A person can now produce work that looks expert without traveling the expected road. The surface no longer tells you how much understanding, labor, intention, borrowed structure, or machine help sits underneath it.

I feel the freedom in that.

I missed the official road into research. For years I treated that miss as a ruling on whether I was allowed to discover anything. These tools gave me a way back toward the questions. They can translate a paper, explain the same idea until one version lands, help someone outside an institution learn that the question they keep circling already has a name, and let one person attempt work that once needed a department and a budget.

Pedigree has shut out capable people for reasons that had little to do with what they could contribute: money, disability, geography, race, language, family obligations, neurotype (how a person's brain happens to work), knowing how institutions work, social manner, timing, and the thousand accidents that decide who gets a clean runway.

I will not pretend that this opening means nothing, or that it arrived clean.

Work that looks expert is cheap now, and expertise still costs what it did. Generating work got faster while checking it stayed costly, so the cost moved downstream: software maintainers read the pull requests, the code changes people submit; teachers work out whether a student understands the code; editors sort the plausible from the real; researchers rebuild the proof; and clients and audiences find out which polished surface had nothing underneath it.

I call that review debt. Somebody pays it, even when the bill stays out of sight.

Creative workers pay a different version. Their craft was more than a badge. It paid rent, built identity and community, and gave shape to years of practice. When a client can ask for ten plausible images in an afternoon, the illustrator gets a lower rate, a shorter deadline, or no call at all, and none of it arrives as an abstract seminar about technological progress.

You can tell that person that difficulty was never identical to quality, and it is true. It is also cruelly incomplete.

The old gate was unjust. People still built real lives inside it. Breaking the gate does not remove our responsibility for whoever is standing underneath when it falls.

At this point the public argument usually splits into two performances.

One side says AI is just a tool, as though tools had no owners, training histories, labor effects, energy costs, supply chains or politics. This side celebrates the end of pedigree while it pays monthly rent to a new landlord of cognition. It generates faster than it can understand, and it assumes somebody else will clean up the result.

The other side is right about extraction, imitated identities, collapsing rates and flooded markets. It is right that corporations use the language of inevitability to make dispossession sound natural. Some people in that camp still turn suffering into a credential, treating hours as moral worth and scarcity as authenticity and calling every hybrid practice fraud. Human-made does not automatically mean humane. In that account the assistants, samplers, stock libraries, fabrication chains, software abstractions, unpaid interns, exploited vendors and inherited conventions beneath supposedly pure production drop out of view.

The companies that build foundation models carry their own contradiction. A foundation model is a large general-purpose system trained on vast amounts of published text, and often on images and sound as well. These companies absorb enormous portions of human culture, turn that material into private capability, meter access to the result and call the rental democratization. A library does not become democratic because one company scanned it, sealed the scanner and now charges by the paragraph.

Institutions spent decades telling us that merit lived behind the correct tuition payment, internship, citation network, vocabulary and way of standing in a room. Now that AI lets outsiders produce finished work that passes at the surface, some of those institutions have found a sacred concern for process.

Capital makes the oldest move of all. It promises that productivity will free us, then turns every saved hour into fewer workers, more surveillance or a larger margin. It says abundance is coming and makes sure the means stay scarce.

I am using an AI system to help write this.

No reveal comes later. I am not running a test to see whether you notice. I gave the system my earlier essays, public comments, research notes, personal history, arguments and revisions. It helped me search, sort, challenge and edit. I threw out earlier versions because they sounded like a machine laundering my life through tasteful paragraphs. I still throw out sentences that care more about landing than breathing.

This version is AI-assisted. It is also mine, in the only sense of authorship I am prepared to defend: I can explain why the claims are here, I accept responsibility for the result, and I am willing to be contradicted.

The machine can arrange language, but it cannot apologize for me, owe my family anything, absorb the consequence if I use confession to manipulate people, or decide whether I understand these words well enough to say them in front of another person. That part is mine.

The useful questions go past human or machine. They ask who chose the work, who understood it and who checked it; who consented and who was paid; who can explain the result, who carries the consequence and who gets to say no.

A purity label is easier to apply than any of those, and that is why everybody keeps reaching for one.

The graph and the poem

On July 22, 2026, Dmitry Rybin publicly announced an explicit graph that he says refutes the cost version of the single-source unsplittable-flow conjecture associated with Dinitz, Garg and Goemans. In plain terms, the problem asks how cheaply you can route shipments from one source through a network when each shipment has to travel along a single path. A conjecture is a claim mathematicians suspect is true and have not proved. This one says that for any routing where shipments may split, some single-path routing costs no more, provided each link may carry extra load up to the size of the largest shipment. Rybin's reported construction has a fractional flow, where shipments may split, of cost 58. By his account, every unsplittable flow within the stated additive capacity violation of 15, the extra load each link is allowed to carry, has cost at least 60, so no single-path routing inside that allowance matches the cost of the split one. Rybin says the candidate came out of work with GPT-5.6 Pro.

At the time of this revision, the responsible label is still public counterexample announcement pending independent verification. That label is the point of the example.

A universal conjecture says no counterexample exists, so one exact graph can end it. The graph does not care whether it came from a famous laboratory, a graduate seminar, a private notebook or a long conversation with a model. It asks for no diploma. It only asks whether the stated claim holds up against the object.

This is where AI makes sense to me as a tool for discovery: it can search a space too large and too tedious for one person to walk by hand, keep mutating constructions after a person has gone to sleep, and follow an ugly branch nobody has any prestige invested in. Whatever it finds still has to face something that can say no.

The model's confidence does not count. Rebuild the flow, check the fractional cost and the capacity bound, and prove the lower bound for every allowed unsplittable flow. Then publish the object, the assumptions, the code and enough of the route that another person can look for the crack.

That setup tears at pedigree, and expertise still matters inside it. The person who frames the problem, the model that generates candidates, the exact solver (a program that computes the exact lowest cost), the mathematician who rebuilds the argument and the reviewer who tries to break it may each add something different. Record each part, and stop forcing the process into a myth where one badge has to absorb all of them.

The same speed carries a danger. A model can produce plausible conjectures, counterexamples, proofs and papers much faster than people can check them. A landfill with one diamond in it is still a landfill if nobody can afford to sort it.

So I reject both easy stories. The model solved it and humans are finished is status theater, and so is it is autocomplete and nothing happened. Something did happen: the limit of what we can search moved, and the line of what counts as proved stayed where it was.

Mathematics has a luxury that art lacks: a graph can kill a conjecture. In computer-checked mathematics, a small and strict program called a proof kernel accepts or rejects each proof. A poem has no kernel. A painting cannot compile into beauty, a song cannot fail because its grief went outside tolerance, and a film does not become meaningful because a scoring program called it a match.

Art loses nothing by that absence, which is one reason art remains a place where people can meet without pretending every difference has a final judge.

We can still tell the truth about the process: what went into the work, which model and which version, whose material under what license, which prompt, seed (the starting number that fixes one run of a system), edit, selection, tool, collaborator or physical signal mattered, who rejected the failures, who chose the final piece and who takes responsibility for releasing it. That account does not prove the work is beautiful. It makes the lineage less dishonest.

People cram several arguments into the word authentic. Sometimes they mean factually sound. Sometimes they mean honestly disclosed, economically fair, personally expressive, rooted in a culture, hard to make or aesthetically alive. Those questions are related, and they are still separate questions.

A licensed dataset can produce boring art, and a person working by hand can make something derivative. A generated image can move somebody while it comes out of an exploitative pipeline; a transparent process can produce an ugly result, and a beautiful object can sit on top of abusive labor.

Human-only spaces should survive. A gallery, contest, journal, performance, class or commission can treat process as part of the meaning. An acoustic set has no quarrel with electricity, and a chess tournament that bans engines still accepts that computers can play chess. A hand-thrown bowl is allowed to matter as a hand-thrown bowl.

Hybrid spaces should survive too. The violence begins when one space tries to erase the other and calls the erasure freedom.

Build the instrument

The strongest moral criticism of current generative media lies somewhere other than computation touching art. Artists have used cameras, darkrooms, plotters, samplers, synthesizers, procedural systems (rules that generate images or sound), 3D software, compositing, game engines, custom shaders (small programs that color each pixel), found material, assistants and fabrication shops for a long time. That tool analogy does not erase the scale or the training history of foundation models. It reminds us that creative agency has never meant doing every operation with bare hands.

The sharper question is what went into the generator, with whose permission, and who captured the value.

I think true generative art can stand on another foundation. Start lower than the prompt, and build the instrument.

First choose the primitives, the basic pieces the work is made from: points, curves, glyphs, meshes, particles, colors, marks, materials, sounds, fields.

Then choose the laws that move them, the rules that decide how those pieces move and grow: grammars, collision rules, growth, reaction-diffusion, cellular systems, fluid approximations, signal processing, shaders, topology, custom physics, whatever the work needs.

Variation can come from a seed, a hand gesture, a drawing, a microphone, local weather, motion, a plant, a machine, the electrical noise of the room, material a collaborator chooses to contribute, or a camera that works as a sensor and never as a scraper of other people's culture.

Search can breed variations and keep the strongest (evolutionary search), reward whatever is new (novelty search), write small programs automatically (program synthesis), hunt for arrangements that obey fixed rules (constraint solving) or learn by trial and reward (reinforcement learning), all inside the world the artist built. Search can also be a person hammering on the system for months until they understand its temperament.

In that practice the artist builds a space of possibilities, enters it and learns what the rules made possible by accident. A hidden archive that imitates a finished surface has no part in it. That can be art, and the instrument itself may be the deepest part of the work.

When I think about the books, films, paintings and songs that formed me, the honest question is what reached me underneath their surfaces. How to make a machine copy those surfaces is the wrong question. What reached me was dissonance, sacrifice, impossible space, a body becoming a shell, a threshold that felt like escape and judgment at once, light caught in a ruined world, and a thread still pulling toward something the person cannot name.

Those relations can become forces, constraints, materials, timing, geometry and transformation. Asking for something in the style of another person is a different act. This is a system built to explore the pressure underneath the style. A learned system, a program that learns from examples, can start from random initialization, which means from numbers with no training in them yet. It can then train only on self-authored material, licensed collaborators, public-domain sources, synthetic simulation (data a program generates) or interaction inside a bounded environment, a closed setting built for the purpose. Weights carry no moral curse. The question is what formed them, with whose permission, and whether the account of it is honest.

Another artist may choose a foundation model and disclose it. Fine. Say so. Declare the ancestry and let the audience decide what it means.

I want no new purity hierarchy. I want real choices and a lineage somebody can inspect.

I am not a clean origin point. Nobody is.

I am a living, breathing reaction to my father, my mother, my stepfather, my brother and my peers; to the women I loved and hurt, the men I copied, the rooms I survived and the institutions I distrusted; to the music that taught me how to posture, the art that taught me how to ache, the drugs I used to leave myself and the code that let me build another world; and to every person who showed me kindness before I had learned how to return it.

Environment enters. I transform it. Consequence leaves.

That is no acquittal. A reaction still has products, and people still breathe what comes out of the chamber. The transformation is real, though. If the inputs, constraints, catalysts and feedback change, the reaction can change too.

Sampling is the analogy I keep coming back to. Sampling showed that culture was already recursive; it was never proof that musicians had run out of ideas. A drum break could leave one room, cross a decade, meet a machine, a neighborhood and another grief, and become something that did not exist before. The sampler was only part of the act. Selection, timing, chopping, arrangement, context and performance mattered, and so did the person who heard a possibility inside the earlier sound.

The ugliest part was the ownership system around the master recordings: labels, clearance regimes, contracts, distribution choke points, and people who made themselves indispensable by standing between artists and one another. Transformation itself was not the ugliest part.

AI is rebuilding the record label around the sampler, and I want to interrupt that.

The model can become an instrument. A source graph, a map of what fed into a work, can preserve its lineage. Artists can declare what went in, what was licensed, what was refused, what was transformed, and who should receive credit or an agreed share. Communities can own the tools, the relationships and the shared funds while a steward, one coordinator, keeps the system running without becoming the landlord of the culture moving through it.

Keep the lineage. Lose the label.

The principle applies beyond art. Every field has its record labels: journals that confuse distribution with authority, credentials that confuse access with merit, banks that confuse coordination with ownership, cloud platforms that confuse hosting with a permanent claim on capability, and bureaucracies that turn a necessary boundary into a hereditary class of people allowed to speak.

The aim is to keep expertise, consent, attribution, privacy and payment, and to stop letting the coordinator own the whole reaction.

I know the language I inherited for defiance. The music taught me lines like, "ima fuck yo bitch." I understand the voltage. It is conquest pressed into one sentence: I can enter your world, take what you call yours and make your authority look weak.

Listen to the property claim inside it. A woman is not evidence that I defeated another man, or a territory, a receipt of conquest or a supporting character in a performance of masculinity. If I am trying to dismantle ownership, I cannot keep reaching for a sentence that turns a woman into property whenever I want to sound powerful.

So I want to keep the defiance and kill the possession.

Fuck the monopoly, and leave the woman out of it. Fuck the gate, and spare the person trapped beside it. Fuck the label, the landlord, the lie and the little pipe they expect everybody else to drink from.

Come get me means inspect the work, contradict me, rebuild it, fork it (copy it and take it in your own direction) and show me the receipt that proves the principle fails. I do not need to threaten a body to stand behind an idea.

My principle is fertile. Let it impregnate the culture, not possess a person. Breed it, fork it, mutate it, and give birth to something better than me. Let it reproduce because people choose to carry it, because it gives them power to act, food, time, dignity or a way to make something they could not make before.

I may still use the old language or fall into the old posture. The record already shows that knowing better has never guaranteed that I behave better.

I want to learn to call women women, and I want to see myself as a man. Manhood gives me no license to dominate, collect, conquer or disappear when the bill arrives. The manhood I want provides without possessing, protects without controlling, has a strength that can hear no, and stays present after the explanation stops working.

I want to provide something good. That does not make me good. Sincerity is not a receipt. The work has to survive contact with another person's reality and be useful without anyone having faith in me. It has to keep an exit open for when I become the obstacle, and let somebody take the principle farther than I can, change it, reject me and still keep what helped.

I am a living, breathing reaction. I carry what came before me, and the reaction is still going on.

The same honesty can be built into the tools. A serious creative tool could write a process record as it works: what went in, what stayed out, which parts learned, how candidates were explored, when a human stepped in, what computing power was used and what rights came with the material. It could keep the failed paths and replay the sequence that produced the final piece.

That record would not certify originality. Nothing comes from nowhere. Human artists digest parents, teachers, enemies, neighborhoods, books, accidents, other artists, software, weather, music through a wall and the awful sentence somebody said ten years ago that never stopped echoing.

Novelty has an ancestry too. It is a relation that did not exist before, made through an ancestry we are willing to name.

The little pipe and the earth beneath it

The old world gatekept knowledge through pedigree. The new one is learning to gatekeep it through an invoice.

The invoice arrives as metered tokens, where you pay for each small piece of text a model reads or writes, along with closed weights nobody outside can inspect, plans that shift, account bans and workflows that only run in someone else's cloud. Capability pours toward corporations, governments and the same networks that have always mistaken proximity for merit. The surface stays open for as long as you can keep paying its landlord.

This is one of the places where profanity still feels precise to me.

Fuck the little pipe.

I am tired of watching people receive cognition as a subscription while a few companies decide how much capability may flow this month.

The infrastructure costs money. The models cost money. Somebody has to pay. That is true. It does not follow that permanent dependency is the only architecture available. I am tired of hearing democratization from people who build permanent dependency into the product.

Still, a slogan is not an architecture. I can condemn capitalism, technofeudalism (the idea that tech platforms now collect rent from users the way lords once collected it from tenants) and autocracy all day and wake up inside the same billing portal tomorrow.

So the work has to get concrete: local-first tools that run on your own machine; smaller models made more capable through good scaffolding (the software built around a model), memory, routing and checking, in place of permanent dependence on the largest private cluster, meaning the biggest privately owned data centers; portable histories you can take with you when you leave a service, open protocols, community-owned computing power, cooperative licensing and data trusts, which hold people's data and bargain on their behalf; labor agreements that return productivity gains to workers; and the right to run offline, inspect, repair, fork and leave.

The productivity gain arrives with no moral owner installed. If a week's work becomes a day and the worker gets four days back, that is liberation. If the employer removes four people, that is extraction. The difference is politics.

Include the earth. Burning the planet to produce endless disposable content liberates nobody. Data centers are physical. The cloud has a grid connection. Local computing has a material cost too. Hardware comes from mines, factories, water, energy, transport, labor and landscapes somebody lives beside.

The planet is the final outside check, and physics does not accept a pitch deck.

If our creative future needs hidden sacrifice zones, poisoned water, fossil expansion and communities priced out of their own power grids, then all we did was export the cage.

So the new ethic needs compute budgets, meaning limits on the computing power a project may use, along with efficiency targets, open reporting of energy use, hardware that lasts, hardware people can repair and a preference for smaller systems when smaller systems can do the work. Local-first cannot become an excuse for every home to burn power wastefully either. Whatever way these systems run, ecological cost has to go into the ledger, where the word cloud can no longer hide it.

AI can help us search for materials, balance power grids, model biology, explore mathematics and find structures we could not see yesterday. It can also become a machine that turns the living world into synthetic content nobody asked to remember.

The questions from earlier come back in a new form once you treat AI as a vehicle: who owns the vehicle, who chooses the direction, who receives the gain, and who carries the exhaust, the costs it leaves behind?

What I owe the Mad-Happy Scientist

I keep returning to the child I called the Mad-Happy Scientist because I think a lot of people have some version of him.

Maybe yours was an artist, an astronaut, a mechanic, a naturalist, a writer, a musician, a teacher, a healer, or the kid who took apart every broken object in the house to learn whether failure had a shape.

Then life happened. Some people jumped through the hoops and made it; others tripped, were pushed out, turned left, or found work that paid the rent and slowly separated them from the part of themselves that asked questions out of plain wonder.

I treated the missed road into formal research as a verdict. I let pedigree become metaphysics. The institution had a map, and I decided that any territory outside it must be imaginary. Then I resented the institution for a cage I was helping to maintain.

That resentment fed my grandiosity. Every project had to prove everything, every good week had to become an empire, and every new tool had to repay every lost year at once. The bipolar tendencies I recognize in myself can turn light into appetite: I find something real and want all of it at once (more capability, projects, proof, impact and recognition, every door open) until the structure outruns me, I collapse, and the people around me inherit the debris. I have spent a lot of time trying to swallow the sun and acting surprised when it burns.

Grandiosity and shame are close relatives. Grandiosity says I deserve the keys to the city and shame says I am too corrupt to enter it, and both keep the world organized around me. Ordinary work starts to feel too small, which is convenient, because ordinary work is where accountability usually lives.

In practice that means one test that can fail, one claim another person can inspect, one tool that runs on a machine somebody already owns, one artist who names the lineage, one apology made without controlling the response, one boundary accepted even though it hurts, one child who does not have to manage the adult's weather and one door left open behind you.

None of those feels large enough when I am trying to repay a whole life in a weekend, and they are still the things that count.

I still want the keys to the city. That hunger did not go away because I found better language for it. If somebody handed me the keys, I would probably notice the lock and wonder whether I could open it another way.

The change is that I no longer want to pick the lock only for myself. I want to open the cage, throw away the key and let the walls rot into soil. I want someone who never learned the password to have a surface where their work can be examined without anyone first asking whether they belong, artists to have more choices than extraction or martyrdom, workers to keep some of the time the machine saves, people to use tools they can inspect and leave, and a structure where the person maintaining the bridge cannot quietly claim both shores.

That sounds beautiful, and it should also make you suspicious.

People who promised universal liberation have worn a lot of robes. The founder who says he builds for everyone can become another bishop faster than he can say open source. A movement can start by rejecting the old gate and end by deciding that disagreement itself is sabotage.

So contradict me. Inspect the system, reject the doctrine, fork the tool, leave the platform, protect your inner life and tell me where the argument breaks.

Nothing I build gets to inherit authority from the fact that I hate authority.

The promise I can make

I used to want a killing blow against my ego. Even that can become theater.

The self has no clean terminal command, no line typed into a computer that resets a person. The ego does not need root access (full control of the machine). Even so, I cannot delete it and call the deletion repair.

At sixteen I thought disappearing might solve the contradiction of being alive inside myself. Later I found slower ways to disappear: drugs, fights, code, isolation, ambition, fantasy, collapse, and a show of hating myself so thorough that nobody could ask me for anything more specific.

I am done using annihilation as evidence that I understand the harm. The apology has to be less cinematic:

  • Tell the truth before exposure forces it.
  • Correct the record.
  • Return what can be returned.
  • Pay the cost you moved.
  • Accept another person's boundary without turning it into a vote on your worth.
  • Listen without editing their account into something easier to survive.
  • Do not demand forgiveness as the prize for finally admitting what happened.
  • Put friction around the behavior that good intentions have failed to stop again and again.
  • Then keep going, because repair needs somebody alive to do it.

My mistakes are part of the record. They inform the risk, and they still do not get to write every future verdict alone.

I am not asking to be seen as good.

I am trying to become answerable.

Maybe the human quality I was looking for underneath all of this is a different kind of thing from a protected list of tasks a machine can never do. Maybe it is the ability to owe, to be contradicted, to change without deleting the record, to care what our actions do inside another life and to stay present after the explanation runs out.

I believe this strongly enough that I am afraid of failing it. Even after writing all of this, I do not trust myself to live by every word for the rest of my life. I know my patterns: conviction can burn hot and still lose to illness, appetite, exhaustion, shame, fear or the next idea that makes everything else disappear. I am leaving that doubt in the ending, even though an ending without it would feel more complete.

Mental health belongs here because no system built for people gets to pretend that people are stable machines. Everybody needs some way back to themselves, whether faith, meditation, therapy, medication, curiosity, reflection, movement, art or community. It may be one of these or several, and the mix may change over time.

Accepting help is no surrender, and autonomy grants no immunity.

The question I have to ask is whether a route makes me more present, less destructive and more able to answer for what I do. If it only helps me disappear more comfortably, choosing it does not make it freedom.

I have said I like to sit in my pain. The more honest answer is that I do a bit of both. Sometimes I sit beside it because I need to hear what it has been trying to tell me. Sometimes I sit inside it because punishment feels safer than change. I have made a home there before, and I know the furniture. I own that.

I am also beginning to learn how to love myself again.

I believe people deserve the possibility of redemption, which differs from erasure, automatic forgiveness or access restored on demand. Redemption means the record does not have to become the rest of a life. If only clean people qualify, it becomes another gate.

Sometimes the bridge over the canyon fails and you go tumbling down. You do not wake up back on the road. You still have to get up, however slowly, and march out. Maybe you crawl for part of it, or somebody walks beside you, or the first day of progress looks almost exactly like lying still, except that you have stopped digging. If you never get up, you become a memory of yesterday while you are still alive.

I spent much of my life taking pain in, mixing it with my own and throwing it back into the room in worse shape.

I do not want martyrdom to become another escape. Nobody should have to absorb the whole world's pain until they disappear. The gift has to last longer than sacrifice does. It can be a thought, a tool, a structure, or a place where the next person does not have to carry the same weight alone.

So, to younger people and to anybody else who recognizes part of themselves here: do not take me as an example of what to do. I repeated mistakes that people before me made. I made the same excuses, and then I found new language and better tools for making them.

I took what I received from family, peers, relationships, music, institutions, biology and every room that taught me how to survive. I internalized it, distorted it and sometimes handed it to somebody else.

My behavior reflects that environment. It does not fully reflect my intentions or the world I believe should exist. It is still my behavior.

Environment explains a route. I still have to walk the route myself.

Environment is the medium. It shapes a person, an animal, a model, an idea, a piece of art, a song, a wound and a criticism. It feeds some tendencies and starves others. That is the reason transformation is possible, and it acquits nobody.

Change the conditions, keep the record, make another choice, and something else may grow.

Until a life ends, another move remains. Even after a life ends, its impact keeps moving through other people. A person may cause damage and still leave behind a lesson that stops somebody else from repeating it. The damage does not become good, and the record can still serve life.

I believe in people who cannot currently believe in themselves. I am trying to extend the same belief to myself without turning it into permission.

I want to build for the people who endure, for the people we lost, and for the people who may not know whether they can endure what comes next. Not as a monument to suffering. As a handrail.

A handrail is a place to put weight down, a structure that does not need everybody to be strong at the same time.

Maybe I will fail the conviction, contradict it or leave somebody else to carry it farther than I can. The structure should survive that. It should survive me.

The clock keeps moving

Now I want to ask you something more uncomfortable than whether you agree with me.

Are you preparing for the coming world, or defending the last arrangement in which your identity still made sense?

The clock keeps ticking. The world keeps turning. Neither one pauses while we negotiate with the version of ourselves we expected to become.

This is no argument for surrender. Preparing does not mean accepting every machine, market, political program, new norm or company that calls extraction inevitable. Refusal and preservation can both be preparation. A human-only space, a local tool, a union, a family boundary, a language class, a repaired ecosystem or a decision not to automate something can each be an active design for the future.

Clinging is different: it protects an arrangement because that arrangement is the last surface that still reflects a self you recognize.

Clinging can turn a profession into proof that your years mattered and a family role into proof that you are needed. A diagnosis can become the only account that makes your pain readable, and a grievance the last reliable evidence that what happened was real. A political identity can become the room where every contradiction gets translated into betrayal. Expertise can harden into a demand that the world stop changing at the exact point where you learned to find your way in it, and purity into a way to stay innocent while other people carry the cost.

Which part are you protecting: your humanity, your livelihood, your craft, your community's right to continue, or the last semblance of a self that knew where it stood because somebody else was still outside the gate? Those are different defenses.

The coming world will rearrange work, art, intimacy, authority, knowledge, climate, institutions and the stories people tell about what a person is for. Some of that change should be resisted, some redirected and some welcomed, and much of it will arrive mixed, with liberation and extraction inside the same product, the same policy or the same room. A simple position will not survive contact with it, and neither will a self built entirely from being for the machine or against it.

What do you refuse because it violates a principle, and what because it threatens a role? What do you accept because it expands what people can do, and what because it flatters your importance? Decide what should stay human-only and what should become available to everybody. Then ask who owns the transition, and who is being told to adapt while somebody else collects the gain.

Preparing means deciding what must be protected and what must be allowed to change, and learning before the emergency which values hold weight and which customs only inherited the look of a foundation. It means building exits before the person inside the cage needs a rescuer. It also means refusing to preserve the cage because it is the last structure that still knows your name, and refusing to worship the machine because it offers you a new name with no history.

I know what it is to cling to an identity built around injury. The unheard child becomes the person who must always be heard, and the outsider becomes the person whose exclusion proves his insight. The person who missed the official road comes to need that road to be fraudulent in every case; the critic comes to depend on the system staying bad enough to keep the criticism at the center.

A wound can become a job, the job can become a self, and that self can start defending the conditions that keep the job necessary. The clock continues anyway.

You do not have to abandon the person you were. You may have to stop requiring the future to preserve every condition that made that person recognizable.

You cannot keep your identity unchanged until death. The question is whether you can change without making everybody around you pay for the grief of changing.

Can you let go of a role before it becomes a prison? Can you preserve a craft without turning mastery into hereditary authority, or protect a community without needing an enemy to hold it together? Use a tool without letting the tool choose the world, or refuse one without asking other people to stay powerless so that your refusal keeps its meaning? And can you meet a future child without demanding that they inherit the exact conditions under which you learned to matter?

The world keeps turning, and the next layer is already settling on top of this one.

What remains

AI is here. Hypocrisy was already here. Trauma is here too, and it is neither the oldest thing in us nor the last. Before the alarm there was a capacity to learn. Beside the wound there can still be curiosity, play, relation, music, humor and the strange animal joy of two brontosauruses chasing each other across a hill.

You are here too, for now. We can keep arguing about which contamination is pure enough to condemn the others. Or we can examine the structures that surround all of them.

Who owns the vehicle? Who chooses the direction? Who receives the gain? Who carries the exhaust? Who can inspect the route, object to the destination and get out before the door locks?

Let go of the ethics that ask people to die proving they were pure. Keep the ethics that protect agency, consent, livelihood, relationship, accountability, ancestry, children and the earth.

Stop mistaking a route for a destination, suffering for value, legality for justice, difficulty for meaning, inheritance for destiny and the robe for morality.

Then aim the machinery somewhere useful: toward discovery, local capability, workers who share the gain, art with an honest lineage and science that keeps its receipts. Aim it toward communities that can refuse, families where a child can ask what the money means, adults who can hear a question without shrinking the person who asked it, ancestors remembered in full and recruited for neither the prosecution nor the defense, forgiveness that releases the future without rewriting the past, children who inherit our stories without inheriting every alarm, and an earth still alive enough to receive whatever future we keep bragging about.

Then picture the record without you standing beside it.

One day there may be layers of dirt, mud, stone, roots, roads, ruins, oceans, cities or materials no language has named yet, lying above whatever remains of the places where we lived. Most of us will leave no fossil anyone could recognize. The matter that became our bodies for a while will enter other arrangements.

The matter changes custody.

The impact does not wait for a body to stay intact. A future person may live inside an institution we strengthened, or escape through a door we did not lock; drink water we protected, or pay to repair what we treated as somebody else's problem; inherit a family rule we interrupted, or spend another generation mistaking it for character. They may use a tool whose author is forgotten and carry a fear nobody remembers teaching.

We will not be there to dress up the result, explain what we meant, bask in the monument, defend the omission, revise the caption, or tell the person living with the consequence that our intentions were more beautiful than the effect. The next person receives the trace.

Will you be leaving behind something you would have wanted someone to leave behind for you?

It could be a door in place of a doctrine, a handrail in place of a pedestal, a source in place of a rumor, a repaired relationship in place of a story about why repair was impossible, or a tool that can be inspected in place of a dependency disguised as freedom. It could also be a child who knows that love does not require becoming a stump, a culture that can transform without pretending it had no ancestry, an institution that can be contradicted, or an earth we do not treat as the disposable ground of our self-importance.

You will be neither the beginning of what follows nor its end. You are here for a narrow stretch of the record, long enough to receive conditions you did not choose and to change some of the conditions another person will receive, which is smaller than immortality and larger than nothing.

I do not know how to solve the human condition. I do not know much at the scale where people start pretending to have final answers. I am trying to stop turning that uncertainty into paralysis one day and authority the next.

I know the cage was built, and built things can be taken apart. I know a black box can be opened without turning a person into glass, and that a theorem can meet a kernel while a poem stays free. I know a confession is not a receipt. It can still be the first honest entry in a record that has been lying for a long time. Forgiveness deletes nothing; it is one way the record stops being a prison.

I know the Mad-Happy Scientist is still in there: tired, impatient, angry and curious, holding a book in one hand and trying to become an atlas with the other. The child at the park still opens his mouth, and this time words come out.

I am lost. I hurt people while trying to escape being lost.

I come from people who were lost, taken, abandoned, left by mothers, passed over by fathers, intoxicated, imprisoned and frightened. They were made to survive worlds that often lied about what survival should cost.

I forgive them where I can, and I forgive myself enough to continue the work. I am not asking the people I hurt for forgiveness; I am asking myself to become someone who can receive their answer.

The Earth Fair in the dream was closed. Behind the door was a factory, and the factory had taught its machines to chase the child who arrived expecting wonder.

I cannot go back into that dream and make the old fair stay. I can refuse to build another factory behind the door, help make a clearing, put down a handrail, leave the ledger open and build a surface where another person gets traction.

So get out of the mud, without pretending the mud is imaginary and without climbing onto somebody else's back. Get out, turn around and put something down for the next person. Leave the kind of trace you would have been grateful to find.

Pick the lock for everyone. Throw away the key. Let the walls rot into soil. Open the fair. Make room for something our ancestors could not protect, our parents could not name, we are only beginning to practice, and our children should not have to dream to find.

It had read the GitHub repositories I connected to it during the preceding revision process. The files on my own computer were out of its reach, and this revision does not treat them as read.

In September 2026 I had the wording revised again for plain language with Claude Opus 5.5, an AI system, and checked with Articulate, my writing checker. That pass rewrote sentences, added plain definitions of technical terms and linked sources. I asked it to keep every claim, source, date and position as it was.

My readings of Silverstein, Prelutsky, Seuss, Cleary, Eastman, Strayhorn, Funkadelic, drum 'n' bass, Pink Floyd, Orwell and Gibran are interpretive. They describe what the works opened in me and make no claim to one final meaning for any work. I kept the quotations from Where the Sidewalk Ends and Maggot Brain short on purpose. I use an artist's biography to clarify questions of voice, credit, lineage, constraint and survival, never to reduce the art to biography or to recruit the artist as proof of my argument.

I deliberately framed the family accounts as memory and inherited story. They do not claim to be a complete public verdict on any relative. The account of the prison-gang affiliation gives context without treating the affiliation or its symbolism as harmless, and it does not demand forgiveness from anyone affected by it. The discussion of racial organization in California prisons describes a documented institutional history. It does not establish every condition my father personally experienced. The Klamath Agency Boarding School appears as part of the documented policy history in Klamath County. The essay does not claim that my stepfather's father attended that exact school. That claim would need confirming evidence.

The discussion of formerly enslaved people searching for separated relatives rests on the Last Seen archive, which the National Archives supports. I do not use those notices as a metaphor for my family, and the essay explicitly refuses to equate a children's book, a private family fracture and the history of slavery. The connection stays limited to recognition, name, return, kinship, and the documented ways power can attack them.

The account of sexual abuse is limited to my own experience and does not identify the other person. The essay separates explanation from excuse and forgiveness from exoneration, and it keeps unequal forms of harm distinct from one another.

The epigenetics section keeps its language narrow. Epigenetics studies chemical marks that change how genes are used without changing the genes themselves. Research supports several pathways by which effects pass between generations: prenatal conditions, parenting, culture, stress physiology and shared environments. Animal studies give stronger evidence than human studies that such marks pass through eggs and sperm. Claims that effects pass to human generations that were never directly exposed remain hard to establish, because genetic, prenatal, ecological and cultural inheritance are hard to separate. So the essay does not claim that ancestral memories are encoded in DNA.

The neurobiology section treats trauma and PTSD as variable and dependent on the stage of development at which they occur. It does not treat them as a single brain signature. Plasticity, the brain's ability to change, appears as a capacity for continued adaptation. It is no promise that every consequence reverses. The discussion of fear extinction rests on evidence that new learning about safety can inhibit or weaken an old fear while relapse stays possible. Adverse childhood experiences appear as a public-health framework. They are neither a fixed score that decides a life nor a substitute for an individual history.

The geological section uses established concepts: superposition, unconformity, lithification, metamorphism, fault displacement, body fossils and trace fossils. The metaphors have limits: people are not rocks, pressure ennobles nobody, and death does not guarantee a fossil. The claim is that material records can preserve effects, gaps and transformations after intention is no longer there to explain them. The comparison between people and models has limits too. Flywheel treats its harness, the software a model works inside, as a developmental environment: experience that passes a check becomes material for later improvement. That gives an engineering analogy for conditioning and feedback. It does not make models equivalent to embodied people, attribute trauma to models, or reduce human forgiveness and moral responsibility to optimization.
