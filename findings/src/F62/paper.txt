# abstract
Bright, saturated and warm pictures are often said to stick in memory. We tested how much of that survives once the subject of the photograph is held fixed. The data are the 9,988 colour photographs of the public MemCat set, each with a memorability score from a repeat-detection game and a label for one of 134 subject subcategories. Subcategory alone predicts held-out memorability with an R2 of 0.558. {c1}Adding lightness, chroma and colour temperature lifts held-out R2 by 0.0022 (95% interval 0.0009 to 0.0034), from 0.558 to 0.560; the content-matched shuffle reaches 0.0001 at its 95th percentile.{/c1} {c2}Within a subcategory each property correlates weakly with memorability: lightness +0.027, chroma +0.041 and log colour temperature -0.056, each above a shuffle bar near 0.019.{/c2} {c3}Colour alone, with no subject label, predicts memorability at R2 0.096, about 44 times the gain it adds once subcategory is known.{/c3} The effect is real under our controls and small. A subcategory is a coarse label, so the remaining link may still stand for scene content such as snow or sky. This page reports one of the study's four tests; the other three are left out for the reasons given in the design section.

# section What this means for a reader {sec:reader}
In this image set, most of what looks like a link between colour and memorability is a link between subject and memorability: food and animals are remembered better than landscapes, and they also differ in colour. Once the subject is fixed, colour explains well under one percent of the remaining differences. A claim that a warmer or more saturated image is more memorable should be read with the subject controlled, and with the size of the effect beside its p value.

# section Introduction {sec:intro}
{g:memorability}Memorability{/g} is the chance that a viewer who has seen a photograph once recognises it when it comes round again. Isola and colleagues showed that this chance is stable across viewers and so belongs partly to the image {cite:isola2014}. Papers that cite that work report its colour result as weak: mean hue, saturation and similar simple features predict memorability poorly. We read that through citing papers and a search summary; the paper's own tables were not read.

Goetschalckx and Wagemans built MemCat to study memorability inside subject categories: 10,000 photographs in five broad categories, each split into subcategories, with a memorability score for every image {cite:memcat2019}. That structure allows a question the earlier sets answer less directly. Hold the subject fixed at the subcategory level, and ask what colour still adds.

The decision this informed was whether a tool of ours that tunes images should carry a colour control aimed at memorability. Our searches returned the MemCat paper and the weak colour result of Isola and colleagues, and no measurement of the within-subcategory colour gain on MemCat. That is what two searches returned; it does not show that none exists.

# section Preregistration and design {sec:prereg}
The {g:preregistration}preregistration{/g} was written and hash-locked on 29 October 2026, before any statistic of the study was computed. Before the lock we had seen the layout of the data files and the mean and standard deviation of MemCat memorability (0.693 and 0.137). No colour property had been correlated with anything.

The study held four tests. This paper reports the third, colour and memorability on MemCat, and leaves out the other three:
- The first test compared the colour of one person's saved images with seasonal day length. It rests on a private image collection, and its planted check fell just short of its own bar, so it is not published.
- The second test, colour and mood, was recorded as untestable before the run: no mood record overlapping the images exists.
- The fourth test reused an image index and word ratings from an earlier study of ours that are not a public dataset.

The memorability test carried five {g:verdict}verdict{/g}s, each a rule fixed before the run and read off by code: a sanity verdict, a gain verdict and one verdict per colour property. The rules are in Appendix A. The multiple-test correction was fixed over the eight primary p values of the whole study, four of which belong to this test.

# section Data and provenance {sec:data}
MemCat {cite:memcat2019} holds 10,000 photographs in five categories (animal, food, landscape, sports, vehicle) and 134 subcategories. Its memorability score is the hit rate in a repeat-detection game corrected for false alarms, from about 99 viewers per image. The files come from the Open Science Framework node kvghu. The node carries a CC BY 4.0 licence, and its README restricts use to academic research and to the terms of the source image sets. We report statistics only and redistribute no image.

The images were read from the archive as data. We dropped 12 images that looked greyscale (mean absolute difference between the red and blue channels under 2), which left 9,988.

# section Methods {sec:methods}
## Colour properties
Each image was resized so its longest side is 256 pixels and converted to the OKLab colour space. Three properties were fixed in advance. {g:oklab-lightness}Lightness{/g} is the mean OKLab L over the frame. {g:chroma}Chroma{/g} is the mean OKLab chroma. The third is the natural logarithm of the {g:cct}correlated colour temperature{/g} of the image's mean colour, by McCamy's approximation, clipped to the range 1,500 to 25,000 kelvin. A higher value means a cooler, bluer image.

## The gain
Content is the subcategory, coded as 134 indicator variables. The baseline model predicts memorability from subcategory alone. The full model adds the three colour properties, standardised, as linear terms. Both were scored by {g:held-out-r2}held-out R2{/g} over 10 folds stratified by subcategory. The primary statistic is the gain
$$ G = R^2_{full} - R^2_{content} {eq:gain}
with both terms computed on held-out images.

## Within-subcategory correlation
For each property we removed the subcategory mean from the property and from memorability and took the Pearson correlation of the two residuals,
$$ r_w = corr(m - m_s, c - c_s) {eq:rw}
where {m:m} is memorability, {m:c} the property and the subscript {m:s} marks the subcategory mean.

## Nulls, intervals and correction
The {g:control}control{/g} for the gain permutes the colour rows among images of the same subcategory, 1,000 times, and refits on the same folds. The control for each correlation permutes the property within subcategory, 1,000 times, and compares absolute values. Each p value is a {g:p-value-draws}p value from draws{/g},
$$ p = \frac{k + 1}{n + 1} {eq:p}
with {m:k} the number of draws at or above the real value and {m:n} the number of draws. With 1,000 draws the smallest possible value is 0.001. Each {g:interval-95}95% interval{/g} comes from 2,000 bootstrap resamples over images. The p values were corrected with the {g:benjamini-hochberg}Benjamini-Hochberg correction{/g} {cite:bh1995} over the study's eight primary tests.

# section Controls and planted checks {sec:controls}
The sanity control shuffles memorability scores across all images and refits the full model, 1,000 times. Its 95th percentile is an R2 of -0.012, so a model with no real signal scores below zero on held-out images, and the real content-only R2 of 0.558 is far above it.

The content-matched shuffle is the control that matters for the claim. It keeps each image's subject and score and gives it the colour of another image of the same subcategory. Its gains have a mean of -0.0002 and a 95th percentile of +0.0001. Any gain the real colours show beyond that comes from colour varying with memorability inside subcategories.

This test had no {g:planted-check}planted check{/g}: no known colour effect was inserted to measure what size the method can see. The shuffle bars stand in for it. A within-subcategory correlation has to exceed about 0.019 to clear its shuffle, so effects smaller than that are not excluded.

# section Discussion {sec:discussion}
The result agrees in direction with the weak colour link reported for earlier image sets and puts a size on it for MemCat with the subject controlled. A lift of 0.0022 in R2 against a baseline of 0.558 is a detectable effect of no practical use for predicting which photograph will be remembered. For the decision the study was run to inform, the answer was no: a colour control aimed at memorability is not worth building on this evidence.

The contrast between the two columns of Table {ref:tab:props} is the more useful part. A reader who correlated colour temperature with memorability across the whole set would find -0.307 and might call warmth memorable. Fixing the subcategory cuts that to under a fifth of its size. The same care applies to any image property that differs between subjects.

The remaining link should not be read as colour acting on memory. A subcategory such as a kind of landscape still mixes scenes, and a snowy scene differs from a green one in both colour and content. The food category runs opposite to the others, which fits a reading in which the property stands for what is in the picture.

# section Limitations {sec:limits}
- One image set and one memory task. Memorability here is recognition in a repeat-detection game over minutes, and it may not carry to memory in daily life or to other image sets.
- Subcategory is coarse content. Colour inside a subcategory can still stand for scene content, so the within-subcategory link is an upper bound on what colour itself contributes.
- The models are linear in three whole-frame averages. Colour layout, contrast between regions and nonlinear effects were not tested.
- The test had no planted check, so the smallest effect the method could detect is known only from the shuffle bars.
- The per-category values and the six-property gain are secondary and have no intervals or corrected p values.
- The correction was made over eight tests, four of them in parts of the study not published here.
- Three of the study's four tests are left out: one rests on private images, one was untestable, and one rests on data that is not public.
- Scoring was by our own code and was not blind. The check with the other two properties held fixed was made after the results were seen.
