# abstract
Microbes that release costly products into their surroundings are exposed to cheaters that take the benefit and skip the cost. A standard result in social evolution says that when relatives stay near each other, producers can persist anyway. We tried to reproduce that on a simple grid model before using the idea in our own writing, and in the second of three preregistered rounds the model seemed to succeed, with a threshold that matched a rule from the theory literature. It had a flaw: each producer received a share of its own goods. {c1}The shuffled control, which should have collapsed, kept producers at a mean share of 0.37 (95th percentile 0.52), and with a 3 by 3 goods box a producer recovers a ninth of its own output, so producing paid for itself whenever the benefit-to-cost ratio passed 9.{/c1} The third round paid goods to others only. {c2}With goods paid to others only, local producers ended at a share of 0.03 at a benefit-to-cost ratio of 100, 0.19 at 200 and 0.48 at 400, and never reached the preregistered bar of 0.5.{/c2} Under this update rule, space alone gave producers no foothold at any benefit-to-cost ratio up to 50, where their share was 0.00.

# section What this means for a reader {sec:reader}
A simulation can match a known answer for the wrong reason. Ours produced a clean threshold for when cooperation survives, close to a number from the theory literature, and we credited the theory. The match came from a coding choice that let each cooperator keep part of its own contribution, which turns cooperation into private investment. If you build or read individual-based models of microbes, markets or teams, check whether the "cooperators" are paid by their own act.

# section Introduction {sec:intro}
Many bacteria release a {g:public-good}public good{/g}, a product that costs its maker something and helps others nearby. Iron-scavenging siderophores are a studied case. In a population where everyone meets everyone, a {g:producer-cheater}cheater{/g} that makes none and takes the benefit should win. Kin selection gives one way out: if producers mostly help relatives, which are also producers, production can spread.

Space is the usual route to relatedness in microbes, because daughter cells stay near their parents. Nadell, Drescher and Foster {cite:nadell2016} review how the spatial arrangement of clones and species in biofilms shapes cooperation and competition. Space also has a cost. Taylor {cite:taylor1992} showed with an inclusive fitness model that in a patch-structured population the benefit of helping relatives and the cost of {g:local-competition}local competition{/g} among them can cancel exactly, the same result Wilson, Pollock and Dugatkin {cite:wilson1992} reached by simulation. Griffin, West and Buckling {cite:griffin2004} tested it in the bacterium Pseudomonas aeruginosa by setting relatedness and the scale of competition independently, and found that cooperation rose with relatedness and fell when competition was more local.

On graphs, Ohtsuki, Hauert, Lieberman and Nowak {cite:ohtsuki2006} found that selection favours cooperation when the benefit-to-cost ratio exceeds the average number of neighbours. We used that rule in our second round to forecast a threshold, and the forecast came true for a reason the rule had nothing to do with.

# section Preregistration and design {sec:prereg}
The study ran in three rounds on 31 October 2026, each under its own {g:preregistration}preregistration{/g}, hash-locked before that round ran and written after reading the round before. The decision they inform was stated in the third: whether our own writing may say that space protects cooperators on the strength of our own model, or must only cite published work for it.

- Round one asked whether producers persist under local rules at {m:b} of 2 and {m:c} of 0.3 and collapse when mixed or shuffled. Its {g:kill-condition}kill conditions{/g}: if the mixed arm does not collapse or its early decline misses a closed form, the model is wrong; if the shuffled arm keeps producers, space was not the cause.
- Round two, after round one missed, swept the {g:bc-ratio}benefit-to-cost ratio{/g} from 5 to 16 at {m:b} of 1, 2 and 4 and forecast that persistence would begin between ratios of 7 and 12. Its kill conditions: a mean-field error above 0.03, or a shuffled arm that keeps producers.
- Round three, after reading round two's control, ran goods to others only beside the old version, at ratios from 2 to 400. It had no kill condition: it said in advance that whatever it gave would be the report.

The SHA-256 of each preregistration is in the reproduction section.

# section Data and provenance {sec:data}
No external data were used. The model and all its numbers are our own and invented for the check. It describes no organism. Every number in this paper is read from the three rounds' result files.

# section Methods {sec:methods}
## The model
A {g:torus-grid}torus grid{/g} of 100 by 100 cells holds producers and cheaters, starting half and half at random. Let {m:z} be 1 for a producer and 0 for a cheater. A cell's fitness is
$$ W = 1 + b \phi - c z {eq:fit}
floored at a small positive value, where {m:\phi} is the share of producers in the cell's {g:goods-box}goods box{/g}. Each generation is a {g:synchronous}synchronous update{/g} by {g:fitness-proportional}fitness-proportional replacement{/g}: every cell's next occupant is copied from a parent drawn from the 3 by 3 window around the cell, the cell included, with chance in proportion to fitness.

In rounds one and two the producer share counts the cell itself:
$$ \phi = \frac{n}{N} {eq:self}
where {m:n} is the number of producers in the box, the cell included, and {m:N} the number of cells in the box, 9 for a 3 by 3 box. In round three, goods reach others only:
$$ \phi = \frac{n - z}{N - 1} {eq:others}

## Arms
The local arm uses the rules above. The {g:mixed-arm}mixed arm{/g} sets {m:\phi} to the producer share of the whole grid and draws parents from the whole grid. The {g:shuffled-arm}shuffled arm{/g} shuffles all positions before each generation and then applies the local rules, so neighbours are strangers.

## The flaw
Under eq. {ref:eq:self} a producer is one of the {m:N} cells its own goods reach, so it receives {m:b/N} of its own output. Producing then pays the producer directly whenever
$$ \frac{b}{N} > c {eq:flaw}
that is, whenever {m:b/c} exceeds {m:N}, which is 9 for a 3 by 3 box. That is a private return. It needs no relatives nearby, so it survives in the shuffled arm too. In round three it did so from a ratio of 20 (a share of 0.37) and fully from 30; at ratios of 12 and 16 the shuffled producers ended at 0.00 while local producers ended at 1.00, so in that band clustering still mattered.

## Measures
The {g:final-share}final producer share{/g} is the share of producers at the end of a run, averaged over replicate runs; a run stops early when one type fills the grid. A ratio counts as persistence when that average reaches 0.5. Round one ran 1,500 generations and 8 replicates; round two 1,200 generations and 6 replicates; round three 1,000 generations and 5 replicates.

# section Controls and planted checks {sec:controls}
The shuffled arm is the {g:control}control{/g} on which the study turns. If space is what keeps producers alive, removing space must make them collapse. Rounds two and three ran it 20 times at the most favourable setting.

Round two added a known answer for the mixed arm. Producers have fitness {m:1 + bp - c} and cheaters {m:1 + bp}, where {m:p} is the producer share, so the {g:mean-field}mean-field recursion{/g} is
$$ p_{t+1} = \frac{p_t (1 + b p_t - c)}{1 + b p_t - c p_t} {eq:rec}
The simulated mixed arm, on a 300 by 300 grid averaged over 8 runs, had to match it within 0.03 over the first 20 generations. It did, with a maximum error of 0.0019.

Round one's known answer for the mixed arm was a closed-form slope for the early decline of the producer share on the log-odds scale. It failed, with an error of 0.117 against a bar of 0.05. The closed form was our mistake: it held the producer share fixed at one half while the share fell. Round two replaced it with eq. {ref:eq:rec}.

# section Results {sec:results}
## Round one
At {m:b} of 2 and {m:c} of 0.3, a ratio of 6.7, local producers collapsed in every run, against our forecast of a final share of 0.6. The mixed and shuffled arms collapsed too. The cost sweep at {m:b} of 2 kept producers at {m:c} of 0.1 and 0.2, ratios of 20 and 10, and lost them from 0.3 up. Read with eq. {ref:eq:flaw}, that pattern already fits the flaw.

## Round two
Figure {ref:fig:round2} shows the threshold. Local producers first reached a final share of 0.5 at a ratio of 9 for every value of {m:b}: 0.52 at {m:b} of 1, 0.66 at 2 and 0.88 at 4. We had forecast 7 to 12 from the rule that cooperation wins when the ratio exceeds the number of neighbours, 8 here, and counted it a pass.

The control did not hold. {c1}The shuffled control, which should have collapsed, kept producers at a mean share of 0.37 (95th percentile 0.52), and with a 3 by 3 goods box a producer recovers a ninth of its own output, so producing paid for itself whenever the benefit-to-cost ratio passed 9.{/c1} The control ran at a ratio of 20 with 20 draws; its 5th percentile was 0.26. The same round's goods-range sweep at a ratio of 20 kept producers at range 1 and lost them at range 2, where the box holds 25 cells and the private return {m:b/25} falls below {m:c}. The threshold was the size of the goods box.

# section Discussion {sec:discussion}
The second round looked like a textbook reproduction, and the shuffled control is what showed it was not one. Scrambling positions every generation removes relatives from a producer's neighbourhood, so any persistence that survives scrambling cannot come from kin. Producers kept a mean share of 0.37 in the scrambled grid. Reading that number led to eq. {ref:eq:flaw}: the threshold of 9 was the size of the goods box, and the match with the neighbour count of 8 in the rule of Ohtsuki and colleagues {cite:ohtsuki2006} was a coincidence. Their rule concerns a game played between pairs of neighbours on a graph. Ours shares a public good over a box, a different model, so the match was never a test of their rule.

With the private return removed, local producers did not persist anywhere up to a ratio of 400, and widening the goods box made them worse off. This fits the result of Taylor {cite:taylor1992} and of Griffin and colleagues {cite:griffin2004}: when relatives compete for the same space, the competition can cancel the benefit of living near them. In our model the parent window and the goods box overlap, so a producer's goods feed the same neighbours that compete for its offspring's cells. The model does not show that space never protects cooperators. It shows that this model, with this update rule, gives them no protection at benefit-to-cost ratios up to 50.

The claim that space protects cooperators in microbes therefore stays a citation of published work, such as the review of Nadell and colleagues {cite:nadell2016} and the experiment of Griffin and colleagues {cite:griffin2004}. It is not a result of ours.

# section Limitations {sec:limits}
- One update rule, fitness-proportional and synchronous, with invented numbers. Other update rules, asynchronous timing, other neighbourhood sizes or a parent window separate from the goods box could let producers persist.
- The model describes no organism and says nothing about any real microbe.
- The ratio grid is coarse. Round three's threshold with own goods lies somewhere between ratios of 5 and 9, and round two's between 8 and 9.
- Few replicates: 5 per setting in round three. The others-only local share of 0.48 at 400 sits close to the 0.5 bar, and another 5 runs could fall on either side.
- The final share averages runs that mostly end at 0 or 1, so it tracks the share of runs won by producers more than any mixed state.
- The runs were scored by code and checked by the same analyst who wrote them, with no outside review.

# section Corrections {sec:corrections}
- 31 October 2026: round two was first read as reproducing the kin-and-space result, with a threshold credited to the neighbour rule of Ohtsuki and colleagues. The threshold came from producers receiving their own goods. Round three tested this and the reading was withdrawn.
- 31 October 2026: round one's closed-form slope check failed because the formula held the producer share fixed. Round two replaced it with the exact recursion.
- 9 November 2026: our earlier write-up said local producers in round two "persisted in the majority of runs" at a ratio of 9. The rule was a mean final share of at least 0.5, and this paper says so. It also did not report that round three's space verdict was recorded as untestable; this paper does.
- 9 November 2026: first published version.
- 14 November 2026: A review of the published page found wording that the round-three result file narrows. The page said the private return survives in the shuffled arm above a ratio of 9; in the file shuffled producers with their own goods ended at 0.00 at ratios of 12 and 16, 0.37 at 20 and 1.00 from 30, while local producers ended at 1.00 from 12. The methods and the forecast table now give those values, and one chart caption now says space is not the only thing that keeps producers alive. The phrase sensible cost is replaced by the ratios it stood for.

# section Reproduction {sec:repro}
- Build a 100 by 100 torus of producers and cheaters, half and half at random.
- Give each cell the fitness of eq. {ref:eq:fit} with the producer share of eq. {ref:eq:others}, so a producer gains nothing from its own goods. Replace every cell each generation by a parent drawn from its 3 by 3 window in proportion to fitness.
- Run 1,000 generations, 5 replicates per setting, at {m:b} of 2 and ratios from 2 to 400, with the local, shuffled and mixed arms, and repeat with eq. {ref:eq:self} to see the artefact.
- Check the mixed arm against eq. {ref:eq:rec}.
