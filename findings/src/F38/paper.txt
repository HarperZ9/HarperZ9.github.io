# abstract
Most statistics for a series of measurements assume the process behind it holds still. We built tests for three ways it can move (a step, a slow drift, a switch between two states), checked each on synthetic series with known answers, and ran them on crowd forecasts and on mood reported several times a day. Twenty predictions were fixed in advance. {c1}The ledger scores 9 of the 20 as held and 11 as not held, and one of the 9 held only because its rule could never fire.{/c1} {c2}In 238 forecast questions the drift test fired on 97 percent and 96 percent ended nearer the answer than they began, by a median 0.24 in probability, which is what a crowd converging on an outcome is expected to do.{/c2} {c3}On the two slider datasets the change-point test fired in 22 and 40 percent of people and the drift test in 26 and 43 percent, against about 5 percent per test for a stationary AR(1) series.{/c3} {c4}On the 1 to 7 and 1 to 5 scales the raw change-point rates of 47 and 70 percent fell to 18 and 12 percent once ties were broken, so most of those detections came from the rating scale.{/c4} {c5}The switching test used 19 surrogates, so its smallest p value was 0.05 and the rule "p below 0.05" could never fire; no claim about switching is made.{/c5} {c6}Simulated people whose mood relaxes in 5, 15 or 30 minutes, sampled five times a day for 20 days with no measurement noise, were fitted at the sampling floor or with a noise share of 0.8 or more in 89, 98 and 89 percent of 100 runs, against a preregistered bar of 90 percent.{/c6}

# section What this means for a reader {sec:reader}
A crowd forecast moves steadily toward the right answer as the question nears its end, so its average over the whole period describes no moment of it. Mood reported on a phone is steadier, but in a large minority of people it steps or drifts over the weeks of a study by more than their own hour-to-hour carryover explains. A study that prompts five times a day also cannot see feelings that rise and fade within the hour: they show up as noise. More than half of what we predicted about these data was wrong, and one of our tests was built so that it could not give a positive answer, which we report as a defect.

# section Introduction {sec:intro}
A series is {g:stationary}stationary{/g} when its mean and its autocovariance do not change over time, a term from time-series analysis that excludes trends, level shifts and drifting variance. Measures such as an autocorrelation or a spectrum summarise a whole record in a few numbers, and those numbers describe the process only if it is stationary. A recent primer on non-stationarity in psychological time series {cite:ryan2025} warns, in the summary we read, that ignoring a trend biases estimates and distorts their sampling distributions.

Mood research meets the problem in {g:esm}experience sampling{/g}, a design in which people answer short questions on a phone several times a day. Such data are sparse and unevenly spaced, and how spacing biases models fitted to them is a studied question {cite:dehaan2017}. A simulation study found that popular time-series tools lose the dynamics of a toy emotion model once measurements are as sparse as experience sampling, and its authors suspected a mismatch between prompts hours apart and processes that unfold over seconds to minutes {cite:recover2021}. A Monte Carlo study of continuous-time autoregressive models found estimates degraded when the process was observed more slowly than it ran {cite:ctsampling2023}.

We asked three things. Which of the processes we hold data on are stationary, by tests that can tell a step from a drift? How widely do people differ in the time their mood takes to relax? And what is the fastest mood process a five-a-day schedule can measure? The decision this informed was which of our own stationary measures may be applied to which data. Our searches returned the work above and no published share of experience-sampling participants whose series fail a stationarity test; that is what two searches returned and no proof that none exists.

# section Methods {sec:methods}
## The three tests
Each test compares a series with {g:surrogate}surrogates{/g} of itself: simulated series from an {g:ar1}AR(1) process{/g} fitted to it, in which each value is the mean plus a fraction {m:\phi} of the previous value's distance from the mean plus Gaussian noise {m:e_t},
$$ x_t = \mu + \phi (x_{t-1} - \mu) + e_t {eq:ar1}
with the same length, mean, standard deviation and {m:\phi} as the real series. A surrogate keeps the memory between neighbouring values and has no step, drift or switching. For a statistic that is {m:k} times matched or beaten among {m:N} surrogates,
$$ p = \frac{1 + k}{N + 1} {eq:p}
so the smallest possible value is {m:1/(N+1)}.
- {g:change-point}Change-point{/g} test: the number of changes in mean or variance found by a penalised search (PELT, penalty {m:3 \ln n}, segments of 5 or more), against 99 surrogates.
- {g:drift-test}Drift test{/g}: a model whose AR coefficient follows a random walk, {m:\phi_t = \phi_{t-1} + \epsilon_t}, fitted by a Kalman filter; the likelihood ratio against a fixed coefficient, against 49 surrogates.
- {g:switching-test}Switching test{/g}: a two-state Markov-switching AR(1) against a single AR(1), likelihood ratio, against 19 surrogates.

A series is labelled switching if the third test gives p below 0.05 with at least 3 switches, else shift if the first does, else drift if the second does, else stationary. In a stationary AR(1) world each test fires about 5 percent of the time.

## Timescales
For each person we fitted an {g:ou-time-constant}Ornstein-Uhlenbeck time constant{/g} {m:\tau} to the real prompt times. The model is a mood that relaxes toward its mean, observed with independent noise, so two prompts {m:d} hours apart correlate as
$$ r(d) = (1 - \rho) e^{-d / \tau} {eq:ou}
where {m:\rho} is the share of variance that is noise. A person is at the {g:sampling-floor}sampling floor{/g} when the fitted {m:\tau} is shorter than their median gap between prompts within a day; there the time constant is not measured.

## The aliasing simulation
{g:aliasing}Aliasing{/g}, a term from signal processing, is what happens when a process changes faster than it is sampled: the fast change is recorded as something else, here as noise. We simulated people from eq. {ref:eq:ou} with {m:\rho = 0} and true {m:\tau} from 5 minutes to 48 hours, on a schedule of 20 days with 5 prompts a day, 100 runs per value, and fitted them as real people were fitted.

# section Controls and planted checks {sec:controls}
Every measure first had to recover known values from synthetic series, which is a {g:planted-check}planted check{/g}: data built so the right answer is known. Over 40 seeds, the median error had to be below the 5th percentile of a shuffled pairing of recovered and planted values and at most half its median. In the recovery file 15 measures pass every rule. Three are recorded as failing and were not used for claims: a spectral slope on five-a-day sampling (ratio 1.04), variance shares in three bands on records under 90 days, and a fit of two timescales in one person (ratios 0.63 and 0.89).

Two {g:control}controls{/g}, estimators that should fail, passed at first. A single spectral peak passed the test for a frequency sweep (ratio 0.44) and a single AR coefficient passed the test for a drifting one (0.49), because each correlates with the endpoints. The rule was changed so that every moving case is also scored on the movement itself, where both fail at ratio 1.00. A third control showed that a switching model fitted to a shuffled series still recovers the gap between its two levels from the histogram alone; only the durations of the states need time order.

The change-point search was also run on pure noise, 200 series per setting. It reported a change in 2 to 6 percent of white-noise series, and in 83 to 100 percent of AR(1) series with {m:\phi} of 0.6 or more. That is why no real series is read from its raw change count and each is judged against AR(1) surrogates.

# section Results {sec:results}
{c1}The ledger scores 9 of the 20 as held and 11 as not held, and one of the 9 held only because its rule could never fire.{/c1}

## The defect
{c5}The switching test used 19 surrogates, so its smallest p value was 0.05 and the rule "p below 0.05" could never fire; no claim about switching is made.{/c5} The switching share is 0 in every dataset by construction, and the prediction that it would be under 15 percent held for that reason alone. An exploratory rerun with "p at most 0.05" and at least 3 switches flagged 47 to 67 percent of people in the mood datasets and 54 percent of forecast questions. Those rates cannot be read as switching: Gaussian surrogates do not copy a bounded, skewed rating scale, and the control above shows the model reads level gaps from the histogram. A surrogate that keeps each series' own distribution of values is the test this needs, and it was not run.

## Forecasts
{c2}In 238 forecast questions the drift test fired on 97 percent and 96 percent ended nearer the answer than they began, by a median 0.24 in probability, which is what a crowd converging on an outcome is expected to do.{/c2} Only 1.7 percent of questions were labelled stationary. We had predicted that steps would be the most common label, on the idea that news arrives as jumps; drift was, at 78 percent of questions against 20 percent for shift. The median question ran 126.5 days.

Three further timescale predictions did not hold. A model with two timescales beat one on each person's held-out prompts for 50 to 60 percent of people, where we had predicted at most half; people simulated with one timescale on the same prompt times picked two 46 to 51 percent of the time, so the comparison is close to a coin flip and supports no claim either way. In the 37 people with records of 60 days or more, the median share of variance arriving within a day was 0.72 (interquartile range 0.64 to 0.84) against a predicted range of 0.3 to 0.7, a narrow miss. A two-component mixture was preferred over one on that share (BIC -20.3 against -14.8), against our prediction of a continuum; a cluster of people at the floor beside a spread above it would give the same preference, and that was not tested. As predicted, 27 percent of those 37 people could not be told from their own shuffled series.

## The other series
The two animation loops met two of their four predictions: every change point repeated at the same frame in the next loop, and every feature found repeated with the loop. A mode-finding method missed one loop's period because its window of 80 frames was shorter than the 288-frame loop. The forest inventory series, 14 and 12 annual values, are too short for any of the tests; a first run labelled them drifting through a software fault on short series, which was fixed and the faulty output kept. Oregon's mortality share was 0.077 to 0.096 from 2011 to 2020 and 0.125, 0.143, 0.153 and 0.178 from 2021 to 2024. Each year measures a different panel of plots, so this is a description and no test.

# section Discussion {sec:discussion}
The forecast result is the cleanest and the least surprising. A crowd's probability for the outcome that happens rises over a question's life, slowly more often than in jumps. Any stationary summary of a forecast series, such as one autocorrelation for the whole question, averages over a process that is changing throughout.

For mood the supported statement is narrower than our write-up first made it. On continuous sliders, a quarter to two fifths of people show a level change or a drifting coefficient beyond an AR(1) with Gaussian noise, each test taken alone. A rejection means "more than AR(1) with a Gaussian marginal", which a long-memory or skewed process also produces. On coarse rating scales the change-point test mostly detected the scale. A researcher fitting one autoregressive model per person to several weeks of slider data should expect it to be wrong in form for a sizeable minority, and should not use a variance-based change-point cost on a 5-point scale.

The sampling result agrees with the simulation literature cited above {cite:recover2021,ctsampling2023}: a schedule of prompts hours apart cannot recover a process that runs in minutes. Our addition is the size for one common design. With five prompts a day, a person whose feelings turn over within the hour is indistinguishable from a person who is half noise. Measuring that person needs a continuous signal.

The defect has a general form. A surrogate count sets the smallest p value a test can return, and a threshold at or below that value makes the test silent. The count has to be chosen from the threshold.

# section Limitations {sec:limits}
- Eleven of 20 predictions did not hold, and one that held could not have failed. A further one, that the inventory series are too short to test, is a statement about series length.
- The switching test could not fire. No switching result exists, in either direction.
- The headline shares have no intervals. They rest on 113, 70, 34 and 373 people and 238 questions.
- The surrogates copy mean, variance and lag-1 memory only, with Gaussian noise. Bounded probabilities and rating scales sit outside that family.
- The tie correction and the switching rerun were exploratory and made after the results were seen.
- The stationarity tests order prompts and ignore the gaps between them, including nights.
- The aliasing result comes from simulation of one model and one schedule, and follows from sampling theory. It missed its own bar of 90 percent at two of three values.
- That forecasts move toward the answer is expected, and the series was defined as the probability of the realised outcome.
- Forest inventory years are different plot panels; those numbers are descriptive.
- Scoring was done by our own code and our own reading of the result files, and was not blind.
