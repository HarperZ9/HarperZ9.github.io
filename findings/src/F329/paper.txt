# abstract
In a private die or coin task a person sees a random outcome alone and is paid by the number they report, so a lie pays and cannot be caught. Payoff maximisation says everyone should claim the top outcome. We used the open meta-study data of Abeler, Nosenzo and Raymond: 252 first-round treatments from 58 papers with 27,039 subjects, split by paper so that 19 papers with 9,960 subjects were held out. {c1}On held-out tasks with three or more outcomes, reports read as 45 percent honest, 26 percent maximal and 29 percent partial.{/c1} Six preference models were fitted on the other papers and scored on the held-out ones. {c2}All six sit within 0.005 nats per report of each other, and no contrast against the fixed-cost model passes the false-discovery correction.{/c2} {c3}The planted check failed: on data simulated from the reputation model, the reputation model lost to the fixed-cost model by 0.0003 nats per report.{/c3} By the preregistered rule, no ranking among the models counts. {c4}Our subset's mean standardised report was 0.287 against the 0.234 published for the full data, outside the preregistered tolerance of 0.03.{/c4} The departure from strict self-interest is large; the mechanism behind it is not identified by report distributions.

# section What this means for a reader {sec:reader}
Most people given a free, safe chance to lie for money do not take all of it. Close to half report what they saw, about a quarter claim the top number and the rest shade upward partway. A model in which lying has a fixed cost, one in which people care about looking honest, one in which they follow what others do, and a plain payoff model with enough noise all reproduce those reports almost equally well. To learn which motive is at work, you need experiments designed to pull the motives apart. The distribution of reports alone is not enough.

# section Introduction {sec:intro}
Fischbacher and Föllmi-Heusi {cite:ffh2013} made the {g:reporting-task}private reporting task{/g} a standard tool. Subjects rolled a die under a cup, reported the number, and were paid by it. No single lie could be detected, and the honest distribution is known, so lying shows in the reports as a whole. They found many fully honest subjects, a smaller group who lied to the maximum, and a large group of partial liars who lied but did not claim the top payoff, which they read as a wish to keep a favourable self-image.

Gächter and Schulz {cite:gachter2016} ran the die task in 23 countries and related intrinsic honesty to a national index of the prevalence of rule violations built from corruption, tax evasion and political fraud. They found more honest reporting in subject pools from countries with fewer violations.

Abeler, Nosenzo and Raymond {cite:abeler2019} combined data from 90 studies across economics, psychology and sociology and found that people lie surprisingly little. They derived predictions that separate the leading models, tested them with new experiments, and concluded that a preference for being honest together with a preference for being seen as honest best explains the behaviour. They published their data {cite:abelerdata}.

This paper asks a narrower question of that public data. Without the designed treatments that Abeler and colleagues used to separate the models, can the plain report distributions of ordinary first-round tasks tell the models apart on papers the models have not seen? And what share of reports look honest, maximal or partial?

# section Preregistration and design {sec:prereg}
The design was written as a {g:preregistration}preregistration{/g} and hash-locked on 31 October 2026 at 22:02 UTC, before any model was fitted. Two things were seen first, and both are stated in the locked file. The publishers' site gives a mean standardised report of 0.234. Our parsing step printed pooled report distributions over all 252 treatments, held-out ones included, and printed a mean standardised report of 0.287. The forecasts of report shares are therefore not blind to the pooled shape.

The locked file names the data and its filters, the six models and two baselines, the score, the paper-level tests, the three-part reading, the {g:planted-check}planted check{/g}, a reproduction check, a {g:control}control{/g}, the forecasts and the {g:kill-condition}kill conditions{/g}. Its assumptions are listed in it: true outcomes are uniform; upward lies come from preference only and downward lies only from mistakes; one population spans countries and subject pools; only first rounds are used; and the norm model has no measured norm input. Nothing was added after the run.

Kill conditions, without discretion:
- If the planted check fails, no model comparison counts.
- If the best theory's gain over everyone-honest is not above the shuffle control's 95th percentile, the theories carry nothing beyond chance.

Reproduction check: the mean standardised report over the by-treatment set must lie within 0.03 of 0.234.

# section Data and provenance {sec:data}
The data are the merged meta-study file and the data-and-programs archive published by Abeler, Nosenzo and Raymond {cite:abelerdata} on their resources page and on figshare (files 16211150 and 16211414), downloaded on 31 October 2026. The site states that all its data are released under the CC BY licence, with credit and a link back. The files were read as data; no program in the archive was run. The publishers' site describes the full set as 429 treatments from 90 papers with over 44,000 participants in 47 countries.

A parsing step kept first-round reports from tasks with uniform true outcomes and 2, 3, 6 or 10 possible outcomes. It removed treatments coded in the data as internal lying or as having a victim, treatments that asked subjects about the report distribution, and treatments that told subjects what others reported. That left 252 treatments from 58 papers with 27,039 subjects. Papers were split by a hash of the citation, with 30 percent held out: the {g:held-out-papers}held-out papers{/g} were 19 papers with 9,960 subjects, and fitting used 17,079 subjects.

As a check on the parsing, the baseline treatment of Fischbacher and Föllmi-Heusi in our file has counts of 38, 44, 64, 76, 171 and 219 from the lowest to the top payoff. Our results summary records the top report at 36 percent, where honest reporting gives 17.

# section Methods {sec:methods}
## Models
Let a task have {m:K} outcomes, ranked by payoff, with {m:\pi_r} the rank of report {m:r} scaled from 0 for the lowest to 1 for the highest. A person who saw outcome {m:s} and reports {m:r} receives the scaled payoff times a stakes factor
$$ S = (G / 5)^{1 - \epsilon} {eq:stakes}
where {m:G} is the money at stake in the treatment in purchasing-power dollars and {m:\epsilon} is the {g:stakes-elasticity}stakes elasticity{/g}. Each person also carries a lying cost {m:c}, drawn from a lognormal distribution, {m:\ln c = \mu + \sigma z} with {m:z} standard normal, evaluated on a grid of 25 quantiles. Each person picks the report with the highest utility
$$ U = S \pi_r - c a_r - c (d_r)^{\gamma} + \rho c R_r {eq:util}
where {m:a_r} is 1 when {m:r} differs from {m:s} and 0 otherwise, {m:d_r} is the payoff distance {m:|\pi_r - \pi_s|}, and {m:R_r} is the chance that a person making report {m:r} is honest. Each model keeps some of these terms:
- Fixed cost: the first cost term only. A person either reports honestly or claims the top.
- Size cost: the second cost term only, so the cost grows with the size of the lie.
- Reputation: the fixed cost plus the value {m:\rho} on looking honest. Because {m:R_r} depends on everyone's reports, it is solved as a {g:fixed-point}fixed point{/g} by 25 damped updates from honest reporting.
- Norm: the fixed cost scaled by {m:1 - \lambda L}, where {m:L} is the share of others who lie, iterated from no lying, which finds the low-lying equilibrium.
- Full: reputation plus the size cost.
A {g:tremble}tremble{/g} {m:\tau} mixes in uniform reports, so a model's prediction for report {m:r} is {m:q_r = (1 - \tau) m_r + \tau / K}, with {m:m_r} the share of the cost grid choosing {m:r}. The payoff model has no cost and no tremble. It is a logit choice on money alone with one scale parameter {m:\beta}:
$$ q_r = \frac{e^{\beta G \pi_r}}{\sum_{j} e^{\beta G \pi_j}} {eq:logit}
Two baselines frame the models: everyone honest, which predicts uniform reports, and the {g:climatology}training-average baseline{/g}. Every model was fitted by {g:maximum-likelihood}maximum likelihood{/g} on the training papers.

## Score
Models are scored by {g:log-loss}log loss{/g} over the {m:N} held-out subjects, with {m:n_{tr}} the count of report {m:r} in treatment {m:t}:
$$ \ell = - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t} \sum_{r} n_{tr} \ln q_{tr} {eq:loss}
A model's gain is the everyone-honest loss minus its own. Each theory model is compared with the fixed-cost model by a {g:sign-permutation-paper}paper-level sign permutation test{/g}, the p values adjusted together by the {g:benjamini-hochberg}Benjamini-Hochberg correction{/g}. The reputation and full models' differences from fixed cost also get a {g:interval-95}95% interval{/g} from a {g:paper-bootstrap}paper bootstrap{/g}.

## Shares of honest, maximal and partial reports
For each task size of 3, 6 or 10, reports are pooled and given a {g:three-part-reading}three-part reading{/g}. With {m:q_1} the share reporting the lowest outcome and {m:q_K} the share reporting the top,
$$ h = K q_1 {eq:honest}
$$ m = q_K - \frac{h}{K} {eq:max}
with {m:h} capped at 1, and the partial share is {m:1 - h - m}. The three task sizes are combined weighted by subjects. Two-outcome tasks are left out, since they have no partial lie.

## Standardised report
For the reproduction check each report is mapped to {m:x_r = -1 + 2(r - 1)/(K - 1)}, and the {g:standardised-report}standardised report{/g} is averaged over the {m:T} treatments without weights:
$$ M = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t} \sum_{r} \frac{n_{tr}}{n_t} x_r {eq:std}

# section Controls and planted checks {sec:controls}
The planted check simulated report counts from the reputation model at known parameters on the same treatments, kept the same split by paper, fitted the fixed-cost, size-cost and reputation models to the simulated training papers and scored them on the simulated held-out papers. The reputation model had to beat both. {c3}The planted check failed: on data simulated from the reputation model, the reputation model lost to the fixed-cost model by 0.0003 nats per report.{/c3} It beat the size-cost model by 0.0005. The fitted reputation parameters also missed the planted ones: the log of the value on looking honest, {m:\rho}, was planted at 0.2 and fitted at -0.56. The method cannot recover a known model at this sample size, so by the kill condition no ranking of the real models counts.

The shuffle control permuted report labels within each held-out treatment, and the best model's gain over everyone-honest was recomputed 300 times to give a {g:null-distribution}null distribution{/g}. Its 95th percentile was -0.086 (mean -0.112). The real best gain of 0.072 lies far above it ({g:p-value-draws}p from draws{/g} 0.003). The theories carry information beyond chance, and this kill condition did not fire.

The reproduction check failed. {c4}Our subset's mean standardised report was 0.287 against the 0.234 published for the full data, outside the preregistered tolerance of 0.03.{/c4} The two numbers come from different sets: ours uses first rounds only and drops several designs. Our filtered subset does not reproduce the publishers' headline figure, and the 0.287 was printed before the lock, so the outcome of this check was knowable when the plan was written.

# section Discussion {sec:discussion}
The departure from strict payoffs is the part that holds. Payoff maximisation predicts that every report is the top one; held-out reports show a top share of about a quarter beyond the honest share and a large honest share, consistent with what Fischbacher and Föllmi-Heusi {cite:ffh2013} and Abeler and colleagues {cite:abeler2019} report. The reading of those shares rests on the uniform-truth assumption and on there being no deliberate downward lies.

The mechanism is not identified here. Six models built on different motives, and a training average with no motive at all, predict held-out reports to within 0.005 nats per report. The planted check places part of the problem in the design: when the reputation model was true, the method still could not pick it out. Report distributions from ordinary tasks constrain the shape of reporting and leave the motive open.

This does not contradict Abeler and colleagues {cite:abeler2019}. Their conclusion rests on designed treatments that change what a report signals about the reporter, which this study did not refit. Our result says that the distributions alone are not enough, which is the reason such treatments are needed.

The payoff logit's second place deserves care. A logit with a single scale parameter puts most weight on high reports and spreads the rest, and on these data that shape matches the reports about as well as a cost model. Its predicted shares are the furthest from the observed ones (Table {ref:tab:shares}), so a fit on log loss and a fit on shares can disagree.

# section Limitations {sec:limits}
- The planted check failed, so the near-tie says nothing about which model is true, and the model ranking in Table {ref:tab:loss} is not a result.
- The preregistered reproduction check failed: our subset gives 0.287 against the publishers' 0.234, and that value was printed before the lock.
- One held-out paper, Effron and colleagues (2015), carries the full model's edge; without it the sign flips.
- The shares are read from distributions, not observed lies. They assume uniform true outcomes and upward lies from preference only.
- The pooled report shapes were seen before the lock, so the share forecasts were not blind.
- All tasks are laboratory or online die and coin tasks with 2, 3, 6 or 10 outcomes, first rounds only.
- The designed treatments that separate the models were removed by our filters and not refitted.
- Scoring was done by our own code and was not blind.
- The three-part reading is not neutral between models. The fixed-cost model has no partial liars by construction, yet its predicted reports read as 22 percent partial under this reading (Table {ref:tab:shares}). The 29 percent partial and the 45 percent honest read from the real reports carry the same bias, of unknown size.

The original ledger entry did not state it. The claim stands narrowed, and this paper states the failed check in the abstract, the controls and the limitations.
- 9 November 2026: first published version.
- 14 November 2026: A review of the published page found the title and summary wider than the result. The 45 percent comes from held-out tasks with three or more outcomes, part of the 9,960 held-out subjects, and has no interval; 27,039 is the whole sample. The near-tie among six theories was presented as showing the reports cannot identify a theory; the planted check failed, so it shows only that this method could not separate them. The title and summary now say so, and the outcome word changed from held to mixed because a kill condition fired.
- 14 November 2026: A review of the published page found that the three-part reading is biased between models. The limitations and the summary now say so.
