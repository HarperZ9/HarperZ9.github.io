# abstract
Some ideas about quantum gravity predict that light in vacuum travels at a speed that depends slightly on photon energy. A short, distant gamma-ray burst tests this well, because high-energy and low-energy photons leave together and travel for billions of years. We reran the test on the public Fermi Large Area Telescope photons from GRB 090510 with a simple method, fixed in advance. {c1}Our rerun bounds any linear energy-dependent change in light speed to less than 7.6e-18 of c at 29.9 GeV, a linear energy scale above 0.32 Planck energies, about 24 times weaker than the published limit of 7.6 Planck energies from the same burst.{/c1} The value of the rerun is as a reproduction of the method, and the published limits stand. {c2}Planted delays from 1 to 10 times our bound were recovered within 0.9 percent, and the fitted slope of zero sat inside the range that shuffled photon energies gave.{/c2} In the same study we simulated sound, which has a speed limit of its own. {c3}Sound in a uniform flow moved at the flow speed plus or minus the sound speed to within 1e-6, and in a simulated condensate the wave frequencies followed the Bogoliubov law within 2 percent, with a short-wavelength packet moving at 2.89 times the sound speed.{/c3} {c4}Twelve of the 15 preregistered rules passed; the three that failed were two forecasts of our own bound's strength and one test of a packet near a simulated horizon.{/c4}

# section What this means for a reader {sec:reader}
Ask whether some waves can beat the speed of light, and the answer depends on which speed you mean. For light in empty space, the highest-energy photons from a burst 7 billion years away arrived with the low-energy ones, and even our plain reanalysis puts a tight lid on any energy-dependent speed. In a medium, waves have their own limits that bend at short wavelengths by an ordinary, known law. Neither result needs new physics, and the bound from our rerun is weaker than the published one, so quote the published one.

# section Introduction {sec:intro}
Special relativity says every observer measures the same speed of light. Several approaches to quantum gravity allow a small {g:liv}Lorentz invariance violation{/g} in which that speed depends on photon energy. The size of such an effect is usually stated as a {g:eqg}linear energy scale (E_QG,1){/g}, and the natural reference is the {g:planck-energy}Planck energy{/g}. A photon of 30 GeV is about 1e-18 of the Planck energy, so the expected effect on its speed is tiny, and only a very long flight can make it visible.

Gamma-ray bursts give that flight. Jacob and Piran {cite:jacob2008} derived how an energy-dependent speed turns into an arrival-time delay over a path through an expanding universe; that relation is the one used here. GRB 090510 is a short burst at redshift 0.903, measured from the spectrum of its host galaxy {cite:rau2009}. Abdo and colleagues {cite:abdo2009} used the Fermi observation of this burst, with emission up to about 31 GeV, and set a lower limit of 1.2 Planck energies on the linear scale under conservative assumptions about when the photons were emitted. Vasileiou and colleagues {cite:vasileiou2013} analysed four bright Fermi bursts with three complementary methods; their strongest limit, from GRB 090510, is above 7.6 Planck energies at 95 percent confidence for the case where higher energies travel slower. The LHAASO collaboration {cite:lhaaso2024} used the TeV photons of GRB 221009A and reported a linear limit above 10 Planck energies.

We had a narrower aim. A published limit rests on a pipeline most readers cannot run. We asked how close a short, plain analysis of the public photon file comes, with its forecasts and kill rules fixed before the run. The same study asked the companion question for sound: sound in a fluid has its own speed limit, and that limit is known to bend at short wavelength. Trachenko and colleagues {cite:trachenko2020} proposed an upper bound on the speed of sound in condensed matter, about 36 km per second, built from fundamental constants; it entered our study as one table check.

# section Preregistration and design {sec:prereg}
The {g:preregistration}preregistration{/g} and three forecast files were written and hash-locked on 31 October 2026, each before any run or data opening for its item. The hypothesis under test was that some sound and light waves travel faster or slower than c and follow different laws. The decision it informed: if the vacuum form fails, the published limits are quoted and our rerun is used only as a reproduction of method.

The lock records a debug run made before it. That run showed 84 photons in the analysis window and a bootstrap limit weaker than our forecast, so two forecast rows were known to fail. The rows were kept unchanged and are reported as failures. A one-photon bound was added after the debug run, and it is labelled as added wherever it appears. Two simulation settings were retuned in debug: the horizon packet was moved to start near the horizon, and the grid for the frame test was doubled.

Kill conditions, fixed in advance:
- Vacuum form: killed in the tested band if the arrival-time slope against energy is consistent with zero and the implied speed change at the highest tested energy is below 1e-15 of c. It survives outside the tested band.
- Medium form: phase and group speeds above c are already known; the form is killed as new physics if every case keeps its signal front at or below c under the ordinary wave equation.
- Sound form: killed at long wavelength if sound in a moving fluid follows the flow speed plus or minus the sound speed within 1 percent.

Each {g:kill-condition}kill condition{/g} and forecast became a rule set whose pass or fail is a {g:verdict}verdict{/g} read off by code.

# section Data and provenance {sec:data}
The photons are from the Fermi Large Area Telescope public archive at the NASA High Energy Astrophysics Science Archive Research Center {cite:fermi}: the weekly photon file for mission week 49 (processing P305, Source event class), which holds 1,214,533 photons, together with the trigger folder for bn090510016, all fetched on 31 October 2026. Fermi data are public with no restriction on use. The trigger time was read from the trigger file header. The burst position and the redshift of 0.903 were entered from memory before the run; the trigger header gives the same position to one decimal place, and the redshift matches the published value {cite:rau2009}.

Photons were kept if they arrived between 1 s before and 3 s after the trigger, had energy above 100 MeV and zenith angle below 105 degrees, and fell within an energy-dependent radius of the burst, up to 12 degrees at low energy. That radius was set from memory of the telescope's point-spread function; the published response tables were not used. This gave 119 photons. The analysis window, 0.5 to 1.45 s after the trigger, holds 84 of them, with energies from 0.1 to 29.9 GeV. The highest-energy photon carried 29.9 GeV and arrived 0.828 s after the trigger.

The sound part uses no external data. The sound-speed table for the Trachenko check lists 14 materials with values entered from memory.

# section Methods {sec:methods}
## The delay model
If the speed of light changes in proportion to photon energy, a photon of energy {m:E} arrives later or earlier than a low-energy photon emitted with it by a time proportional to {m:E}:
$$ t = t_0 + \tau E {eq:delay}
where {m:\tau} is the delay per GeV. This is {g:dispersion}dispersion{/g} in vacuum. Following Jacob and Piran {cite:jacob2008}, the slope sets the linear energy scale through a cosmological distance factor {m:K}:
$$ K = \frac{1}{H_0} \int \frac{1 + x}{\sqrt{M (1 + x)^3 + 1 - M}} dx {eq:k}
with the integral taken from 0 to the redshift 0.903, {m:H_0} = 67.4 km/s/Mpc and matter density {m:M} = 0.315. The factor came out at 4.58e17 s. A bound {m:\tau_{lim}} on the slope gives
$$ E_{QG} = \frac{K}{\tau_{lim}} {eq:eqg}
and the fractional speed change at the top energy {m:E_{top}} is
$$ \delta = \frac{E_{top} \tau_{lim}}{K} {eq:dv}

## Fitting the slope
For a trial slope {m:\tau}, each photon's time is shifted back to {m:x_i = t_i - \tau E_i}. If the trial slope is right, the shifted times bunch most tightly. The fit maximises a sharpness score
$$ S = \sum \sum G(x_i - x_j) {eq:sharp}
over all photon pairs, where {m:G} is a Gaussian of width 30 ms. The slope was searched on a grid from -250 to +250 ms per GeV in steps of 2 ms per GeV. This is a simple version of the methods in the published analyses, without the instrument response and without a model of the burst's own spectral lag.

## The bound
The 84 photons were resampled with replacement 300 times, a {g:bootstrap}bootstrap{/g}, and the slope refitted each time. The {g:interval-95}95% interval{/g} of the refitted slopes runs from its 2.5th to its 97.5th percentile, and {m:\tau_{lim}} is the larger of its two ends in size. The added one-photon bound assumes the top photon was emitted no earlier than the trigger, so its delay cannot exceed its arrival time:
$$ \tau_{1} = \frac{t_{top}}{E_{top}} {eq:one}
This form follows the conservative argument of Abdo and colleagues {cite:abdo2009} in outline only.

## Sound
Three simulations solved the linear wave equation for sound in a moving fluid, in units where the sound speed {m:c_s} is 1. In a uniform flow of speed {m:u} = 0.5, rays should move at
$$ v = u \pm c_s {eq:rays}
A frame test checked that a solution transforms between frames under the sound speed and fails under a wrong one. A horizon flow {m:u(x) = -1 + 0.5 tanh(2 \lambda x)}, with {m:\lambda} = 0.4, makes an {g:acoustic-horizon}acoustic horizon{/g} at {m:x} = 0 with {g:surface-gravity}surface gravity{/g} {m:\lambda}; a wave packet was released near it and its position and wavenumber compared with ray predictions.

The fourth simulation solved the one-dimensional Gross-Pitaevskii equation for a uniform condensate and measured the frequency of small plane waves at six wavenumbers. The known answer is the {g:bogoliubov}Bogoliubov dispersion{/g}
$$ \omega = c_s k \sqrt{1 + \frac{k^2 h^2}{2}} {eq:bog}
where {m:h} is the {g:healing-length}healing length{/g}. Its slope, the {g:group-velocity}group velocity{/g}, rises above {m:c_s} once {m:k h} nears 1.

# section Controls and planted checks {sec:controls}
Two checks were run on the photon fit.

The first {g:control}control{/g} shuffled the photon energies among the 84 photons, which breaks any link between energy and arrival time, and refitted the slope 300 times. The shuffled slopes ran from -44 to +76 ms per GeV (2.5th to 97.5th percentile). The real fitted slope, 0, sits inside that range, as forecast.

The {g:planted-check}planted check{/g} added a known delay, {m:\tau E}, to every photon at 1, 3, 5 and 10 times our bound, with both signs, and refitted. Table {ref:tab:plants} gives the result: every planted delay came back within 0.9 percent, so the fit sees a delay of the size of our bound when one is there.

# section Results {sec:results}
## Vacuum
Table {ref:tab:vacuum} gives the photon results. The fitted slope is 0, on a 2 ms per GeV grid. The bootstrap interval runs from -117 to +46 ms per GeV, so the bound is 117 ms per GeV. Through eq. {ref:eq:eqg} that is a linear energy scale of 3.9e18 GeV, or 0.32 Planck energies. {c1}Our rerun bounds any linear energy-dependent change in light speed to less than 7.6e-18 of c at 29.9 GeV, a linear energy scale above 0.32 Planck energies, about 24 times weaker than the published limit of 7.6 Planck energies from the same burst.{/c1} The speed change is below the preregistered 1e-15, so the vacuum form of the hypothesis is killed in the band tested, 0.1 to 30 GeV.

# section Discussion {sec:discussion}
The rerun did what a reproduction of method should do. On the same burst, with a plain fit, it finds no energy-dependent delay, and its controls behave: shuffled energies give a range that holds the real slope, and planted delays of the bound's size come back almost exactly. The number it produces is weaker than the published limits, by a factor of about 24 for the bootstrap bound and about 5.6 for the one-photon bound.

The gap has visible causes. Our bootstrap is dominated by whether the single 29.9 GeV photon is drawn, which makes the interval wide. The published analyses used the instrument response, careful event selection and several estimators, and they modelled or bounded the burst's own spectral lag {cite:vasileiou2013}. We did none of that. The top energy also differs: the published abstract reports emission up to about 31 GeV {cite:abdo2009}, and our selection's top photon has 29.9 GeV in the weekly file's reconstruction; we did not trace the difference.

So the vacuum result is a check that the method can be run from public data and lands in the right place. It sets no new limit. The published limits of 7.6 Planck energies from this burst {cite:vasileiou2013} and 10 from GRB 221009A {cite:lhaaso2024} are the numbers to quote.

The sound results confirm known theory. Sound in a moving fluid obeys its own cone, flow speed plus or minus sound speed, at long wavelength, and it leaves that cone at short wavelength by the Bogoliubov law. A wave can then move faster than the sound speed of its medium by an ordinary mechanism, and it remains far below c. The hypothesis that some waves follow different laws holds only in that ordinary sense.

# section Limitations {sec:limits}
- The bound holds only in the tested band, about 0.1 to 30 GeV, and only for a delay linear in energy. The quadratic form, other energies and other carriers were not tested.
- No instrument response and no intrinsic spectral lag were modelled. The selection radius was set from memory of the point-spread function, and the burst position and redshift were entered from memory, though both match published values.
- The bootstrap is unstable when one photon sets most of the fit, which is part of why our bound is weak.
- The one-photon bound was added after the debug run, and its assumption about emission time is not tested.
- A later review of our ledger noted that the rerun does not reproduce the published number. We agree, and the paper is framed as a reproduction of method for that reason.
- The sound simulations test known theory and add no new law. The Trachenko table values were entered from memory.
- Scoring was by our own code and was not blind. Settings tuned in the debug run before the lock are listed in the preregistration.

# section Reproduction {sec:repro}
- Data: download the Fermi LAT weekly photon file for mission week 49 and the trigger folder bn090510016 from the HEASARC Fermi archive.
- Select photons from 1 s before to 3 s after the trigger, above 100 MeV, zenith angle below 105 degrees, within an energy-dependent radius of the burst; keep the 0.5 to 1.45 s window.
- Fit the slope of eq. {ref:eq:delay} by maximising eq. {ref:eq:sharp} on a 2 ms per GeV grid; bootstrap 300 times; convert the larger end with eqs. {ref:eq:k}, {ref:eq:eqg} and {ref:eq:dv}.
- Shuffle energies 300 times for the null, and plant delays at 1, 3, 5 and 10 times the bound.
- For sound, solve the linear wave equation in uniform and horizon flows and the Gross-Pitaevskii equation for a uniform condensate, and compare with eqs. {ref:eq:rays} and {ref:eq:bog}.
