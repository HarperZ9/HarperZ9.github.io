# abstract
In 1951 Claude Shannon published a table of how many bits each letter of printed English carries when the reader knows 0, 1 or 2 letters before it. We recomputed that table on three pooled corpora: books from 1788 to 1859, fiction from 1892 to 1925, and present-day Wikipedia featured articles, each reduced to 26 letters and a space. {c1}The single-letter and letter-pair rows match Shannon's within 0.12 bits on all three corpora.{/c1} {c2}His third-order value of 3.1 bits sits 0.39 bits above ours on the old books and 0.23 above ours on Wikipedia.{/c2} We also trained a letter-count reader on each corpus and scored it on held-out text. {c3}With the corrected split, the reader needs 1.63 to 2.41 bits per letter on its own kind of text and never reaches Shannon's 1.3.{/c3} {c4}Within fiction, a change of era costs the reader 0.10 and 0.25 bits per letter, and a change from fiction to The Federalist in the same era costs 0.44, so the preregistered claim that era costs more than genre failed.{/c4} A planted Markov text with known entropy was recovered within 0.004 bits.

# section What this means for a reader {sec:reader}
Shannon's 1951 table is still quoted as a fact about English. The rows built from single letters and letter pairs hold on text two centuries apart. The row built from letter triples comes out about a third of a bit lower than his, so quote the higher rows with care. A reader that knows only letter counts stays well above his 1.3 bits per letter. His figure describes a human reader who brings knowledge of words and of the world.

# section Introduction {sec:intro}
Shannon {cite:shannon1951} described two ways to measure how predictable English is. The first counts letters. Let the text be reduced to 26 letters and a space, so 27 symbols. The {g:n-gram-entropy}N-gram entropy{/g} is the uncertainty of the next letter when the N minus 1 letters before it are known; F1 uses single-letter frequencies, F2 knows one letter back and F3 two. All are in {g:bits-per-letter}bits per letter{/g}, the average number of yes-or-no answers needed per letter. Blind choice among 27 symbols costs 4.75 bits. His table for 27 symbols reads 4.76, 4.03, 3.32 and 3.1 for F0 to F3, and 2.14 bits per letter from word statistics. The second way asks a person to predict the next letter of a passage. From that experiment he put an upper bound near 1.3 bits per letter on the entropy of English when 100 letters of context are known.

Later work kept both routes. Cover and King {cite:coverking1978} replaced guessing with betting, so a subject's stake on each letter gives a probability, and reported estimates near 1.3 bits per character. Brown and colleagues {cite:brown1992} built a word trigram model, measured its cross-entropy on a balanced sample of English and gave an upper bound of 1.75 bits per character. Takahira, Tanaka-Ishii and Debowski {cite:takahira2016} compressed large corpora in six languages with prediction by partial matching {cite:cleary1984} and extrapolated the code length to infinitely long text to estimate the entropy rate itself.

Those studies push the estimate of the entropy rate downward. Our question is narrower. Do the low rows of Shannon's table, which are easy to recompute, still hold on text of other eras and kinds? And when a reader built from letter counts is trained on one kind of text and reads another, which costs more, a change of era or a change of genre?

# section Preregistration and design {sec:prereg}
A {g:preregistration}preregistration{/g} is a dated plan fixed and hash-locked before any result is seen. This study had two. The first was locked on 1 November 2026 at 08:13 UTC. It named the corpora, the statistics, the reader, the planted text, the control, nine numeric forecasts and the {g:kill-condition}kill condition{/g}, a rule that decides against the run with no judgement after it. The decision it informed was which claims in material we had saved still hold when the text or the reader changes, and so which ones we can reuse.

The first run had a fault of ours. The code promised in a comment to shuffle the books before splitting training text from test text and never did. The old corpus's test text was therefore all The Federalist, and the mid corpus's all Fitzgerald, so era was read through one book. We wrote a second preregistration to fix the split and locked it at 08:20 UTC the same day. It was not blind: it was written after the first run had been read. The first run stands as recorded, and both are reported here.

Claims and kill condition, as locked:
- Claim 1, first run: the 1951 table holds on present data within 0.15 bits for F1, 0.2 for F2 and 0.3 for F3.
- Claim 2, first run: the reader reaches 1.3 bits per letter. The forecast said it would not.
- Claim, second run: era costs a reader more than genre does. The forecast said it would not.
- Void if the reader misses the entropy of the planted text by more than 0.1 bits.

# section Data and provenance {sec:data}
The books are public-domain texts from Project Gutenberg {cite:gutenberg}: The Federalist (1788), Austen (1813), Bronte (1847), Melville (1851) and Dickens (1859) make the old corpus; Doyle (1892), Joyce (1914 and 1916), Wodehouse (1920) and Fitzgerald (1925) make the mid corpus. The new corpus is a sample of Wikipedia featured articles {cite:wikipedia}, about 0.77 MB, fetched through the Wikipedia API on 1 November 2026. Wikipedia text is licensed CC BY-SA; we use it for statistics only and release no text from it. The source record names each file and its size; the text files themselves were not hashed at download, so a reproduction may draw slightly different editions.

Each text was cut to the Project Gutenberg start and end markers, lowercased, and every run of characters outside a to z was replaced by a single space. In the first run each corpus takes an equal slice from each of its books, 750,000 characters in all, and the first 600,000 train the reader. In the second run every book gives its first 80 percent to training and its last 20 percent to test, and the sets are kept apart by genre: old fiction (four novels), mid fiction (five works), old nonfiction (The Federalist) and new nonfiction (Wikipedia). Each set has 600,000 training characters, except Wikipedia with 588,712, and 100,000 test characters.

Shannon's own test passages are not openly available, so we compare against his printed table. The table values were recorded in the first preregistration from a scan of the 1951 paper.

# section Methods {sec:methods}
## The letter statistics
For a block {m:b_i} of N minus 1 letters and a next letter {m:j}, the N-gram entropy is
$$ F_N = -\sum_{i,j} p(b_i, j) \log_2 p(j | b_i) {eq:fn}
with probabilities taken as relative frequencies in the pooled corpus. This is a {g:plug-in}plug-in estimate{/g}: observed frequencies go straight into the formula with no correction for sample size. F0 is {m:\log_2 27}, 4.755 bits.

The word-based figure divides the entropy of the word frequency distribution, {m:H_w}, by the mean number of characters per word including the following space, {m:L}:
$$ F_w = \frac{H_w}{L} {eq:fw}
Shannon reached his 2.14 by extending a fitted {g:zipf}Zipf's law{/g} tail, the rule that a word's frequency falls in inverse proportion to its rank. We counted words directly.

## The reader
The reader is a {g:context-model}context model{/g}: it predicts the next letter from counts of what followed the same preceding letters in training text, in the manner of prediction by partial matching. It blends contexts of length 0 to 6. Starting from a uniform distribution over 27 symbols, each context length {m:k} with {m:n_k} counts over {m:d_k} distinct next letters updates the estimate as
$$ p_k(x) = \frac{c_k(x) + d_k p_{k-1}(x)}{n_k + d_k} {eq:ppm}
where {m:c_k(x)} is the count of letter {m:x} after that context. The reader is scored by its {g:log-loss}log loss{/g}, the average of minus {m:\log_2} of the probability it gave to the letter that came next:
$$ R = -\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i} \log_2 q(x_i | c_i) {eq:ll}
over {m:n} letters of {g:held-out}held-out text{/g}, text the reader was not trained on. The first run scores 40,000 letters of each test text and the second 30,000.

The reader also plays Shannon's {g:guessing-game}guessing game{/g}: it ranks the 27 symbols and we record the rank {m:r} of the true letter. With {m:q_r} the share of letters found at rank {m:r}, Shannon's upper and lower bounds are
$$ U = -\sum_{r} q_r \log_2 q_r {eq:up}
$$ B = \sum_{r} r (q_r - q_{r+1}) \log_2 r {eq:low}

## Transfer penalties
A {g:penalty}transfer penalty{/g} is the extra log loss a reader pays on another kind of text, against a reader trained on that kind, on the same held-out passage. With {m:R(a, b)} the log loss of the reader trained on set {m:a} reading the test text of set {m:b},
$$ D(a, b) = R(a, b) - R(b, b) {eq:pen}
The era penalty in fiction uses {m:a} and {m:b} from old and mid fiction. The genre penalty uses old fiction and The Federalist, which share an era.

# section Controls and planted checks {sec:controls}
A {g:planted-check}planted check{/g} is data built so the right answer is known. Ours is a random {g:markov-chain}order-2 Markov chain{/g} over 27 symbols, 2,000,000 symbols long, with transition probabilities drawn from a Dirichlet distribution. Its entropy per symbol is 2.673 bits by eq. {ref:eq:fn} with N of 3. A reader of context length 2 was trained on the first half of the chain and scored on 20,000 later symbols. {c5}It missed the true entropy by 0.004 bits, against a kill threshold of 0.1.{/c5} The void condition did not fire.

A {g:control}control{/g} is a version of the data with the structure under test removed and scored the same way. Here the held-out test text has its characters shuffled 20 times, 4,000 letters each, and the trained reader reads each shuffle. The real text should cost far less than any shuffle. In the first run the shuffles cost a mean of 8.51 bits per letter against 2.73 for the real Wikipedia text; in the second, 9.26 against 1.93 for old fiction. The shuffles cost more than 4.75 bits because the reader is confident and wrong on scrambled text. The real text beat all 20 shuffles in both runs. The {g:p-value-draws}p value from draws{/g}, {m:p = (k + 1)/(n + 1)} with {m:k} shuffles at or below the real loss out of {m:n}, is 0.048, the smallest value 20 draws allow.

## The reader
In the first run the reader trained on the old corpus needed 2.13 bits per letter on the old held-out text, inside the forecast band of 1.85 to 2.35. Because of the faulty split, that held-out text was all The Federalist. The guessing game gave bounds of 1.34 and 2.35 by eqs. {ref:eq:low} and {ref:eq:up}, and the reader's first guess was right on 58 percent of letters. The claim that this reader reaches 1.3 bits failed, as forecast.

The second run, with the split fixed, gives the matrix in Figure {ref:fig:cross}. {c3}With the corrected split, the reader needs 1.63 to 2.41 bits per letter on its own kind of text and never reaches Shannon's 1.3.{/c3} The Federalist is the easiest text for its own reader, 1.63, and Wikipedia the hardest, 2.41.

# section Discussion {sec:discussion}
The low rows of Shannon's table describe English letters across two centuries of text. Single-letter frequencies and letter-pair statistics differ by 0.03 and 0.09 bits between the oldest books and Wikipedia. That is the part of the table a reader can quote.

F3 is different. Our value is lower than his on every corpus, by more than our forecast allowed on the old books. We have not found the cause. Two are open, and neither was tested. A plug-in estimate falls below the true value as the number of contexts grows against the sample, and 27 squared, 729, two-letter contexts is many for 750,000 characters if rare pairs matter. Shannon's F3 also rests on letter counts from other text than ours, and a difference in the source counts would move it. Either way the third row should be quoted with its source and method.

The word-based gap fits a difference of method. Counting words directly misses the long tail of rare words that a fitted Zipf curve adds, and a missing tail lowers the entropy. We did not refit his tail to confirm it.

The reader result agrees with the line of work after Shannon {cite:coverking1978} {cite:brown1992}: letter counts alone do not reach a human reader's figure. Our context model sits at 1.63 to 2.41 bits per letter on held-out text. Shannon's 1.3 is a bound on a reader who knows words, grammar and the world.

The transfer result was the condition we most wanted to vary. A reader from one era of fiction reads another era at a cost of 0.10 to 0.25 bits, and the same reader reads The Federalist at 0.44. What a text is about and how it is argued changes letter statistics more than when it was written.

# section Limitations {sec:limits}
- Wikipedia is the only new text, so era, genre and topic stay tangled in every new-text row.
- No large language model was used as a reader. That was the change we most wanted to make, and it stays open.
- The word-based gap may come from Shannon's fitted Zipf tail against our direct count. We did not test that.
- The cause of the F3 gap is unknown. Sampling bias in the plug-in estimate and a difference in his source counts are both candidates, and neither was measured.
- Shannon's own test passages are not openly available, so the comparison is with his printed table.
- The first run's split was faulty, and the second preregistration was written after reading the first run, so it is not blind.
- Scoring was done by our own code and was not blind.

# section Reproduction {sec:repro}
- Data: the Project Gutenberg books listed in the data section, and a sample of Wikipedia featured articles of about 0.77 MB.
- Strip the Project Gutenberg header and footer, lowercase, and map every run of characters outside a to z to a single space.
- Compute eq. {ref:eq:fn} for N of 1 to 3 and eq. {ref:eq:fw} on each pooled corpus, and compare with Shannon's table.
- Train the context model of eq. {ref:eq:ppm} with contexts up to 6 letters on 600,000 characters of each set, and score eq. {ref:eq:ll} on held-out text of every set. For the second run, take the first 80 percent of each book for training and the last 20 percent for test.
- Check the reader on a planted order-2 Markov chain, and against 20 character shuffles of the held-out text.
- First run: preregistration SHA-256 551f636e8364984be905e2266229c4b94f031c3f6f7a4262c9934f377e2e3344. Plan SHA-256 0da1855b4a3831bcf4cca09a1493645a2c81601f2d3a83a4a4722b752bdd3a97. Run 20261101T081322Z, seed 20261113.
