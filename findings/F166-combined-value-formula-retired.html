Our combined value formula lost to plain prospect theory on 11,759 risky choice problems

We tested our own formula for how people value risky options, prospect theory plus terms for information, threat and drive, against a richer version of prospect theory on the public choices13k data. It never beat that richer model by the margin we set in advance (with feedback it led by 0.001 against a bar of 0.005), so we retired it. Fitted loss aversion came out at 1.16 when people chose from descriptions and 0.89 when they learned from feedback.

What this means for a reader

Adding terms to a model of choice will almost always improve its fit a little. The fair test is whether the new terms beat a version of the old theory given a similar number of extra parameters, three against our four. Ours did not, so the terms we named after information, threat and drive describe where prospect theory misfits, and they do not add a new account of why people choose. The loss aversion we measured sits near 1, well below the classic 2.25, which says this number depends on the task that measures it.

Objection

Entropy, loss probability and the mean and mean square of outcomes are one reading of information, threat and drive. Another reading could score differently, so the result covers this operationalisation only.

What would change the call. Person-level data and a preregistered reading of information gain.

From the paper's first stated limitation; our register records no separate objection.

Abstract

We had written a single value formula for risky choice: prospect value plus a term for the information a gamble resolves, a term for the threat of a loss, and a term for drive toward a setpoint. We tested it on the public choices13k data, 1,928 problems answered without outcome feedback and 9,831 answered with it, against cumulative prospect theory and against a flexible prospect theory with comparable room to absorb misfit (three extra parameters against our four). The kill rule was fixed before any real fit. The combined formula never beat the flexible prospect theory by the preregistered margin: cross-validated R² 0.634 against 0.645 without feedback and 0.597 against 0.596 with it, against a bar of 0.005.c1 Without feedback, the joint gain of the four added terms, 7.6 in deviance, sat below the 95th percentile of both null controls, 52.0 and 58.9.c2 With feedback two of the terms help as corrections to prospect theory, and the drive term does not. The kill rule fired, and the formula is retired as a model of risky choice. Fitted loss aversion was 1.16 (1.05 to 1.30) without feedback and 0.89 (0.86 to 0.93) with feedback.c4

Introduction

Cumulative prospect theoryDefinition: cumulative prospect theory (CPT) behavioural economics A model of choice between gambles with a value function concave for gains and steeper for losses, a loss aversion coefficient and a probability weighting function applied to cumulative probabilities. Here six parameters. Excludes any account of the feelings or brain processes behind a choice. full definition [2] describes a choice between gambles with three ingredients: a value function that is concave for gains and steeper for losses, a loss aversionDefinition: loss aversion behavioural economics The factor lambda by which a loss weighs more than an equal gain in the value function. 1 means losses and gains count alike. Excludes a dislike of risk as such, which curvature and weighting carry. full definition coefficient λ that scales losses, and a weighting functionDefinition: probability weighting function behavioural economics A function that maps a stated probability to the weight a choice gives it, so that small chances can count for more than their size and large ones for less. Excludes a person's belief about the odds, which in these problems are stated. full definition that bends probabilities. The theory comes from behavioural economics and describes choices; it gives no account of the feelings behind them. Loss aversion is the factor by which a loss weighs more than an equal gain, so 1 means losses and gains count alike, and it says nothing about a dislike of risk as such. The weighting function maps a stated probability to the weight a choice gives it, so small chances can count for more than their size; it is no belief about the odds. Tversky and Kahneman reported a median λ of 2.25 from their own experiment. Brown, Imai, Vieider and Camerer [3] collected 607 estimates of λ from 150 articles published from 1992 to 2017 and pooled them in a meta-analysisDefinition: meta-analysis statistics, research synthesis The statistical pooling of published estimates of one quantity into a summary and a measure of their spread. Excludes collecting new data. full definition, the statistical pooling of published estimates of one quantity, which collects no new data of its own.

Peterson, Bourgin, Agrawal, Reichman and Griffiths [1] ran the largest risky choice experiment to that date, choices13k, with choice rates on 13,006 problems collected online. They used it to compare classical decision theories with machine-learning models and to search for better theories. Thomas and colleagues [6] argued that the choice behaviour in choices13k contains structured decision noise when compared with a laboratory data set, which bears on how much of the gap between theories and flexible learners is psychology.

The data set also separates problems answered with and without feedback on outcomes. Hertwig, Barron, Weber and Erev [5] reported that people choose as if they overweight rare events when choosing from descriptions and underweight them when choosing from experience, the pattern later called the description-experience gapDefinition: description-experience gap decision research The pattern that people choose as if they overweight rare events when choosing from stated odds and underweight them when they learn the odds from sampled outcomes. Excludes differences in the gambles themselves. full definition. In decision research the term means that difference between choices from stated odds and choices learned from sampled outcomes; it excludes any difference in the gambles themselves. We analysed the two settings apart for that reason.

Our own starting point was a combined value formulaDefinition: combined value formula (COMB) our usage CPT's value difference plus four added terms in one choice rule: entropy for information, loss probability for threat, and the mean and mean square of outcomes for drive toward a setpoint. Ten parameters. Excludes any claim about mechanisms in the brain, and any reading of the terms other than this operationalisation. full definition from earlier notes in this project, a name of ours for prospect value plus four added terms in one choice rule, standing for three ideas: information, threat and drive, which makes no claim about mechanisms in the brain. It adds to prospect value a term for the information a gamble resolves, a term for the threat of a loss, and a term for reduction of a drive toward a setpoint. This paper is a test of that formula, and the formula is ours to retire.

Data and provenance

The data are choices13k [1], the selections table and the problems file from the authors' public repository. Each row gives, for one problem, the share of participants who chose option B over option A, the number of participants and the between-person standard deviation of that share. There is no person-level input.

We kept rows marked unambiguous and split them by the feedback flag: 1,928 problems answered without outcome feedback, which we call the description settingDefinition: description setting our usage, after decision research The 1,928 choices13k problems answered without outcome feedback, where people chose from stated odds alone. Excludes the 9,831 problems answered with feedback on outcomes. full definition because people chose from stated odds alone and learned no outcomes along the way, and 9,831 answered with feedback. In each setting each problem appears once. The repository states no licence. We used a local research copy and report only statistics computed from it.

A join check ran before the lock. The problems file uses row positions in the selections table as its keys, and problem identifiers do not match them. On a dry run with simulated outcomes the join by identifier matched 0.015 of rows; after the fix the match was 1.0 in both settings, checked by recomputing each problem's expected value from both files.

Methods

The choice rule

Every value model predicts the log oddsDefinition: log odds statistics For a rate p, the natural log of p divided by 1 minus p. 0 at an even split and unbounded toward certainty. Excludes any information beyond the rate, of which it is a rescaling. full definition of choosing B from the difference in value between the options. The log odds of a rate p is ln⁡(p/(1−p)), a statistics rescaling that is 0 at an even split and adds no information to the rate:

ln⁡PB1−PB=b0+τ(VB−VA)(1)

where PB is the predicted rate, b0 an intercept and τ a sensitivity. Models were fitted by binomial likelihoodDefinition: binomial likelihood statistics Fitting by choosing the parameters under which the observed counts of B choices are most probable, treating each choice as an independent draw at the predicted rate. Here five choices per listed participant. Excludes extra scatter between problems, which the dispersion handles. full definition on the observed rates, the statistics method that picks the parameters under which the observed counts of B choices are most probable, treating each choice as an independent coin with the predicted rate, counting five choices per listed participant, the count the study code uses for each problem.

The models

The expected valueDefinition: expected value (EV) probability theory The probability-weighted average payoff of a gamble, used here as the baseline value model. Excludes any allowance for risk, loss or probability weighting. full definition baseline (EV), the probability-weighted average payoff from probability theory with no allowance for risk or loss, uses V=∑ipixi for outcomes xi with probabilities pi. Cumulative prospect theory (CPT) uses rank-dependent weightsDefinition: rank-dependent weights decision theory Decision weights that depend on where an outcome ranks among a gamble's outcomes, taken as differences of the weighting function at cumulative probabilities. Excludes weighting each outcome's probability on its own, as the original 1979 prospect theory did. full definition πi, decision weights that depend on where an outcome ranks among the gamble's outcomes and are taken as differences of the weighting function at cumulative probabilities, which excludes weighting each probability on its own, and a value function that for gains is

v(x)=xα(2)

and for losses is v(x)=−λ(−x)α, with V=∑iπiv(xi). The weights come from the Tversky and Kahneman weighting function applied to cumulative probabilities, with its own γ for gains and for losses:

w(p)=pγ(pγ+(1−p)γ)1/γ(3)

CPT has six parameters. The flexible prospect theory (FLEX) has separate curvature for gains and losses, λ, and the two-parameter Prelec weighting functionDefinition: Prelec weighting function decision theory A two-parameter probability weighting function, exp of minus delta times minus log p to the power gamma, that sets the curvature and the crossing point of the curve separately. Excludes the one-parameter Tversky and Kahneman function used in CPT. full definition [4] on each side, nine parameters in all. Prelec's function, from decision theory, sets the curvature and the crossing point of the weighting curve separately, a shape the one-parameter function cannot take:

w(p)=e−δ(−ln⁡p)γ(4)

The combined formula

The combined formula (COMB) keeps CPT and adds four terms to the log odds of eq. 1, each the value for option B minus the value for option A:

ln⁡PB1−PB=b0+τ(VB−VA)+β1(HB−HA)+β2(TB−TA)+β3(MB−MA)+β4(QB−QA)(5)

Here H is the Shannon entropyDefinition: Shannon entropy information theory Minus the sum of p log2 p over a gamble's outcomes, in bits: how uncertain the outcome is before it happens, 0 for a sure thing. Excludes the sizes of the payoffs. full definition of the outcome distribution in bits, read as the information gained when the gamble resolves. In information theory entropy measures how uncertain an outcome is before it happens, 0 for a sure thing; it ignores the sizes of the payoffs:

H=−∑ipilog⁡2pi(6)

T is the probability of a loss, read as threat. M is the mean outcome and Q the mean squared outcome. Those two carry the drive term: for a setpoint S, the drive reductionDefinition: drive reduction motivation psychology, our operationalisation For a setpoint S, the quantity S squared minus (S minus x) squared for an outcome x, whose expectation is linear in the mean and mean square of outcomes. Excludes any measured need or state of the people choosing. full definition of an outcome is

S2−(S−x)2=2Sx−x2(7)

so its expectation is linear in M and Q. Drive reduction is a term from motivation psychology, here only the quadratic form above, and it excludes any measured need or state of the people choosing. The mean was divided by 10 and the mean square by 1,000 before fitting. COMB has ten parameters. Three reduced models add each term alone: CPT+H (entropy), CPT+T (loss probability) and CPT+D (mean and mean square).

Scoring

Each model was scored by 10-fold cross-validated R²Definition: cross-validated R² statistics and machine learning One minus the squared error of out-of-fold predictions over the variance of the observed rates, with 10 folds grouped by problem: the share of variance that predictions made without seeing those rates account for. Excludes in-sample R², which rewards fitting noise. full definition on the rates, with folds grouped by problem. Cross-validated R², from statistics and machine learning, is the share of variance in the observed rates that predictions made without seeing those rates account for, so it does not reward fitting noise as in-sample R² does. With out-of-fold predictions qi, observed rates yi and their mean my,

R2=1−∑i(yi−qi)2∑i(yi−my)2(8)

Each model was also scored by a quasi-binomial BICDefinition: quasi-binomial BIC statistics, model selection The Bayesian information criterion with the log-likelihood divided by the dispersion: a score that trades fit against the number of parameters, lower being better. Excludes an absolute test of a model, since it only compares models on the same data. full definition, with the dispersionDefinition: dispersion statistics, quasi-likelihood The factor phi by which observed rates scatter more than independent binomial choices would allow, taken from the COMB fit in each setting. Excludes a parameter of choice behaviour. full definition ϕ taken from the COMB fit in each setting (5.70 without feedback, 7.58 with it):

Bq=−2ln⁡Lϕ+kln⁡n(9)

where L is the binomial likelihood, k the number of parameters and n the number of problems. The Bayesian information criterion, a statistics score, trades fit against the number of parameters, lower being better; it compares models on the same data and gives no absolute test. The dispersion scales the likelihood for rates that scatter more than independent choices would allow, the quasi-binomial adjustment, and it is no parameter of choice.

Two references bound the value models. Gradient-boosted treesDefinition: gradient-boosted trees machine learning A predictor built by adding many small decision trees, each fitted to what the previous ones missed. Here a flexible learner's score on the same folds. Excludes a theory of choice, since it states no value function. full definition, fitted to the raw problem parameters and the four added terms with the same folds, give a flexible learner's score. The method, from machine learning, adds many small decision trees each fitted to what the previous ones missed; it predicts well and states no theory of choice. A sampling ceilingDefinition: sampling ceiling our usage, after reliability analysis One minus the mean sampling variance of the observed rates over their variance across problems: the share of variance not explained by finite samples of participants. Excludes noise of other kinds, such as the structured decision noise reported for these data. full definition, our bound, is the share of variance across problems not explained by sampling noise, C=1−ms/vy. Here ms is the mean over problems of the squared between-person standard deviation divided by the number of participants, and vy is the variance of the observed rates.

Tests on the added terms

Each added term was tested with the fitted CPT log odds held as a fixed offsetDefinition: offset regression A part of the prediction fixed in advance and never refitted. Here the fitted CPT log odds, so an added term can only explain what CPT leaves over. Excludes refitting CPT together with the added term. full definition: the term was standardised, fitted by iteratively reweighted least squaresDefinition: iteratively reweighted least squares statistics, generalised linear models The standard routine for fitting a logistic model by repeated weighted least-squares steps. Excludes any effect on what is tested, since it is only the fitting method. full definition, and its devianceDefinition: deviance statistics, generalised linear models Twice the log-likelihood shortfall of a model against a perfect fit. A deviance gain is the fall in deviance when a term is added. Excludes any penalty for the added parameter. full definition gain was compared with 200 fits on the same term permuted across problems. An offset, in regression, is a part of the prediction fixed in advance and never refitted, so the term can only explain what CPT leaves over. Iteratively reweighted least squares is the standard fitting routine for such models, a means of fitting with no effect on what is tested. Deviance is twice the log-likelihood shortfall of a model against a perfect fit, so a deviance gain is the improvement in fit from the term, before any penalty for its parameter. The p valueDefinition: p value (from draws) statistics The share of control draws scoring at or above the real score, with one added to the count and to the number of draws, so it is never zero. Excludes a p value from a textbook formula that assumes a distribution. full definition is a p value from draws, as in permutation statistics, and assumes no textbook distribution; it counts one added to the numerator and the denominator:

p=k+1n+1(10)

where k is the number of permuted gains at or above the real one and n the number of permutations. The smallest possible value at 200 permutations is 0.005. The six p values, three terms in two settings, were corrected by Benjamini and Hochberg's procedureDefinition: Benjamini-Hochberg correction statistics, multiple testing A rule that adjusts a set of p values into q values so that, among the tests called significant, the expected share of false calls stays at or below a chosen rate q, here 0.05. Excludes the stricter Bonferroni rule, which bounds the chance of any false call. full definition at q 0.05. That multiple-testing rule keeps the expected share of false calls among the tests called significant at or below q, which is looser than the Bonferroni bound on any false call; the corrected values are q values.

The loss aversion intervalDefinition: 95% interval statistics The range that holds the middle 95 percent of the bootstrap or control draws for a number. We report it beside the number. Excludes a statement that the true value lies inside with 95 percent probability. full definition comes from refitting CPT on problems resampled with replacement: 100 resamples without feedback and 30 with feedback, with the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles reported. This is a bootstrapDefinition: bootstrap statistics Refitting a model many times on problems resampled with replacement to read how much an estimate varies. Excludes an interval from a textbook formula that assumes a distribution. full definition, the statistics method that reads a number's spread from resampled data with no distribution assumed, and the interval holds the middle 95 percent of the resamples; it is no statement that the true value lies inside with 95 percent probability.

Controls and planted checks

The planted check ran on the description problems with their real participant counts. Three data sets were simulated from CPT with λ 1.6, and three from CPT plus an entropy term with coefficient 0.25, each with extra-binomial noiseDefinition: extra-binomial noise statistics Scatter in a choice rate from problem to problem beyond what independent choices would give, simulated here with a Beta distribution. Excludes a bias in the rate. full definition drawn from a Beta distribution. That is scatter in the choice rate from problem to problem beyond what independent coin flips would give, the kind of spread real aggregate rates show, and it adds no bias to the rate. Selection between CPT and COMB was by the quasi-binomial BIC. The rule required the right model in at least 5 of 6 data sets and λ recovered within 0.3 in at least 2 of 3. Model selection picked the true model in 6 of 6 planted data sets and recovered loss aversion within 0.3 in 3 of 3.c5 The three recovered values were 1.64, 1.55 and 1.59. The check passed, and no verdict was voided.

Results

Table 1 gives every model's score in both settings, and Figure 1 draws the main rows. Prospect theory improves on expected value by a wide margin in both settings. The flexible prospect theory improves on CPT by 0.017 without feedback and 0.012 with it.

The combined formula never beat the flexible prospect theory by the preregistered margin: cross-validated R² 0.634 against 0.645 without feedback and 0.597 against 0.596 with it, against a bar of 0.005.c1 The differences are -0.011 and +0.001. Without feedback COMB also loses on BIC, by 58.7. With feedback COMB has the lower BIC by 38.4, which meets the BIC half of the rule, and the R² half fails. The kill rule fired in both settings.

Discussion

The test was built so that the combined formula had to beat a pure prospect theory with comparable freedom. It did not. Without feedback the added terms carry no signal beyond what the nulls produce, and three extra prospect theory parameters do more than four added terms. With feedback the added terms and the flexible theory tie. The preregistered reading follows: the terms are patches for places where CPT misfits.

The terms themselves suggest which places. The drive term expands into the mean and the mean square of outcomes, so it adds an expected value and a variance correction to CPT. That is a change to the shape of the value function, which FLEX can also make through separate curvatures. Entropy and loss probability help only with feedback. Rare events are underweighted when people learn from experience [5], and a gamble with more outcomes, or with a small chance of loss, is the kind of gamble where that matters. This reading is inferred and was not tested here; a prospect theory with weighting fitted to experience could absorb these terms as well.

Both prospect theories fall well short of boosted trees: the flexible one by 0.09 without feedback and 0.21 with it, the plain one by 0.11 and 0.22, and the trees fall short of the sampling ceiling. Peterson and colleagues [1] reported a similar gap between classical theories and learned models on the same data. Part of the gap may be structure that a value model cannot express, such as correlation between payoffs; part may be the structured noise Thomas and colleagues [6] described. We did not separate the two.

Loss aversion of 1.16 without feedback, with an interval that excludes 1 and lies far below 2.25, adds one more estimate to the spread Brown and colleagues [3] pooled. With feedback the estimate falls below 1. This is an aggregate estimate on small online stakes and says nothing about any single person's loss aversion.

Limitations

  • Entropy, loss probability and the mean and mean square of outcomes are one reading of information, threat and drive. Another reading could score differently, so the result covers this operationalisation only.
  • The data are aggregate rates per problem, so no individual parameters were estimated, and the planned mapping of individual parameter tails was not possible here.
  • Stakes are small and the sample was collected online.
  • The repository states no licence, so the data are used as a local research copy.
  • Each cross-validation fold was refitted from the full-data optimum with a capped number of iterations. That start leaks a little information from the held-out fold, the same for every value model.
  • The interval for loss aversion with feedback rests on 30 bootstrap resamples, and the simulated null control on 100 draws.
  • The planted check ran on the description problems only.
  • One author wrote the forecasts, the rules and the scoring code, and scoring was not blind.

Evidence

Prediction failed. The combined formula never beat flexible prospect theory by the preregistered 0.005 in cross-validated R2 (0.634 against 0.645 without feedback, 0.597 against 0.596 with it).

Steps

  1. Data: download choices13k from the authors' public repository (the selections table and the problems file). Join the problems file by row position in the selections table.
  2. Keep rows marked unambiguous and split them by the feedback flag.
  3. Fit EV, CPT, FLEX, CPT+H, CPT+T, CPT+D and COMB under eq. 1 by binomial likelihood, with the terms of eqs. 5 to 7.
  4. Score by 10-fold cross-validated R², eq. 8, and quasi-binomial BIC, eq. 9, with the dispersion from COMB. Fit boosted trees on the same folds and compute the sampling ceiling.
  5. Test each added term with CPT as an offset against 200 permutations, correct the six p values by Benjamini and Hochberg, and run both null controls on the description setting's joint gain.
  6. Bootstrap CPT's loss aversion over problems: 100 resamples without feedback, 30 with feedback.
  7. Preregistration SHA-256: cecb9ceb76ab37020c14039391fd85eece47333c4b75a2cf3f87cd0a6133103a. Plan SHA-256: b8e125eabcc4160e6dad5de3d90207cbae261b9a0779fb1f56dd6ded054fba32. Run 20261031T080226Z, seed 20261031.

Cost. The analysis took 641 seconds on one workstation, and both controls together under one second. The study was not re-run for this paper.
