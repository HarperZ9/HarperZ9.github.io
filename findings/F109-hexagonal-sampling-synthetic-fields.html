Hexagonal sampling kept its textbook saving on synthetic fields; three forecasts about why failed

Three forecasts about hexagonal grids failed on synthetic signals: the densest packing lattice lost in three dimensions, the sphere test failed its jitter control, and hexagonal sampling scored 0.43 dB below square on 30 synthetic images. The textbook savings held as instrument checks; tests on private images and a voice recording are left out.

What this means for a reader

The hexagon's advantage is a statement about ideal signals with no edges, and it is smaller in practice than the famous 13.4 percent: 8 to 9 percent for a smooth spectrum at matched error. On a sphere most of the gain over a latitude-longitude grid comes from covering the surface evenly, and our test could not credit the regular hexagonal arrangement beyond that. In a rectangular image at equal sample count, the hexagonal grid lost on our synthetic set. Anyone choosing a grid should test it on bounded data of the kind they will store.

Objection

Everything here is synthetic. The fields have Gaussian isotropic spectra, and the synthetic images are constructed patterns. Results on natural images, recorded sound or measured volumes may differ.

What would change the call. A fresh preregistration of the border effect on a licensed open image set with padded or periodic scoring, and sparse coding with lattice and window separated.

From the paper's first stated limitation; our register records no separate objection.

Abstract

A hexagonal grid covers the plane more evenly than a square one, and sampling theory says it needs fewer samples for the same signal. We asked whether that idea carries beyond the plane, to volumes, the sphere and sound, and why it works where it does. Three forecasts failed. Introduction

Petersen and Middleton showed in 1962 that a signal whose spectrum is confined to a disc can be sampled on a hexagonal lattice with 13.4 percent fewer samples than on a square one [1]. The same argument in three dimensions favours the body-centred cubic lattice, because the best sampling latticeDefinition: sampling lattice signal processing A regular arrangement of points at which a continuous signal is measured. Square, hexagonal, cubic and body-centred cubic are examples, and each can represent signals up to a limit of fineness set by its geometry. Excludes irregular or adaptive sample placements, and the pixel shape of a display. full definition is the one whose reciprocal lattice packs spheres most densely [2]. A later analysis reports that this holds near the strict sampling limit and that another lattice can win at low sampling rates [3]. In time-frequency analysis, Strohmer and Beaver conjectured that the hexagonal lattice gives the best-conditioned Gabor frame for a Gaussian window, with a condition number near 1.26 at redundancy 2 against the square lattice's 1.41 [4, 5]. We know these sources through search summaries and abstracts.

The study began from a 2026 preprint on rendering scenes with soft hexagonal patches that each carry a first-order colour field [6]. That design has two ingredients, a hexagonal layout and a field that varies inside each cell. The question was whether either ingredient pays in other media, and the decision it informed was which grid and which kind of cell our own tools use to store sampled images, volumes, spherical fields and sound.

Data and provenance

Every signal in this paper was generated by the study's code from a fixed seed: random fields with a Gaussian isotropic spectrum in two and three dimensions, random spherical-harmonic fields to degree 40, 30 synthetic test images (isotropic noise at two slopes, random discs, axis-aligned blocks, grids and text, the same rotated by 30 and 45 degrees, smooth gradients, and gradients cut by straight edges), and three kinds of synthetic sound (a slow chirp, chords with vibrato and glides, and fast frequency sweeps). No external dataset is used.

Methods

Sampling in the plane and in three dimensions

A field with power exp⁡(−k2/2) at spatial frequency k was sampled on a lattice and rebuilt with that lattice's ideal low-pass filter; the error is exact by Parseval's theorem. The strict test used a flat disc (or ball) spectrum and found the lowest density with no aliasingDefinition: aliasing signal processing The error made when a signal holds detail finer than the samples can represent, so that the fine detail is rebuilt as false coarse detail. Excludes noise in the measurement, and blur from the rebuilding filter. full definition. For the plane, theory gives the ratio of hexagonal to square density as

ρhexρsq=32(1)

which is 0.866, and for the body-centred cubic latticeDefinition: body-centred cubic lattice crystallography and sampling theory The cubic lattice with one more point at the centre of every cube. The face-centred cubic lattice instead adds a point at the centre of every face, and each of the two is the reciprocal of the other. Excludes a claim about any material's crystal structure. full definition against the cubic lattice 1/2, which is 0.707. The matched-error test found the density at which the expected relative error crosses 0.01, 0.001 and 0.0001, by Monte Carlo over one million frequencies. The paired advantage of lattice b over lattice a on one field is

D=log⁡10eaeb(2)

with e the relative squared error. In the plane it was measured at a density of 36 samples per unit area on 200 fields.

The sphere

Fields were sampled at the points of a geodesic gridDefinition: geodesic grid geometry and climate modelling A set of points on a sphere made by subdividing the faces of an icosahedron and pushing the points out to the surface. Each point has six near-equal neighbours except twelve that have five, so the layout is close to hexagonal everywhere. Excludes a latitude-longitude grid, whose points crowd together at the poles. full definition, of a latitude-longitude grid sampled at cell centres, and of a Fibonacci spiral, and rebuilt by least squares in spherical harmonics. Errors were interpolated to 1,000 samples on 40 fields. Isotropic fields spread power evenly over orders; zonal fields vary with latitude only.

Images

Each 256-pixel grey image was smoothed, sampled at exactly 4,096 points on a square or a hexagonal lattice, and rebuilt with a Gaussian kernel. The two kernel widths were tuned per lattice on 8 held-out tuning images, 4 of them private, then frozen. The score is the difference in PSNRDefinition: PSNR image processing Peak signal-to-noise ratio, a measure in decibels of how closely a rebuilt image matches the original. Higher is closer, and 1 dB is a change of about 21 percent in mean squared error. Excludes a measure of how good the image looks to a viewer. full definition over all pixels.

Cells with a field inside

A volume or a spherical field was stored as cells at a budget of 1,500 numbers: one number per constant cell, or a value and its gradient per first-order cell (4 numbers in three dimensions, 3 on the sphere). The field effect is eq. 2 for first-order against constant cells, and the grid effect is the same for body-centred against cubic cells or geodesic against latitude-longitude cells, on 6 random smooth fields.

Time-frequency lattices

For a signal of 960 samples, a rectangular lattice of time-frequency points was compared with a hexagonal one at the same number of points. The frame condition numberDefinition: frame condition number time-frequency analysis The ratio B/A of a frame's upper and lower bounds, which says how much rebuilding can amplify errors in the coefficients. 1 is ideal. Excludes a measure of how few coefficients a signal needs. full definition B/A of the Gabor frameDefinition: Gabor frame time-frequency analysis A set of copies of one short window, shifted in time and in frequency onto a lattice of points, such that any signal can be rebuilt stably from how strongly it matches each copy. Excludes a basis; a frame has more members than strictly needed, by a factor called the redundancy. full definition was computed for the best of 41 Gaussian window widths at five redundancies. Sound segments were then approximated by 120 numbers with a greedy sparse solver, using atoms of fixed frequency (3 numbers each) or chirplets whose frequency changes linearly inside the atom (4 numbers).

Intervals and correction

Each 95% intervalDefinition: 95% interval statistics The range that holds the middle 95 percent of the bootstrap or control draws for a number. We report it beside the number. Excludes a statement that the true value lies inside with 95 percent probability. full definition comes from 2,000 bootstrap resamples over fields, images or segments. One-sided p values were corrected with the Benjamini-Hochberg correctionDefinition: Benjamini-Hochberg correction statistics An adjustment of a set of p values that holds the expected share of false discoveries among the results called significant at a chosen level. Excludes a guarantee that no result in the set is a false discovery. full definition [7] over the study's 24 tests, which include the tests left out here.

Controls and planted checks

The controlDefinition: control statistics and experimental design A version of the data with the structure under test removed (for example shuffled or pooled), scored the same way as the real data, so the real score has something to beat. Excludes a comparison with a different dataset or with a published number. full definition for a lattice claim is the same lattice with each point moved at random by 0.15 of a spacing. A jittered lattice keeps the even coverage and loses the regular arrangement. A lattice result passes only if the regular lattice's advantage exceeds the 95th percentile of the jittered lattice's advantage over the same reference, across 40 control draws.

The strict density ratios serve as the planted checkDefinition: planted check research method; called a positive control in experimental science Data built so that the right answer is known, run through the same analysis before the real data, to show that the analysis can see the effect when it is there. Excludes any check made on the real data, which cannot show what the analysis misses. full definition: they are known from geometry, and the code had to reproduce them without being given them. Those values, and the frame condition numbers, are deterministic. They could not have come out otherwise if the code is right, so they test the instrument and are not findings.

Results

What failed

In three dimensions the forecast that the densest packing lattice samples best failed: the body-centred cubic lattice needed 0.707 of the cubic sample count at the strict limit against 0.770 for the face-centred lattice.c1 The counter-forecast, that the winner is the lattice whose reciprocal packs densest, passed. Table 1 gives the counts. At matched error the body-centred lattice also needed fewer samples than the face-centred one, but only by 1 percent at an error of 0.01 and 0.3 percent at 0.001, and the result file holds no interval for these counts. We do not claim a difference between those two lattices away from the strict limit.

On the sphere a geodesic grid had a tenth of the error of a latitude-longitude grid at 1,000 samples (1.00 log10 units, 95% interval 0.99 to 1.02), but a jittered geodesic grid reached 0.83 on average and 1.10 at its 95th percentile, so the test failed its control.c2 Figure 1 shows the plane and the sphere side by side. The regular geodesic grid is above the jittered grid's average and inside its spread. To reach a relative error of 0.01 the geodesic grid needed 680 samples, the Fibonacci spiral 742 and the latitude-longitude grid 1,124. The geodesic grid's error was below the Fibonacci spiral's by 0.24 log10 units (0.22 to 0.25). The reading that fits these numbers is that even coverage carries the gain over latitude and longitude, and that the test could not show the hexagonal arrangement adds to it.

The third failure is a transfer forecast. If the hexagon's gain came from treating all directions alike, the geodesic advantage should be smaller for zonal fields, which line up with the latitude-longitude grid. It was not: the advantage was 1.08 for zonal fields against 1.00 for isotropic ones, a difference of -0.08 (-0.18 to +0.03) in the direction opposite to the forecast.

On 30 synthetic test images at 4,096 samples, hexagonal sampling scored 0.43 dB below square sampling (interval -0.85 to -0.12).c3 This is a subset of the preregistered pool and so carries no verdict. A check made after the run scored only pixels two sample spacings or more from the image edge; there the difference on the same 30 images was +0.09 dB (-0.13 to +0.39). That fits a loss at the border, where a square lattice tiles a rectangle exactly and a hexagonal one leaves ragged margins, but the check was chosen after the result was seen and its interval includes zero.

What held

In the plane the hexagonal lattice needed 0.866 of the square count for strictly band-limited fields and 0.905 to 0.922 at matched error for a Gaussian spectrum.c4 The saving at matched error is 8 to 9 percent. The hexagonal lattice's expected error was lower at every density tested, and at a density of 36 its advantage was 0.178 log10 units (0.174 to 0.181, 200 fields) against a jittered-lattice 95th percentile of 0.031.

Hexagonal time-frequency lattices had the lower frame condition number at all five redundancies (Table 2). Our value at redundancy 2 is 1.262, close to the 1.26 reported in the literature for the continuous case.

A first-order field inside each cell beat a constant cell at equal parameter count in every draw: by 0.26 log10 units in three dimensions and 0.54 on the sphere.c5 The grid effect at the same budget was smaller: 0.04 for body-centred against cubic cells and 0.18 for geodesic against latitude-longitude cells, also with the same sign in all 6 fields. Figure 2 shows every draw.

In sound, the first-order idea paid only where the frequency moves fast. On fast sweeps chirplets beat fixed atoms by 1.86 dB (0.89 to 2.76, 16 segments), which passed. On the slow chirp they lost 2.53 dB (-2.83 to -2.23) and on synthetic chords 0.68 dB (-0.80 to -0.56). The gain rose with sweep rate at a rank correlation of 0.52 (-0.01 to 0.77), which did not survive correction (corrected p 0.054). On six smooth synthetic images a first-order colour field gained 0.38 dB (0.08 to 0.72) at equal parameter count; this is a subset with no verdict.

With fixed atoms, the hexagonal time-frequency lattice beat the rectangular one on each synthetic clip: by 3.29 dB on the slow chirp, 0.72 dB on chords and 1.03 dB on fast sweeps. This comparison mixes two things. At redundancy 2 the hexagonal lattice's best window is 1.8 times wider than the rectangular lattice's (20.6 against 11.3 samples), so the gain cannot be assigned to the lattice alone.

Discussion

The forecasts that failed share one assumption: that the hexagon wins because it is the most even or most isotropic arrangement, and that this reason travels. In three dimensions the lattice with the best packing lost to the lattice whose reciprocal has the best packing, which is the textbook result and which our literal forecast had ignored. On the sphere, isotropy did not predict where the gain was largest, and jittering the grid did not remove the gain. On bounded images the lattice's interior efficiency was outweighed by something else, plausibly the border.

The ingredient that did transfer is the first-order field, and only where the signal is smooth across a cell: volumes, spherical fields, smooth images and fast sweeps. It lost on slowly changing sound, where a fixed atom already fits.

For our own tools the result set these rules: store volumes on the body-centred lattice and spherical fields on a geodesic or Fibonacci grid, keep the square grid for bounded images unless a fresh test with padded or periodic borders says otherwise, and give cells a first-order field where the signal is smooth at the cell scale.

Limitations

  • Everything here is synthetic. The fields have Gaussian isotropic spectra, and the synthetic images are constructed patterns. Results on natural images, recorded sound or measured volumes may differ.
  • The image result is a subset of a preregistered pool whose other half is private, so it carries no verdict. The kernel widths were tuned on 8 images that included 4 private ones.
  • The border explanation for the image loss was formed after the result was seen, and its supporting check has an interval that includes zero.
  • The sphere test used one jitter size (0.15 of a spacing) and 40 control draws. A smaller advantage of the regular arrangement over jitter is not excluded; the averages differ by 0.17 log10 units.
  • Matched-error sample counts have no intervals in the result file. The body-centred and face-centred lattices differ there by 1 percent or less.
  • The cell tests used 6 fields and one budget.
  • The lattice gain in sparse coding of sound is confounded with window width.
  • The strict ratios and the frame condition numbers are deterministic checks of the code and add nothing new to the literature.
  • The comparison says nothing about hexagonal patches whose positions and shapes are optimised, as in the preprint that prompted the study; its claims were not tested here.
  • The study was not blind: trial runs were seen before the lock. Scoring was by our own code.

Evidence

Mixed. Failed: face-centred cubic needed 0.770 of the cubic count against 0.707 for body-centred; the geodesic advantage of 1.00 log10 units sat below the jittered control's 95th percentile of 1.10; the zonal forecast missed by -0.08 (-0.18 to +0.03); hexagonal sampling lost 0.43 dB (-0.85 to -0.12) on 30 synthetic images.
