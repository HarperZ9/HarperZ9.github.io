Published 1 sigma error bars hold near nominal; a 95 percent interval needs 3 to 5 sigma

When a particle mass or an atomic mass moves between editions of its reference table, the old 1 sigma error bar covers the new value about as often as it should. The tails are far heavier than a bell curve, so covering 95 percent of later values takes 3 to 5 times the stated sigma.

What this means for a reader

When a table prints a value plus or minus one sigma, the next edition usually lands about as close as that promise says. Large misses are the problem: shifts of four or five sigma happen far more often than a bell curve allows. If you need 95 percent confidence from a published plus-or-minus, multiply it by three to five, and by more for an astronomical quantity that is still being settled.

Objection

Later tables reuse inputs from earlier ones (shared inputs), so the later edition is not independent of the earlier one. That flatters every coverage figure here. Editions that share no inputs would be the cleaner test.

What would change the call. No condition for raising or overturning the call is on file in the register.

From the paper's first stated limitation; no reviewer attack is on file.

Abstract

A stated uncertainty is a forecast of where later, better measurements will put a value. We scored that forecast on four reference sources: particle masses and widths in the Particle Data Group tables (2010 to 2025), atomic masses in the Atomic Mass Evaluation (2003 to 2020), the CODATA recommended constants (2002 to 2022) and Huchra's compilation of published Hubble constant values. For each quantity we measured the shift between the first and last edition in units of the first edition's stated sigma. The earlier 1 sigma interval covered 0.68 of later PDG values that moved (n 227) and 0.66 of later AME values (n 2,213, nearly all of which moved), close to the nominal 0.6827.c1 The tails are far heavier than a normal curve. Covering 95.45 percent of later values took 3.8 times the stated sigma for moved PDG values, 4.0 for AME and 4.9 for CODATA.c2 Student t fits to the nonzero shifts gave nu of 1.67 for PDG, 1.22 for AME and 2.17 for CODATA, close to the Cauchy shape.c3 Published Hubble constant values before 1990 covered a proxy truth of 70 km/s/Mpc within 1 sigma in 0.35 of 115 cases.c4 Later tables reuse inputs from earlier ones, which flatters every coverage figure here.

Introduction

A stated 1 sigma uncertaintyDefinition: stated 1 sigma uncertainty metrology (the standard uncertainty of the GUM) and physics data tables The plus-or-minus a table prints beside a value. Meant as one standard deviation of the value's error; for a normal error the interval of one sigma either side holds the truth 68.27 percent of the time and two sigma 95.45 percent. Excludes an expanded uncertainty already multiplied by a coverage factor, and any bound meant to hold always. full definition is the plus-or-minus a table prints beside a value, meant as one standard deviation of its error. For a normal error, one sigma either side should hold the truth 68.27 percent of the time and two sigma 95.45 percent. Later editions of the same table give a better estimate of the true value, so the share of later values that fall inside an earlier interval, its coverageDefinition: coverage statistics and metrology The share of cases in which an interval drawn round an earlier value contains the value that later replaced it. Here the interval is one or two of the earlier value's own stated sigma either side. Excludes the share of intervals that contain the true value, which no later table can supply. full definition, can be counted.

Henrion and Fischhoff [5] examined the history of the physical constants, including the speed of light, and found that reported uncertainties were consistently too small, with recommended values often moving by more than their stated errors. Chen, Gott and Ratra [6] built the error distribution of 461 Hubble constant measurements in Huchra's compilation against a central value of 71 km/s/Mpc. They found it non-Gaussian, with 95.4 percent limits at 7.0 times the quoted errors and a shape described by a widened Student t with two degrees of freedom. Bailey [7] analysed about 41,000 measurements of 3,200 quantities from medicine, nuclear and particle physics and interlaboratory comparisons, and found the normalised differences between measurements of the same quantity close to Student t distributions, often almost Cauchy. Its reading is that researchers estimate the size of typical errors well and underestimate the chance of large ones.

This paper asks the same question of edition-to-edition changes in four large tables at once, with a planted check, a normal-world control, a test that removes unchanged values, and forecasts fixed in advance. Bailey's data include nuclear and particle physics. Our searches on 9 November 2026 did not return a study that scores the PDG mass-width files edition against edition; that is what the searches returned, and it does not show that none exists.

Methods

Shift in units of the earlier sigma

For quantity i with value ai and stated sigma si in the first edition and value bi in the last, the shift in units of the earlier sigmaDefinition: shift in units of the earlier sigma statistics, our usage; called a normalised residual in metrology The size of the change in a quantity's value between an earlier and a later edition of a table. Divided by the uncertainty stated in the earlier edition. Excludes the sign of the change, and any uncertainty in the later value. full definition is

zi=|bi−ai|si(1)

Only quantities present in both editions with si>0 enter.

Coverage and needed multiple

Coverage at m sigma is the share of quantities with zi≤m:

Cm=N(z≤m)n(2)

where N(z≤m) counts the quantities whose shift is at most m and n is the number of quantities. The needed multipleDefinition: needed multiple metrology, our usage; a coverage factor fitted after the fact The multiple of the stated sigma that would have covered 68.27 or 95.45 percent of the later values. It is the matching percentile of the shifts in units of the earlier sigma, written k68 and k95. Excludes a factor known before the later values were published. full definition k68 is the 68.27th percentile of the shifts and k95 the 95.45th percentile: the multiples of the stated sigma that would have covered those shares. For honest normal errors both coverages sit at 0.6827 and 0.9545 and the two multiples at 1 and 2.

Tail shape

A folded Student t distributionDefinition: Student t distribution and its tail index nu statistics A bell-shaped family with heavier tails than the normal curve. Its degrees of freedom nu set the tail weight, so small nu means large shifts are common, nu of 1 is the Cauchy distribution and nu without limit is the normal curve. Excludes skewed or bounded shapes. full definition with a scale and degrees of freedom nu was fitted by maximum likelihood to the nonzero shifts. Small nu means heavy tails; nu of 1 is the Cauchy distributionDefinition: Cauchy distribution probability The Student t distribution with nu equal to 1. Whose tails are so heavy that its mean and variance do not exist. Excludes any distribution with a finite variance. full definition, and a normal curve has nu without limit.

Intervals

The interval on each 1 sigma coverage comes from a bootstrapDefinition: bootstrap statistics, resampling Redrawing the quantities of a table with replacement. Here 300 times, and recomputing the coverage each time; the middle 95 percent of the recomputed values is the interval. Excludes uncertainty from the choice of editions or from shared inputs, which resampling quantities does not touch. full definition: quantities are redrawn with replacement 300 times and the coverage recomputed; the 95% intervalDefinition: 95% interval statistics The range that holds the middle 95 percent of the bootstrap or control draws for a number. We report it beside the number. Excludes a statement that the true value lies inside with 95 percent probability. full definition is the middle 95 percent of the redrawn values.

The Hubble constant

Huchra's table has no later edition of each entry. Each published value was scored against a proxy truth fixed in advance, 67.4, 70 or 73 km/s/Mpc, reported separately, and the values published before 1990 were scored against 70.

Changes of assumption

Each was run apart: the moved-values arm; the top 1 percent of shifts removed; the last pair of editions alone; and, for CODATA, one row per distinct shift rounded to two decimals, since many constants are derived from the same inputs. A shrink test asked, for each quantity in three or more editions, whether its stated error fell faster than its edition-to-edition steps. An out-of-sample test fitted the needed multiples on the earlier half of the editions and applied them to the later half.

Controls and planted checks

The planted check built 4,000 shifts in a world where the stated sigma is half the true error and the errors follow a Student t with three degrees of freedom. The true k68 is

ktrue=2q3(3)

where q3 is the 0.84135 quantile of that t distribution. On a planted world with stated errors set to half the true error, the method recovered the needed multiple as 2.405 against a true 2.394.c5 The fitted nu was 3.002 against a true 3. The kill condition did not fire.

Results

Table 1 gives every field and Figure 2 draws the same numbers. The earlier 1 sigma interval covered 0.68 of later PDG values that moved (n 227) and 0.66 of later AME values (n 2,213, nearly all of which moved), close to the nominal 0.6827.c1 The 95% intervals on those two coverages are 0.63 to 0.74 for PDG and 0.65 to 0.68 for AME; only 0.3 percent of the AME values did not move. At 2 sigma both fall short of 0.9545, at 0.85 and 0.82.

Covering 95.45 percent of later values took 3.8 times the stated sigma for moved PDG values, 4.0 for AME and 4.9 for CODATA.c2 Student t fits to the nonzero shifts gave nu of 1.67 for PDG, 1.22 for AME and 2.17 for CODATA, close to the Cauchy shape.c3

Over all PDG values, including the 0.35 that never moved, the tables over-cover at 1 sigma (0.79). In the last PDG step, 2024 to 2025, 0.96 of 421 values did not move. Coverage that counts those values says more about which values were carried forward than about how honest the stated errors are.

The CODATA step from 2018 to 2022 covered only 0.47 at 1 sigma. A later reading of this run, made after the lock and recorded as a separate finding, traced most of that step to one input: 99 of the 273 rows moved by 4.2 to 4.7 of their 2018 sigma when the fine-structure constant moved by 4.55 of its own. The CODATA 2022 adjustment reports that move; we did not re-read the report for this paper. The rows are not independent: kept to one row per distinct shift, CODATA has 76 rows, with 1 sigma coverage 0.63 and k95 5.1.

Published Hubble constant values before 1990 covered a proxy truth of 70 km/s/Mpc within 1 sigma in 0.35 of 115 cases.c4 Over all 544 values the needed k95 was 5.4 to 7.7 depending on the proxy, the worst field in the set at every proxy. Ten published speed-of-light values from before 1983, scored against the defined value, covered 0.5 at 1 sigma; six of those came from fetched sources and the rest were transcribed, so we give that row no weight.

Removing the top 1 percent of shifts changed 1 sigma coverage by about 0.01 (PDG 0.80, AME 0.67, CODATA 0.86) and lowered k95 to 2.8 for PDG, 3.6 for AME and 3.2 for CODATA. The largest single shift, the alpha particle molar mass in CODATA at 74 sigma, comes from the 2019 redefinition of the SI changing the molar mass rows. The largest PDG shift (12.7) and the largest AME shift (silicon-32, 64) were not traced.

Needed multiples did not carry forward in time where values moved. Fitted on CODATA 2002 to 2014 and applied to 2014 to 2022, k68 of 0.65 lowered 1 sigma coverage from 0.40 to 0.32, and k95 of 1.46 reached 0.79 where 0.9545 was the aim. In AME, 2016 to 2020 hardly moved, and coverage stayed at 0.94.

The shrink test found stated errors falling faster than the steps between editions in 0.66 of 287 CODATA quantities, 0.52 of 104 PDG quantities and 0.25 of 2,227 AME quantities. No general rule that uncertainty shrinks faster than values settle follows from that.

Discussion

The pattern matches Bailey [7]: the core of a stated uncertainty is about right and the tails are not. Our fitted nu values, 1.2 to 2.2, sit at or below the heavy end of what that paper describes, and nearer the Cauchy shape. Our Hubble constant result agrees with Chen, Gott and Ratra [6] in kind: they put 95.4 percent limits at 7.0 times the quoted errors against a central value of 71, and we find 6.5 against 70 on a larger version of the same compilation.

In these tables the fault sits in the tails. Bailey draws the same distinction: underestimating every sigma by one factor widens a normal curve without giving it heavy tails. In our tables, the 1 sigma multiple sits near 1 while the 95.45 percent multiple sits near 4. A single scaling of every error bar cannot fix both.

The CODATA step from 2018 to 2022 shows how the tails arise in a table built from shared inputs: one input moves and carries many derived values with it. Henrion and Fischhoff [5] found the reported uncertainties of the constants too small across their history. A correction factor fitted on quiet years will miss the next one, which is what the out-of-sample test found.

Limitations

  • Later tables reuse inputs from earlier ones (shared inputsDefinition: shared inputs metrology and data evaluation Measurements or derived values that feed both an earlier and a later edition of a table. So that the two editions are not independent and agree more than independent work would. Excludes measurements made only for the later edition. full definition), so the later edition is not independent of the earlier one. That flatters every coverage figure here. Editions that share no inputs would be the cleaner test.
  • The Hubble constant numbers depend on the proxy truth chosen. Read them per proxy.
  • PDG editions before 2010 could not be reached. The older AME files came through the Internet Archive and their terms were not read.
  • An unchanged value counts as covered. The moved-values arm removes those values but keeps quantities whose change was small for reasons of bookkeeping.
  • Needed multiples fitted on early CODATA editions did not carry forward. The 3 to 5 sigma figure describes the past and will miss when a shared input breaks.
  • Our forecast of 0.55 for PDG coverage over all values missed at 0.79, because 0.35 of those values never moved.
  • Scoring was by our own code and was not blind.

Evidence

Held. Real public tables with planted and honest controls, and a clear statement of correlated inputs.
