Colour adds 0.002 of explained variance to photo memorability once the subject is known

On 9,988 MemCat photographs, lightness, chroma and colour temperature add 0.0022 of held-out R2 (0.0009 to 0.0034) to memorability once the subject subcategory is known. One image set and one memory task; a subcategory is coarse, so the small link that remains may still stand for scene content.

What this means for a reader

In this image set, most of what looks like a link between colour and memorability is a link between subject and memorability: food and animals are remembered better than landscapes, and they also differ in colour. Once the subject is fixed, colour explains well under one percent of the remaining differences. A claim that a warmer or more saturated image is more memorable should be read with the subject controlled, and with the size of the effect beside its p value.

Objection

One image set and one memory task. Memorability here is recognition in a repeat-detection game over minutes, and it may not carry to memory in daily life or to other image sets.

What would change the call. Repeat on another memorability image set with scene-content controls, using the locked feature code.

From the paper's first stated limitation; our register records no separate objection.

Abstract

Bright, saturated and warm pictures are often said to stick in memory. We tested how much of that survives once the subject of the photograph is held fixed. The data are the 9,988 colour photographs of the public MemCat set, each with a memorability score from a repeat-detection game and a label for one of 134 subject subcategories. Subcategory alone predicts held-out memorability with an R2 of 0.558. Adding lightness, chroma and colour temperature lifts held-out R2 by 0.0022 (95% interval 0.0009 to 0.0034), from 0.558 to 0.560; the content-matched shuffle reaches 0.0001 at its 95th percentile.c1 Within a subcategory each property correlates weakly with memorability: lightness +0.027, chroma +0.041 and log colour temperature -0.056, each above a shuffle bar near 0.019.c2 Colour alone, with no subject label, predicts memorability at R2 0.096, about 44 times the gain it adds once subcategory is known.c3 The effect is real under our controls and small. A subcategory is a coarse label, so the remaining link may still stand for scene content such as snow or sky. This page reports one of the study's four tests; the other three are left out for the reasons given in the design section.

Introduction

MemorabilityDefinition: memorability vision science and memory research The share of viewers who correctly recognise a photograph as a repeat when it comes round again in a stream of images, corrected for false alarms. It is a property measured per image across many viewers. Excludes how well one person remembers, memory over days or years, and how much viewers like the image. full definition is the chance that a viewer who has seen a photograph once recognises it when it comes round again. Isola and colleagues showed that this chance is stable across viewers and so belongs partly to the image [2]. Papers that cite that work report its colour result as weak: mean hue, saturation and similar simple features predict memorability poorly. We read that through citing papers and a search summary; the paper's own tables were not read.

Goetschalckx and Wagemans built MemCat to study memorability inside subject categories: 10,000 photographs in five broad categories, each split into subcategories, with a memorability score for every image [1]. That structure allows a question the earlier sets answer less directly. Hold the subject fixed at the subcategory level, and ask what colour still adds.

The decision this informed was whether a tool of ours that tunes images should carry a colour control aimed at memorability. Our searches returned the MemCat paper and the weak colour result of Isola and colleagues, and no measurement of the within-subcategory colour gain on MemCat. That is what two searches returned; it does not show that none exists.

Preregistration and design

The preregistrationDefinition: preregistration research method (metascience) A dated written plan for a study, fixed and hash-locked before any result is seen: the question, the data, the statistic, the controls, the forecasts and the kill conditions. Excludes a plan revised after looking at the outcome, and a protocol written only for the record after the run. full definition was written and hash-locked on 29 October 2026, before any statistic of the study was computed. Before the lock we had seen the layout of the data files and the mean and standard deviation of MemCat memorability (0.693 and 0.137). No colour property had been correlated with anything.

The study held four tests. This paper reports the third, colour and memorability on MemCat, and leaves out the other three:

  • The first test compared the colour of one person's saved images with seasonal day length. It rests on a private image collection, and its planted check fell just short of its own bar, so it is not published.
  • The second test, colour and mood, was recorded as untestable before the run: no mood record overlapping the images exists.
  • The fourth test reused an image index and word ratings from an earlier study of ours that are not a public dataset.

The memorability test carried five verdictDefinition: verdict research method (our usage) The pass or fail of one preregistered rule set against the run's numbers, read off by code. Excludes an interpretation written after reading the numbers. full definitions, each a rule fixed before the run and read off by code: a sanity verdict, a gain verdict and one verdict per colour property. The rules are in Appendix A. The multiple-test correction was fixed over the eight primary p values of the whole study, four of which belong to this test.

Data and provenance

MemCat [1] holds 10,000 photographs in five categories (animal, food, landscape, sports, vehicle) and 134 subcategories. Its memorability score is the hit rate in a repeat-detection game corrected for false alarms, from about 99 viewers per image. The files come from the Open Science Framework node kvghu. The node carries a CC BY 4.0 licence, and its README restricts use to academic research and to the terms of the source image sets. We report statistics only and redistribute no image.

The images were read from the archive as data. We dropped 12 images that looked greyscale (mean absolute difference between the red and blue channels under 2), which left 9,988.

Methods

Colour properties

Each image was resized so its longest side is 256 pixels and converted to the OKLab colour space. Three properties were fixed in advance. LightnessDefinition: lightness (OKLab L) colour science How light or dark a colour appears on a scale built to match human judgement, here averaged over every pixel of the image. Excludes physical light intensity, and contrast between parts of the image. full definition is the mean OKLab L over the frame. ChromaDefinition: chroma colour science How far a colour is from grey at the same lightness, here the mean distance from the grey axis in OKLab over the image. Our measure of saturation. Excludes hue, which says which colour it is, and lightness. full definition is the mean OKLab chroma. The third is the natural logarithm of the correlated colour temperatureDefinition: correlated colour temperature colour science and lighting The temperature in kelvin of the glowing body whose colour is closest to a given colour. Low values look warm and orange, high values look cool and blue. Here it is computed from the image's mean colour. Excludes the temperature of anything in the scene, and the white balance setting of the camera. full definition of the image's mean colour, by McCamy's approximation, clipped to the range 1,500 to 25,000 kelvin. A higher value means a cooler, bluer image.

The gain

Content is the subcategory, coded as 134 indicator variables. The baseline model predicts memorability from subcategory alone. The full model adds the three colour properties, standardised, as linear terms. Both were scored by held-out R2Definition: held-out R2 statistics and machine learning The share of the variation in a quantity that a model predicts on data it was not fitted to. 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than predicting the average, and it can fall below 0. Excludes R2 computed on the same data used to fit the model, which rises whenever a variable is added. full definition over 10 folds stratified by subcategory. The primary statistic is the gain

G=Rfull2−Rcontent2(1)

with both terms computed on held-out images.

Within-subcategory correlation

For each property we removed the subcategory mean from the property and from memorability and took the Pearson correlation of the two residuals,

rw=corr(m−ms,c−cs)(2)

where m is memorability, c the property and the subscript s marks the subcategory mean.

Nulls, intervals and correction

The controlDefinition: control statistics and experimental design A version of the data with the structure under test removed (for example shuffled or pooled), scored the same way as the real data, so the real score has something to beat. Excludes a comparison with a different dataset or with a published number. full definition for the gain permutes the colour rows among images of the same subcategory, 1,000 times, and refits on the same folds. The control for each correlation permutes the property within subcategory, 1,000 times, and compares absolute values. Each p value is a p value from drawsDefinition: p value (from draws) statistics The share of control draws scoring at or above the real score, with one added to the count and to the number of draws, so it is never zero. Excludes a p value from a textbook formula that assumes a distribution. full definition,

p=k+1n+1(3)

with k the number of draws at or above the real value and n the number of draws. With 1,000 draws the smallest possible value is 0.001. Each 95% intervalDefinition: 95% interval statistics The range that holds the middle 95 percent of the bootstrap or control draws for a number. We report it beside the number. Excludes a statement that the true value lies inside with 95 percent probability. full definition comes from 2,000 bootstrap resamples over images. The p values were corrected with the Benjamini-Hochberg correctionDefinition: Benjamini-Hochberg correction statistics An adjustment of a set of p values that holds the expected share of false discoveries among the results called significant at a chosen level. Excludes a guarantee that no result in the set is a false discovery. full definition [3] over the study's eight primary tests.

Controls and planted checks

The sanity control shuffles memorability scores across all images and refits the full model, 1,000 times. Its 95th percentile is an R2 of -0.012, so a model with no real signal scores below zero on held-out images, and the real content-only R2 of 0.558 is far above it.

The content-matched shuffle is the control that matters for the claim. It keeps each image's subject and score and gives it the colour of another image of the same subcategory. Its gains have a mean of -0.0002 and a 95th percentile of +0.0001. Any gain the real colours show beyond that comes from colour varying with memorability inside subcategories.

This test had no planted checkDefinition: planted check research method; called a positive control in experimental science Data built so that the right answer is known, run through the same analysis before the real data, to show that the analysis can see the effect when it is there. Excludes any check made on the real data, which cannot show what the analysis misses. full definition: no known colour effect was inserted to measure what size the method can see. The shuffle bars stand in for it. A within-subcategory correlation has to exceed about 0.019 to clear its shuffle, so effects smaller than that are not excluded.

Results

Adding lightness, chroma and colour temperature lifts held-out R2 by 0.0022 (95% interval 0.0009 to 0.0034), from 0.558 to 0.560; the content-matched shuffle reaches 0.0001 at its 95th percentile.c1 The p value is 0.001, the smallest the 1,000 draws allow, and 0.003 after correction. A secondary model with six colour properties (adding colourfulness, the spread of lightness and hue entropy) gains 0.0033 over content.

Within a subcategory each property correlates weakly with memorability: lightness +0.027, chroma +0.041 and log colour temperature -0.056, each above a shuffle bar near 0.019.c2 Table 2 gives the intervals and Figure 1 sets each value beside the same correlation taken across all images with no subject control. The squared correlation for colour temperature is 0.003, so it accounts for about 0.3 percent of the differences in memorability inside a subcategory.

Colour alone, with no subject label, predicts memorability at R2 0.096, about 44 times the gain it adds once subcategory is known.c3 Across content, cooler images are less memorable at a correlation of -0.307; inside a subcategory the same correlation is -0.056. Most of the across-content link is carried by which subjects tend to be warm or cool.

The direction of the colour temperature link differs by category: +0.050 in food and between -0.041 and -0.113 in the other four.c4 Figure 2 shows all three properties by category. These per-category values are secondary, carry no verdict and have no intervals in the result file.

Discussion

The result agrees in direction with the weak colour link reported for earlier image sets and puts a size on it for MemCat with the subject controlled. A lift of 0.0022 in R2 against a baseline of 0.558 is a detectable effect of no practical use for predicting which photograph will be remembered. For the decision the study was run to inform, the answer was no: a colour control aimed at memorability is not worth building on this evidence.

The contrast between the two columns of Table 2 is the more useful part. A reader who correlated colour temperature with memorability across the whole set would find -0.307 and might call warmth memorable. Fixing the subcategory cuts that to under a fifth of its size. The same care applies to any image property that differs between subjects.

The remaining link should not be read as colour acting on memory. A subcategory such as a kind of landscape still mixes scenes, and a snowy scene differs from a green one in both colour and content. The food category runs opposite to the others, which fits a reading in which the property stands for what is in the picture.

Limitations

  • One image set and one memory task. Memorability here is recognition in a repeat-detection game over minutes, and it may not carry to memory in daily life or to other image sets.
  • Subcategory is coarse content. Colour inside a subcategory can still stand for scene content, so the within-subcategory link is an upper bound on what colour itself contributes.
  • The models are linear in three whole-frame averages. Colour layout, contrast between regions and nonlinear effects were not tested.
  • The test had no planted check, so the smallest effect the method could detect is known only from the shuffle bars.
  • The per-category values and the six-property gain are secondary and have no intervals or corrected p values.
  • The correction was made over eight tests, four of them in parts of the study not published here.
  • Three of the study's four tests are left out: one rests on private images, one was untestable, and one rests on data that is not public.
  • Scoring was by our own code and was not blind. The check with the other two properties held fixed was made after the results were seen.

Evidence

Mixed. Colour's gain beyond subject is 0.0022 of R2 (interval 0.0009 to 0.0034) against a subject-only R2 of 0.558; within-subcategory correlations are 0.027, 0.041 and -0.056, so each property explains under 0.4 percent of the differences. The study's seasonal test on private images failed its four verdicts and its mood test was untestable; neither is on this page.
