Titius-Bode regularity in 122 exoplanet systems sits in gap sizes; their order adds nothing

Exoplanet systems do look more regular than random gaps would make them, but shuffling each system's own gaps into a new order leaves that regularity in place. A planet's position is predicted better by its two neighbours than by a Titius-Bode fit.

What this means for a reader

If a paper says a Titius-BodeDefinition: Titius-Bode rule planetary astronomy Any formula that places a system's planets at distances or periods growing by a near-constant ratio from one planet to the next, here fitted in three forms to the log of orbital period. Excludes a physical model of how planets form or migrate. fit predicts a missing planet at a given period, ask what the two neighbouring planets alone would predict. A Titius-Bode rule, in planetary astronomy, is any formula that places a system's planets at distances or periods growing by a near-constant ratio from one to the next; it is a description of where planets sit and carries no model of how they formed or migrated. In our sample the neighbours win. A system's planets do tend to sit at similar spacing ratios, a pattern known from Kepler data as "peas in a podDefinition: peas in a pod exoplanet surveys The pattern, reported from Kepler data, that planets inside one system tend to be alike in size and in the ratios between neighbouring periods. Excludes a rule for the order of the gaps, and any likeness between planets of different systems." [8], and any rule that fits a geometric sequence will fit such a system well. That good fit is not evidence that the sequence itself is a law. The exoplanet survey phrase covers planets inside one system being alike in size and in spacing ratio; it states no rule for the order of the gaps.

Abstract

The Titius-Bode rule says planets sit at distances that grow by a steady ratio, and its generalised forms have been used to predict planets nobody has seen. We asked whether exoplanet spacing follows such a rule beyond what each system's own set of gaps explains. On 122 systems with four or more planets (543 planets) from the NASA Exoplanet Archive, three forms of the rule were scored against two controls fixed in advance. The real systems beat a control that draws gaps from the whole catalogue (p 0.0005, 2,000 draws), and none of the three forms beat a control that keeps each system's gaps and shuffles their order (p 0.85, 0.995 and 0.68).c1 When an interior planet is dropped, the geometric mean of its two neighbours predicts its period better than the rule does: median error 0.107 against 0.131 in log period, a ratio of 1.23.c2 The regularity is real, and it is the regularity of similar gaps inside one system. The order of those gaps adds nothing we could detect. The preregistered law-like verdict failed.

Introduction

Johann Titius noticed in 1766 that the planets then known sit near distances given by a simple doubling sequence, and Johann Bode popularised it. The rule matched Uranus and the asteroid belt and missed Neptune. It has been rewritten many times since. Blagg [2] fitted the Solar System and the moons of the giant planets with a periodic correction to a geometric progression; Richardson [3] gave another version.

The question of whether the rule means anything was put sharply by Hayes and Tremaine [4]. They drew random planetary systems, rejected the ones that would be unstable, and found that the survivors fit a Titius-Bode law about as well as the Solar System. Their conclusion was that stable systems tend to be regularly spaced, and that this is the whole content of the rule.

Exoplanets gave the rule new material. Bovaird and Lineweaver [5] fitted a generalised Titius-Bode relation to multi-planet systems, allowed for unseen planets in the gaps, and predicted 141 new planets. Huang and Bakos [6] searched the Kepler data for 97 of those predictions and found 5. Bovaird, Lineweaver and Jacobsen [7] replied that many predicted planets would not transit and should not be expected in the data, and ranked new predictions by their chance of transiting.

This paper adds one controlDefinition: control statistics and experimental design A version of the data with the structure under test removed (for example shuffled or pooled), scored the same way as the real data, so the real score has something to beat. Excludes a comparison with a different dataset or with a published number. to that argument. A control, in the sense of experimental design, is a copy of the data with one structure removed and scored exactly like the real data; a comparison with another catalogue or a published number would not count as one. A rule about a sequence should beat a version of the same system with its gaps put in a different order. If it does not, the rule describes the sizes of the gaps and nothing about their arrangement.

Methods

The three forms

For a system with planets sorted by period, let Pn be the period of the nth planet. The plain form is log-linear in the index:

ln⁡Pn=a+bn(1)

The Blagg-Richardson style form adds an offset period P0, a three-parameter form in period:

ln⁡(Pn−P0)=a+bn(2)

The gapped form, in the style of Bovaird and Lineweaver, replaces the index by integers kn that may skip up to two places, so that up to two unseen planets can be inserted wherever they lower the residual:

ln⁡Pn=a+bkn(3)

All three forms were written from the published descriptions as remembered, not copied from the papers. Blagg's own formula has a periodic correction term that eq. 2 does not carry. The result is therefore about these three versions.

The regularity score

For system s, let σs be the root mean square residualDefinition: root mean square residual statistics The square root of the mean squared difference between fitted and observed log period across a system's planets, a measure of scatter about the fit. Excludes any offset or trend the fit has already absorbed into its parameters. of the fit in ln⁡P, and gs the mean gap in ln⁡P between neighbouring planets. The per-system statistic divides one by the other, so that systems with wide and narrow spacing are compared on one scale. The residual term is from statistics: the square root of the mean squared difference between fitted and observed ln⁡P, a measure of scatter about the fit that leaves out any offset the fit has already absorbed:

rs=σsgs(4)

The sample score S is minus the median of rs over the systems, so a higher score means more regular. Each form has its own score.

Prediction by leave-one-out

For each interior planet j, the planet is dropped, the plain form is fitted to the rest, and the fitted ln⁡Pj is compared with the true value. The baseline predicts ln⁡Pj as the mean of its two neighbours' ln⁡P, which is the geometric meanDefinition: geometric mean statistics For two numbers, the square root of their product, which is the plain average taken on a log scale; here the neighbour baseline for a dropped planet's period. Excludes the ordinary average of the two periods, which is larger whenever they differ. of their periods. The geometric mean of two numbers is the square root of their product, the plain average taken on a log scale; it is smaller than the ordinary average whenever the two differ. The ratio of median absolute errors is

L=MfitMnb(5)

where Mfit is the median error of the fit and Mnb that of the neighbour baseline. A ratio above 1 means the neighbours win.

Controls

The order-shuffle control keeps each system's set of gaps in ln⁡P and puts them in a random order, then rebuilds the periods from the innermost planet. The pooled-gap control rebuilds each system from gaps drawn at random from all systems' gaps. Each control is scored with the same form and the same freedom as the real data; for the gapped form that includes the inserted planets. The plain form's controls ran 2,000 draws each and the other two forms' order controls ran 200 each.

The p valueDefinition: p value (from draws) statistics The share of control draws scoring at or above the real score, with one added to the count and to the number of draws, so it is never zero. Excludes a p value from a textbook formula that assumes a distribution. reported for each comparison is the share of control draws scoring at or above the real score, counted with one added to both the numerator and the denominator. It is a p value from draws, as in permutation statistics, and assumes no textbook distribution:

p=k+1n+1(6)

where k is the number of draws at or above the real score and n the number of draws. This is why the smallest possible value at 2,000 draws is 0.0005.

Controls and planted checks

The instrument was checked on planted systems before the real data were read. Planted systems have exactly log-linear periods with 5 percent jitter and the same numbers of planets as the real systems. They scored -0.050 against the pooled-gap control's 95th percentile of -0.190, so the score sees regularity when it is there.c3 The void condition did not fire.

Discussion

Hayes and Tremaine [4] argued that a Titius-Bode law is what stable spacing looks like. Our result is a direct check of one consequence of that view on exoplanets. If the rule only describes similar gaps, then a system with its gaps shuffled should fit as well as the real one. It does, for three forms.

The prediction test points the same way. A rule that captured a law of sequence should predict a missing planet better than a local average of its neighbours, because it uses the whole system. It predicts worse. Local spacing carries the information, which fits the correlated period ratios of neighbouring planets reported by Weiss and colleagues [8].

This bears on the prediction debate between Bovaird and Lineweaver [5] [7] and Huang and Bakos [6] in one way. A predicted planet in a gap is a claim that the gaps should be more equal than they look. That claim can be useful as a search target. It does not need a law of sequence, and it should be weighed against the neighbour baseline rather than against no prediction at all.

Limitations

  • The three forms were written from memory of the published ones, and a later review of our own ledger flagged that. A published form that differs could score differently, so the result covers these three versions only. Blagg's formula with its periodic term was not tested.
  • Transit surveys miss long periods and tilted planets, so the gaps are survey-shaped. The order shuffle keeps that effect, which is why it is the fair control, but the pooled-gap result is partly a survey result.
  • The catalogue's multi-planet entries are a selected set, and the sample is 122 systems, short of the 150 we forecast.
  • The 200-draw controls for the two richer forms give coarse p values.
  • The finding says nothing about the physics that sets spacing.
  • Scoring was done by our own code and was not blind.

Evidence

Honest null. Against each system's own gaps put in a new order, none of the three forms scored above the shuffled range (p 0.85, 0.995 and 0.68). Regularity in gap sizes is shown; an ordering law is not.
