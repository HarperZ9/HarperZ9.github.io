Forecasters who skip questions score better, and the best filter reads the probability

In 410,451 forecasts from the Good Judgment Project, a filter that holds back forecasts by their stated probability cut the average error far more than one that reads the forecaster's own expertise rating. People who skip more questions also score better on their first answers, though a person who skips more in one year is not clearly better for it.

What this means for a reader

When a forecaster, a panel or a software system has to choose which answers to withhold, the number it already states is a much better guide than how expert it says it is. In a large public forecasting tournament, a filter on the stated probability cut the error of kept forecasts about six times as much as a filter on self-rated expertise, and the self-rating added nothing a shuffled rating did not also add. People who answer selectively are better forecasters on average, so a habit of skipping marks a careful source. The data does not show that skipping a question makes the remaining answers better.

Objection

Inferred skips may be questions a forecaster never saw; matched strata may explain any difference in sign from Merkle and colleagues, whose sign we have not confirmed; a filter on stated probability rewards confidence by construction.

What would change the call. Replicate on another tournament where question exposure is visible; read Merkle and colleagues in full.

From the strongest objection recorded in our publication register.

Abstract

A source that may decline to answer needs a rule for which answers to hold back. We tested two candidate inputs for that rule on the public survey forecasts of the Good Judgment Project: the probability the forecaster stated, and the forecaster's own 1 to 5 rating of their expertise on that forecast. On 410,451 binary forecasts, filters were scored by the area under the risk-coverage curve, the mean Brier score of kept forecasts as coverage falls from 100 to 10 percent. Introduction and motivation

The Good Judgment Project ran from 2011 to 2015 as one of several research teams in a geopolitical forecasting tournament. Thousands of volunteers gave probabilities for world events, and the project tested training, teaming and tracking of top performers as ways to raise accuracy [2]. The survey forecasts, with each forecaster's self-rated expertise on each forecast for the first three years, are public.

Forecasters in the project chose which questions to answer. That choice is part of the data. Merkle, Steyvers, Mellers and Tetlock [3] asked whether good and poor forecasters choose questions differently, and whether those choices can sharpen estimates of a forecaster's ability beyond what a proper scoring ruleDefinition: proper scoring rule decision theory and forecast verification A score for probability forecasts on which a forecaster does best in expectation by stating their true belief. Excludes scores that pay for shading a forecast toward some answer, and any rule for which questions to answer. full definition reads from the forecasts alone. A proper scoring rule, in decision theory, is a score that a forecaster does best on in expectation by stating their true belief; a rule that pays for shading toward a favoured answer is not proper. Sempere and Lawsen [5] showed that a mean Brier scoreDefinition: Brier score forecast verification, from weather forecasting The squared difference between the stated probabilities and the outcomes, here summed over the answer options in the GJP form so it runs from 0 to 2. Lower is better. Excludes questions left unanswered, which it does not score. full definition gives a forecaster a reason to skip. The Brier score, from weather forecasting, is the squared difference between the stated probabilities and what happened, so lower is better; it scores each answer given and says nothing about questions left unanswered: a forecaster whose average Brier score is b2 should not forecast any question whose true probability lies between b and 1−b, even when they know it exactly. Skipping and scoring are therefore tangled before any data is read.

Machine learning has a matching problem under the name selective predictionDefinition: selective prediction machine learning Prediction with a reject option, where a system answers only the inputs it ranks most likely to be right and declines the rest. Excludes how the kept answers are produced. full definition. A classifier with a reject option keeps the inputs it is most confident on and declines the rest; the term covers that choice of what to answer and leaves out how the kept answers are made. Its quality is drawn as a risk-coverage curveDefinition: risk-coverage curve machine learning, selective prediction The error on kept items against the share kept. Here risk is the mean Brier score of kept forecasts and coverage the share kept. Excludes any score for the forecasts withheld. full definition, the error on kept inputs against the share kept [6]. Here risk is the mean Brier score of kept forecasts and coverage the share kept, so the curve says nothing about the forecasts withheld. We use the same curve to score abstain filters for human forecasts.

The third question concerns the hedge. An earlier round of this study found that a forecast a person rates as expertise 1 is worse than the same person's higher-rated forecasts and sits nearer 50 percent. ExtremisingDefinition: extremising forecast aggregation Pushing a probability away from 50 percent by multiplying its log-odds by a factor a, with a below 1 pulling it inward instead. Excludes changing which side a forecast leans to. full definition, the transformation Baron, Mellers and colleagues studied for aggregated forecasts [4], pushes a probability away from 50 percent by scaling its log-odds by a fixed factor, and a factor below 1 pulls it inward instead; it changes no forecast's direction. It can test whether that pull toward 50 percent is too large, too small or the right size.

The study was built to inform one design choice: how the abstain filter in Flywheel, our accountability software, should rank a source's answers. Should it credit a source for abstaining, and does it need the source's self-rating beside the stated probability?

Methods

Scoring

Each forecast is scored with the Brier score in the GJP form, summed over the answer options, so that it runs from 0 to 2:

B=∑k(pk−ok)2(1)

where pk is the stated probability of option k and ok is 1 if that option occurred and 0 otherwise. The relative scoreDefinition: relative Brier score forecast verification, our usage A forecaster's mean, over the questions they answered, of their first-forecast Brier score minus the median first-forecast Brier score on that question. Lower is better. Excludes the difficulty of the question, which subtracting the median removes. full definition of forecaster i on first forecasts is the mean, over the ni questions they answered, of their first-forecast Brier score minus the median first-forecast Brier score on that question. It is our measure of skill against the crowd on the same question, and it leaves out how hard the question was, which the median absorbs:

Si=1ni∑q(Biq−mq)(2)

The time-matched version Si,t takes the median mq only over first forecasts made in the same tenth of the question's open period, drops cells with fewer than 5 first forecasts, and needs 5 or more valid questions per forecaster-yearDefinition: forecaster-year our usage One person's forecasts within one tournament year, the unit for skip rate and relative score. Excludes pooling of different people, and pooling one person's years. full definition, our unit of analysis: one person's forecasts within one tournament year, so a person active in three years counts three times and no two people are pooled. Lower S is better, so the tests correlate skip rate with −S.

Skip rate and the matched correlation

A forecaster-year is eligible with at least 10 available questions and 5 answered. Its skip rate is

si=1−nansnav(3)

where nans is the number of available questions answered and nav the number available. Busy forecasters answer more, so the correlation is taken inside strataDefinition: stratum survey statistics A group of units alike on the matched variables, here year and twentieth of forecast count, inside which a statistic is computed before averaging. Excludes control for variables that were not matched. full definition h of year and twentieth of forecast count, each with 10 or more forecaster-years, and averaged with weights wh equal to stratum size. A stratum, a term from survey statistics, is a group of units alike on the matched variables, so comparisons inside it cannot be driven by those variables; it does nothing about variables left unmatched:

ρM=∑hwhρh∑hwh(4)

where ρh is the Spearman correlationDefinition: Spearman correlation (rho) rank statistics The correlation between the ranks of two quantities, from -1 to +1, which reads any steady rise or fall. Excludes the shape of the relation and the pull of outliers on the raw values. full definition of si with −Si inside stratum h. Spearman's rho, from rank statistics, is the correlation of the ranks of two quantities, so it reads any steady rise or fall and ignores the shape of the relation and the size of outliers. IntervalsDefinition: 95% interval statistics The range that holds the middle 95 percent of the bootstrap or control draws for a number. We report it beside the number. Excludes a statement that the true value lies inside with 95 percent probability. full definition come from 1,000 bootstrapDefinition: bootstrap statistics Recomputing a number many times on data resampled with replacement, here within strata or over questions, to see how much it varies. Excludes an interval from a textbook formula that assumes a distribution. full definition draws within strata. Each 95 percent interval is the range holding the middle 95 percent of the draws, and it is no statement that the true value lies inside with 95 percent probability. The bootstrap, a resampling method from statistics, recomputes the number on data resampled with replacement and assumes no textbook distribution.

The within-person test takes pairs of consecutive years in which a person kept the same majority condition. Inside each year pair it computes the Spearman correlation of the change in skip rate with the change in time-matched score, and averages over year pairs weighted by pair count. Changing condition between years would change the platform and the questions, so mixed pairs are left out of the primary test.

The size of the hedge

For a stated probability p the extremised probability is

p′=papa+(1−p)a(5)

so the log-oddsDefinition: log-odds statistics For a probability p, the natural log of p divided by 1 minus p. 0 at 50 percent and unbounded toward certainty. Excludes any information beyond the probability, of which it is a rescaling. full definition are multiplied by a. The log-odds of a probability p, a term from statistics, is ln⁡(p/(1−p)), which is 0 at 50 percent and grows without bound toward certainty; it is a rescaling of the probability and adds no information to it. A value a above 1 means forecasts were too hedged, below 1 too extreme, and 1 right-sized. The factor is fitted by minimum Brier score on a grid from 0.30 to 4.00 in steps of 0.01. Questions were split once at random into two halves; the held-out gainDefinition: held-out gain machine learning The drop in Brier score from extremising, with the factor fitted on one half of the questions and scored on the other half. Excludes the in-sample improvement any fitted factor gives on the data it was fitted to. full definition is the Brier score at a=1 minus the Brier score at the a fitted on the other half, summed over both halves and taken per forecast. Held-out, in machine-learning usage, means scored on data the fit never saw, which excludes the in-sample improvement any fitted factor gives. The primary contrast uses the first round's within-person sample: forecaster-years with at least 5 forecasts at expertise 1 and 5 at expertise 2 to 5. Intervals come from 1,000 bootstrap draws over questions.

Abstain filters

A filter ranks forecasts by predicted Brier score and keeps the best-ranked share c, the coverage. Its risk R(c) is the mean raw Brier score of the kept forecasts. Each predictor is an out-of-foldDefinition: out-of-fold prediction machine learning, cross-validation A prediction for each item made from a fit on the other folds only. Excludes any use of the item's own outcome to predict it. full definition cell mean, computed for each forecast from the other folds only, so no forecast helps predict itself: the self-rating filter uses the five expertise levels, the probability filter ten bins of distance from 0.5 in steps of 0.05, and the combined filter their 50 crossed cells, falling back to the probability bin where a cell has fewer than 30 training forecasts. Ties are broken by one fixed random key. The summary is the area under the risk-coverage curveDefinition: area under the risk-coverage curve machine learning, selective prediction The mean risk over coverages from 10 to 100 percent in steps of 5, one number for a whole filter. Lower is better. Excludes coverages below 10 percent. full definition, taken as the mean risk over 19 coverages. The area is a selective-prediction summary of a whole filter in one number, lower being better, and it excludes coverages below 10 percent:

A=119∑jR(cj)(6)

with j running from 1 to 19 and cj=0.05(j+1), so coverage runs from 0.10 to 1.00. Lower is better. A random filter keeps the overall mean at every coverage.

Controls and p values

Every shuffle control is scored with the same code as the real data. The p valueDefinition: p value (from draws) statistics The share of control draws scoring at or above the real score, with one added to the count and to the number of draws, so it is never zero. Excludes a p value from a textbook formula that assumes a distribution. full definition of a comparison, a p value from draws as in permutation statistics, assumes no textbook distribution and counts the control draws at or above the real value, with one added to the numerator and the denominator:

p=k+1n+1(7)

where k is the number of draws at or above the real value and n the number of draws. At 1,000 draws the smallest possible value is 0.001.

Seven controls were fixed in the preregistration. Each breaks one link and keeps the rest of the data. The scores a control gives over its draws form a null distribution, whose 95th percentile is the bar a real score must clear; it is drawn from these data, never assumed from theory.

Discussion

The filter result has a plain mechanism. A forecast near 0 or 1 scores well when it is right, and calibrated forecasters are right about as often as they say. Ranking by stated probability therefore keeps the forecasts that will score best, and part of the gain is arithmetic: the filter rewards confidence by construction. This is the same arithmetic Sempere and Lawsen [5] point to when they show that a mean Brier score pays a forecaster to skip questions in the middle of the probability range. For a filter that only needs to lower the error of what it emits, the arithmetic is the point. For a claim that confident forecasters know more, it is not evidence.

The self-rating adds little because the stated probability already carries it. The first round read "worse and nearer 0.5" as the rating carrying information past the probability. The hedge results settle that point. Expertise-1 forecasts are pulled toward 50 percent by the amount their information warrants, and their worse Brier score is what a calibrated, less informed forecast scores. Once a filter sees the probability, the per-forecast rating adds nothing a within-person shuffle does not also add. The one place the rating says something the probability does not is at the top: forecasters who rate themselves 4 or 5 are over-extreme, so a top rating works as a warning sign for overconfidence.

On skipping, the data separate a trait from an act. People who habitually answer selectively score better on their first forecasts, before any update, at matched timing, in teams and individuals alike. A person who skips more this year than last does not clearly gain from it. Skip rate works as a marker of a careful forecaster; the data do not show that abstaining improves an answer. Merkle and colleagues [3] asked whether good and poor forecasters select questions differently in the same tournament. An earlier search note of ours recorded that they found good forecasters selecting more questions, a sign that runs against ours before matching. We read their abstract only, so we cannot confirm the sign or say whether our forecast-count strata, which compare people of equal activity, account for the difference.

For the filter design, the decision mapping fixed in advance gives a direct reading. The filter ranks by stated probability. It does not take the self-rating as an add-on; a high self-rating is at most a flag to soften a forecast. A habit of abstaining can raise the weight given to a source, but abstention is not credited as something that improves an answer. One setting serves teams and individuals. All of this is human data. For model sources it sets the design to test, not the setting: a model's stated probabilities need their own check against outcomes.

Limitations

  • Skips are inferred. A question counted as skipped may have been unseen, or outside the forecaster's condition; "available" is any closed question open during their active window.
  • Nothing here is causal. People chose their ratings, their questions and when to forecast.
  • The within-person interval reaches +0.095, which leaves room for a small benefit of skipping. Between years the platform, the question mix and the training also changed; same-condition pairs only reduce this.
  • Updating, timing and selection remain tangled in the cross-section: skip rate correlates +0.452 with updates per question inside strata.
  • A probability filter lowers Brier partly by arithmetic, as the discussion sets out.
  • The filters were simulated after the fact on forecasts people chose to make with no filter in place. A live filter would change behaviour.
  • Year 4 has no self-ratings, prediction-market trades were not used, and nothing here speaks for language models.
  • Merkle and colleagues studied question selection in the same tournament and may disagree on the sign; we read only their abstract.
  • Scoring was done by our own code and was not blind.

Evidence

Held. Large real data with working shuffle controls and a preregistration. Skips are inferred and causation is open, and a filter on stated probability lowers the Brier score partly by arithmetic. Merkle and colleagues (2017) is close prior work on selection.

Steps

  1. Data: download the GJP survey forecasts (years 1 to 4) and the question file from the Harvard Dataverse, doi:10.7910/DVN/BPCDH5.
  2. Drop withdrawals, forecasts on voided or unresolved questions, forecasts dated after close and unrepairable probabilities. Score each forecast with eq. 1.
  3. For each forecaster-year with at least 10 available and 5 answered questions, compute the skip rate (eq. 3) and the first-forecast relative score (eq. 2), plain and time-matched. Take the matched correlation (eq. 4) inside year and forecast-count strata, and the within-person version on same-condition consecutive-year pairs.
  4. Fit the extremising factor (eq. 5) by grid search with a random split of questions into halves.
  5. Rank binary forecasts from years 1 to 3 by out-of-fold predicted Brier from self-rating, probability bin and both, and take the area (eq. 6). Shuffle the rating within forecaster-year and refit as the control.
  6. Seed 2026102902 (numpy generator, consumed in the preregistered order).
