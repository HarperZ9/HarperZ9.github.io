Forecasts people rate low are worse and more hedged, and people who skip more score better

In 1,064,355 Good Judgment Project forecasts, a forecast its author rated low was a little worse and already nearer 50 percent, and people who skipped more questions scored better on the ones they answered. Both are associations, and the follow-up study F32 narrowed both: the stated probability already carries what the self-rating knows, and no within-person benefit of skipping was shown.

What this means for a reader

When people in a large forecasting tournament said they knew little about a question, their forecast was a little worse, and they had already moved it toward 50 percent. People who answered fewer of the questions open to them scored better on the ones they did answer. Both patterns are associations in people who chose their own ratings and questions. The follow-up study found that the stated probability already carries what the self-rating knows, and that a person who skips more in one year than the last does not clearly score better, so a habit of skipping marks a careful forecaster and is not shown to improve an answer.

Objection

Nothing here is causal. People chose their ratings, their questions and when to forecast.

What would change the call. Read together with F32, and repeat on data that records when a forecaster declined a question.

From the paper's first stated limitation; our register records no separate objection.

Abstract

A source that can say "I am not sure" or decline to answer is only useful if those signals mean something. We tested both on the public survey forecasts of the Good Judgment Project, where volunteers rated their own expertise from 1 to 5 on each forecast and chose which questions to answer. Introduction

The Good Judgment Project ran from 2011 to 2015 inside a geopolitical forecasting tournament. Thousands of volunteers gave probabilities for world events, and the project tested training, teaming and tracking as ways to raise accuracy [2]. Its survey forecasts are public, and for the first three years each forecast carries the forecaster's own 1 to 5 rating of their expertise on that question.

Two things in those files bear on a practical design question: how software that collects answers from many sources should treat a source's stated confidence, and whether it should let a source decline. The first is the self-rating. A review of ways to identify skilled forecasters lists the same 1 to 5 item among expertise-based predictors and attributes it to the project's own papers [5]. The second is the choice of questions. Merkle, Steyvers, Mellers and Tetlock [3] pointed out that a Brier scoreDefinition: Brier score forecast verification, from weather forecasting The squared difference between the stated probabilities and the outcomes, here summed over the answer options in the Good Judgment Project form so it runs from 0 to 2. Lower is better. Excludes questions left unanswered, which it does not score. full definition, the squared difference between stated probabilities and outcomes, looks only at the forecasts a person submitted, and asked whether the questions a person selects add information about their ability. A Brier score is lower when better, and it gives no score to a question left unanswered.

We asked two questions of the data. Within one person, does a low self-rating mark a worse forecast, and is that forecast already hedged? Across people, do those who skip more score better on what they answer? The decision the study informed was whether an abstain filter in Flywheel, our accountability software, can use a source's own confidence report or needs an outside measure. Our searches for this paper returned the two works above and the project's own account of its best forecasters [4]; none of the search results gave an effect size for the self-rating inside one person, which is what a search returned and no proof that none is published.

Data and provenance

The data are the Good Judgment Project survey forecasts for years 1 to 4 and the question file, published on the Harvard Dataverse under CC0 1.0Definition: CC0 1.0 copyright and data licensing The Creative Commons public domain dedication, under which anyone may reuse the work for any purpose without permission. The survey files hold 3,143,460 rows, one per answer option. Dropping 43,944 withdrawal rows left 1,234,940 forecasts. Only aggregates and distributions were stored; no value for any single forecaster was written to any output.

The files hold no record of a declined question. Methods

Scores

Each forecast is scored with the Brier score in the project's form, summed over the answer options, so it runs from 0 to 2:

B=∑k(pk−ok)2(1)

where pk is the stated probability of option k and ok is 1 if that option occurred and 0 otherwise. The relative Brier scoreDefinition: relative Brier score forecast verification, our usage A forecast's Brier score minus the median Brier score of all scored survey forecasts on the same question. Lower is better. Excludes the difficulty of the question, which subtracting the median removes. full definition of a forecast is B minus the median B of all scored survey forecasts on the same question, so it measures skill against the crowd and leaves out how hard the question was. The secondary form takes the median inside the question and the fifth of its open period in which the forecast was made. ExtremityDefinition: extremity judgment and forecasting research The distance of a binary forecast from 50 percent, from 0 for a coin-flip forecast to 0.5 for a certain one. Excludes whether the forecast was right, and how well calibrated the forecaster is. full definition is the distance of a binary forecast from 50 percent, |p−0.5|, from 0 to 0.5; it measures how hedged a forecast is and says nothing about whether it was right. The unit is the forecaster-yearDefinition: forecaster-year our usage One person's forecasts within one tournament year, the unit for every statistic here. Excludes pooling of different people, and pooling one person's years. full definition, one person's forecasts within one tournament year, so a person active in three years counts three times.

The within-person tests

The positive controlDefinition: positive control experimental design A test of an effect expected to be present, which the instrument must detect for its other readings to be trusted. Excludes a shuffle control, which removes an effect instead. full definition tests an effect that should be there for the other readings to be trusted: that self-rated expertise goes with accuracy at all. For forecaster-years with at least 10 scored binary forecasts using two or more rating levels, relative Brier score and rating were both centred within the forecaster-year, and one slope β was fitted to the pooled data. The reported quantity is −β, so a positive value means a higher rating goes with a better score.

The low-rating test uses forecaster-years with at least 5 forecasts rated 1 and 5 rated 2 to 5. For each it takes

dB=Slow−Srest(2)

where Slow is the mean relative Brier score of the forecasts rated 1 and Srest that of the forecasts rated 2 to 5, and the matching difference in extremity, dE. The statistic is the mean over forecaster-years. The preregistered bar for dB was 0.02 on the 0 to 2 scale.

Skip rate and the matched correlation

A forecaster-year is eligible with at least 10 available questions and 5 answered. Its skip rate is

s=1−nansnav(3)

with nans the available questions answered and nav the number available. Busy forecasters answer more, so the correlation is taken inside each stratumDefinition: stratum survey statistics A group of units alike on the matched variables, here year and twentieth of forecast count, inside which a statistic is computed before averaging. Excludes control for variables that were not matched. full definition h of year and twentieth of forecast count, a group alike on the matched variables, and averaged with weights wh equal to stratum size:

ρM=∑hwhρh∑hwh(4)

where ρh is the Spearman rhoDefinition: Spearman rho statistics The correlation between the ranks of two quantities, from -1 to 1, which counts any steadily rising or falling relation and is not pulled by single extreme values. Excludes the slope of a relation, and evidence of cause. full definition, the correlation of ranks, between skip rate and the negative of the forecaster-year's mean relative score. Strata with fewer than 10 forecaster-years were dropped. Matching controls only the matched variables.

Intervals

Every 95 percent intervalDefinition: 95% interval statistics The range that holds the middle 95 percent of the bootstrap or control draws for a number. We report it beside the number. Excludes a statement that the true value lies inside with 95 percent probability. full definition is the middle 95 percent of bootstrapDefinition: bootstrap statistics Recomputing a statistic many times on forecaster-years resampled with replacement to read how much the estimate varies. Excludes an interval from a textbook formula that assumes a distribution. full definition draws, a resampling of forecaster-years with replacement that assumes no textbook distribution: 2,000 draws for the low-rating test and 1,000 for the others, the skip test resampling inside strata.

Controls and planted checks

Each controlDefinition: control statistics and experimental design A version of the data with the structure under test removed (for example shuffled or pooled), scored the same way as the real data, so the real score has something to beat. Excludes a comparison with a different dataset or with a published number. full definition is a copy of the data with one link broken by shuffling and scored with the same code; a comparison with another dataset is not a control. Three were fixed in advance, with 1,000 draws each, and the 95th percentile of each one's null distributionDefinition: null distribution statistics The spread of scores a control gives over many random draws. Its 95th percentile is the bar a real score must clear. Excludes a theoretical distribution assumed rather than drawn from the data. full definition is the bar the real value must clear.

  • Ratings shuffled within forecaster-year, the null for the mean of dB.
  • Ratings shuffled within forecaster-year, the null for the positive-control slope.
  • Skip rate shuffled within year and forecast-count strata, the null for ρM.

The activity check asks whether matching did its job: the mean correlation of skip rate with forecast count inside strata must be under 0.2 in size. A p value from drawsDefinition: p value (from draws) statistics The share of control draws scoring at or above the real score, with one added to the count and to the number of draws, so it is never zero. Excludes a p value from a textbook formula that assumes a distribution. full definition is the share of control draws at or above the real value, with one added to the count and to the number of draws, so 1,000 draws cannot give less than 0.001.

Results

Self-rating inside one person

The positive control passed. Within a forecaster-year, one point of self-rated expertise went with a relative Brier score better by 0.0226 (95 percent interval 0.0185 to 0.0270) across 3,780 forecaster-years.c1 The shuffle's 95th percentile was 0.0015 (p 0.001), and the test covered 398,463 forecasts. Each point of rating also went with a forecast 0.037 further from 50 percent (0.034 to 0.040).

Forecasts rated expertise 1 scored 0.0218 worse (0.0155 to 0.0285) than the same person's forecasts rated 2 to 5 and sat 0.055 nearer to 50 percent (0.051 to 0.058), across 1,826 forecaster-years.c2 The sample held 243,486 forecasts, 77,572 of them rated 1. The score gap cleared the shuffle's 95th percentile of 0.0048 (p 0.001) and cleared the 0.02 bar by 0.0018. The verdict still failed, because the prediction was "worse and no less hedged" and the extremity rule required the lower bound of dE to be above -0.02; it was -0.058. The effect is small beside the spread between people: the low-rated forecasts were the worse ones in 55 percent of forecaster-years, and the median gap was 0.018.

With question-by-time medians the gap fell to 0.0156 (0.0094 to 0.0219), under the preregistered bar of 0.02.c3 Figure 1 and Table 1 give the three within-person effects.

Skipping

Across the 9,204 eligible forecaster-years (1,781; 1,114; 1,360 and 4,949 in years 1 to 4) the median skip rate was 0.59. At matched forecast count, skip rate correlated with better relative Brier score at rho +0.351 (+0.331 to +0.368) across 9,204 forecaster-years.c4 The shuffle's 95th percentile was +0.017 (p 0.001). The activity check passed: skip rate correlated -0.613 with forecast count overall and -0.078 inside strata. Matching matters to the size of the number. Unmatched, the correlation was +0.119. Matched on questions answered in place of forecasts it was +0.236 (+0.216 to +0.252), and with question-by-time medians +0.211 (+0.188 to +0.229). Figure 2 draws the four.

Inside the same strata skip rate correlated +0.452 with updates per answered question, so this round does not separate skipping from updating.c5 The preregistration named this before the run: at a fixed forecast count, a person who answers fewer questions must update each one more, and updating goes with accuracy in this tournament.

Discussion

The self-rating means something inside one person: a forecast rated low is a worse forecast. People had also acted on that knowledge already, since the low-rated forecasts sit nearer 50 percent. The decision mapping written before the run read this outcome as "self-report modulates the stated probability and is usable as one input" to a filter. The first-round write-up went one step further and read "worse and hedged" as the rating carrying information past the stated probability.

The second round, F32, tested that step and took it back. It found that forecasts rated 1 are pulled toward 50 percent by about the right amount, with a best rescaling factor of 0.92 (0.79 to 1.06) where 1 means right-sized, and that adding the self-rating to a filter on the stated probability gained 0.0026 in area under the risk-coverage curve against a bar of 0.005, while a rating shuffled within each forecaster-year gained 0.0024. A calibrated forecast from a less informed person scores worse, and its stated probability already says by how much.

F32 also removed the confound on skipping. Counting each person's first forecast on a question only, the matched correlation was +0.286 (+0.264 to +0.303), and +0.195 with timing matched as well, so most of the +0.351 reported here survives the removal of every update. Within the same person across consecutive years it was +0.041 (-0.007 to +0.095) over 1,578 pairs, which shows no benefit to a person from skipping more. The supported reading is a trait: people who answer selectively tend to be better forecasters.

Merkle and colleagues [3] studied question selection in the same tournament. We read their abstract only, in a search snippet that was cut short, so we do not compare signs or sizes with their result.

Limitations

  • Nothing here is causal. People chose their ratings, their questions and when to forecast.
  • Skips are inferred. A question counted as skipped may have been unseen, and "available" means any closed question open during the forecaster's active window.
  • The skip correlation in this round includes updates. F32 puts the first-forecast value at +0.286 and found no within-person effect.
  • The low-rating gap clears its 0.02 bar by 0.0018, with an interval from 0.0155 to 0.0285, and falls under the bar with question-by-time medians. The 0.02 bar was a choice.
  • The reading that the self-rating adds information past the stated probability did not survive F32.
  • Year 4 has no self-ratings, prediction-market trades were not used, and nothing here speaks for language models or for any named person.
  • Scoring was done by our own code and was not blind.

Evidence

Mixed. The prediction that low-rated forecasts are worse and no less hedged failed: they sat 0.055 nearer 50 percent (interval 0.051 to 0.058) against a limit of 0.02. With question-by-time medians the score gap was 0.0156, under the 0.02 bar. The skip correlation of +0.351 includes updates; F32 puts it at +0.286 on first forecasts and found no within-person effect (+0.041, interval -0.007 to +0.095).

Steps

  1. Data: Good Judgment Project survey forecasts, years 1 to 4, and the question file, Harvard Dataverse, CC0 1.0, doi:10.7910/DVN/BPCDH5.
  2. Drop withdrawals, forecasts on voided or unresolved questions, forecasts dated after close and unrepairable probabilities. Score each forecast with eq. 1 and subtract the question's median score.
  3. Fit the pooled within-forecaster-year slope of relative score on rating. Compute eq. 2 and the extremity difference for forecaster-years with 5 or more forecasts in each arm.
  4. For each forecaster-year with at least 10 available and 5 answered questions, compute eq. 3 and take eq. 4 inside year and forecast-count strata.
  5. Shuffle ratings within forecaster-year and skip rates within strata, 1,000 times each, and rescore.
  6. Seed 20261029 (numpy generator, consumed in the preregistered order).
  7. Preregistration SHA-256: 723e0f541d7fe7ade90a45e04e6c3c15e853955f1a903c8ff5f75f2f952d12cb. Plan SHA-256: 71bedc68ab6171ff3526e5d0eaa0502204ee4891e1a58103345152786b7bb720. Run 20261029T142954Z.
