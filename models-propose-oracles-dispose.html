Models Propose, Oracles Dispose

A generated claim is a proposal. Nothing a model produces becomes evidence until something the model does not control has had its chance to say no.

Zain Dana Harper/July 29, 2026/2,042 words/Revised September 25, 2026/AI-assisted, author-directed, publicly disclosed

In short

Models crank out drafts and answers for next to nothing now. I don't think any of it should count as evidence until something the model doesn't control has had a fair shot at saying no. I call that something an oracle. When it says no, the claim stops, and the no gets written down on the spot. Why should you care? People cite and build on whatever they trust, so an unchecked claim rides along into every one of those uses. Sections 2 to 4 are how I build that check into my own software. Section 5 is where I stand, including the part where the rule bites me. The code is public at github.com/HarperZ9/buildlang if you'd rather check than take my word for it.

1. The rule

Four words: models propose, oracles dispose. The oracle is the independent check that decides. To dispose, in the old sense the title borrows, is to settle the outcome, and the disposition is that final call. Everything else I've got is commentary on where those four words draw the line.

A model can draft, translate, summarize and search for me, and I'm glad of the help in my shop. None of it is evidence. By the evidence layer I mean any place where later work trusts a result without checking it again, the stuff that gets cited and built on.

Between a model's output and that layer, something has to stand that can say no, and the proposer can't be the one holding its leash. Somebody skimming for tone doesn't count. A second model doesn't count either, because a second model agreeing is just a second proposal. When the check refuses, the claim stops at the door, and the refusal is written down when it happens.

Yes, the phrasing leans on the old proverb "Man proposes, God disposes." That's on purpose, because the structure is the old one: one party proposes, and another decides. A tireless proposer can be brilliant and right most of the time and still not get the last word. What machines changed is the price. Proposals used to be scarce, so treating a confident one as halfway to a finding was something you could survive. Now they're nearly free, and habits built for scarce proposals fail quietly when proposals show up by the truckload.

I get why people want to skip it. Checking is slow, and a model that's right most of the time feels like enough. But a second check is the whole difference between a layer of evidence and a pile of it. Let one generated result in on its own say-so and everybody downstream inherits a claim nobody ruled on, and it compounds with every reuse. So the rule sits at the door, built into the structure, where it keeps working on the days people forget to be careful. I'm one of those people.

2. A receipt that re-derives

Where I hold this line hardest right now is a compiler, the program that turns code into software a computer can run. It compiles buildlang, a programming language whose code is public. A program built with it prints its results and can also emit a receipt: a sealed record of what ran, what came out, and which invariant the run was checked against. Sealed means locked with a digital fingerprint, so any later change to the record shows. An invariant is a condition the run has to satisfy.

A separate program, the verifier, re-derives the result. It works the answer out again from whatever the sealed facts allow and compares. It checks the invariant again. It re-runs the program and compares the exit status, the code a program reports when it finishes, with the sealed one. If they agree, that's a verdict, with the full weight of one.

One record format holds this shape across five kinds of computation: fully deterministic runs, closed-form probability, seeded randomness, sampled estimates, and budgeted search. In plain terms, the same receipt works whether a program gives the same answer every time, works out an exact probability by formula, uses random numbers that can be replayed, estimates an answer from many random trials, or hunts for the best answer it can find on a fixed budget. One shape stretching that far still amazes me. The sampled cases carry their sample counts inside the seal, because an estimate that hides how many samples it rests on can't be checked.

Some facts can't be re-derived exactly, and for those you just say so. Wall-clock time, how long the run took by the clock, is the plain example. The verifier measures it again, then shows the sealed number and the fresh one side by side without forcing them to agree. Calling a second measurement a re-derivation would be the exact overclaim this whole apparatus exists to refuse. Checking this way is slower than trust and more annoying than confidence, and the slowness is the point. A receipt says what was checked and how, nothing stronger, and when a check confirmed nothing, the receipt says that too.

3. A check that can prove it can fail

A check that has never said no isn't known to be a check. It could be a working check or a rubber stamp, and from the outside you can't tell which until the day it matters. Plenty of rubber stamps get called oversight. I'd rather not build another one.

So the negative fixtures, forged inputs the verifier must reject, sit at the core of the test suite. For every way a receipt or a chain of receipts could be forged, there's a test that forges it and demands the right refusal by name. A chain here means receipts bound together in a fixed order, so changing, removing or reordering any one of them breaks the whole. Tamper with one sealed count inside a chained bundle and verification has to fail under the specific name for that tampering, CHAIN_LINK_UNVERIFIED, which says the chain link is unverified. A test that only gets a generic error counts as a failure. Reorder the receipts in a chain, drop one, or swap one in, and each sabotage has its own named failure. The ordinary test run exercises every one of those names end to end. The verifier also carries a self-test whose only job is to show, on demand, that the rejections still fire.

Checking systems drift toward yes. Deadlines want a pass. Demos want a pass. Every false alarm annoys somebody into loosening the check, so all the pressure points the same way. The only thing pushing back is proof, kept up on purpose, that the check can still say no. The named refusal is the less pleasant answer, and it's the product.

4. The boundary, crossed live

Buildlang can call out to a model over a socket, a connection between two programs, and that makes the boundary concrete enough to enforce in code. The receipt layer, the part of the compiler that issues and checks receipts, flat out refuses to emit or verify a scientific receipt over any program that observes a model, meaning any program that calls one. A scientific receipt is the ordinary kind from section 2. The refusal has its own name, which marks the run as inadmissible as evidence. That run still has value. It counts as a proposal, and a proposal gets a different document: a boundary receipt, sealed at the point where data passed between the two programs. It records only that the exchange happened at this boundary under this seal. It makes no claim that the model was right.

This month I watched the loop close live, the full trip from proposal to decision. A local model, one running on the machine itself, proposed a completion, a piece of generated text, over a real socket. The shim on the far side, a small piece of connecting code, sealed a boundary receipt over the exchange. The verifier accepted that receipt and refused a tampered copy because the seal no longer matched. Then a model-free kernel, a small checking program with no model inside it, did the scientific judging under an ordinary receipt of its own. A chain bound the proposal and the disposition into one tamper-evident bundle, and each receipt in it was re-verified on its own. Tampering with the model's receipt broke the chain by name. The proposal and the judgment ended up in one evidence bundle, kept apart, and that separation got tested from both sides.

The branch that carries this end to end, a working copy of the code kept apart from the main project, is in review as I write and hasn't been merged. So what I'm describing is a demonstration, and no shipped release contains it yet. That gap, between a demo and a release, is what this essay is about.

Update, 3 October 2026: the receipt work described here has since shipped. The Corrections section at the end names the release.

5. The stance

I use models all day, and none of this comes from suspicion of them. This essay was written with model help, and the compiler would be years further off without them. The rule is only about which layer a model's words may land in on their own authority. The answer is none, and it doesn't soften as models get better. A better proposer is a better proposer. Its role stays the same. Say a model got it right every single time. You'd only know that because something checked it every single time. The final call still has to belong to something the model doesn't control. Otherwise the evidence layer stops being one, and everything built on it inherits the difference without anyone saying so.

The rule doesn't exempt me either, and that's where it costs something. I'm a bullshitter by my own admission. A rule that only bound the machine would be awfully convenient for a guy like me. Elsewhere in the same set of tools, a preregistered confirmatory run is under way as I write. Preregistered means its plan and pass mark were written down before it started. I won't tell you how it's going, because no verdict has been computed yet, and a discipline that refuses a model's self-report has to refuse mine too. Whatever I publish from it comes with its receipts.

The thing I'm against is quieter than the loud fears about AI. It's the final call sliding over to the proposer, one convenience at a time, until the record is a transcript of whatever the most fluent party said about itself. My bet is we're further down that road than anybody wants to admit. Every mechanism in this essay exists to keep the no alive somewhere the proposer can't reach it. Models propose; oracles dispose. The receipts are public so somebody who is neither can check both. Go check them.
