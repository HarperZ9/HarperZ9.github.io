The held-out set. Learning, or remembering?

A model can learn its training examples by heart and still be lost on new ones. Pylon fits curves at her desk, keeps some points back, and shows what they reveal.

Everything the film says

The held-out set

The held-out set. Learning, or remembering?

I could learn this harbour by heart, every crate in its place. Then one night they send me to a different harbour.

Sixteen points

Here are 16 points. They come from a smooth curve, plus a little noise, the way real measurements do.

Let's fit a curve through them. A straight line misses the bend. A gentle curve follows it. And a wild one, with enough freedom, passes through every single point.

Which curve will do best on points it has never seen? (the film pauses here)

The wild one gets every point right: its error on them is about one part in a hundred billion. That's zero, apart from rounding in the arithmetic.

Points kept back

So keep some points back. 500 of them, drawn the same way, never used for fitting. That's the held-out set.

On those, the gentle curve's error is 0.33. The wild one's is 3,936. Between the points it learned it swings wildly, and past the outermost ones it shoots off the chart.

Make the curve more flexible, step by step, and the error on the training points only ever goes down. The error on the held-out points goes down, then turns around and climbs, with a few bumps on the way.

A leash

One fix is a leash: keep the wild curve, but charge it for every large number it uses.

A moderate charge brings its held-out error down from 3,936 to 0.43. That's the best of seven charges we tried, picked by looking at these same held-out points, so it flatters the leash a little. Pull too hard and it gets worse again: at the heaviest pull we tried, 0.70.

The spirals, with too few points

The bucket chain from last time learned two spirals from 400 points. Give it only 40.

It still gets every training point right. On new points it's right 78.3 percent of the time, against 99.5 with all 400.

Watch the held-out loss. It's lowest early, at step 300, and then climbs while the training loss keeps falling. Its score on new points still creeps up for a while, to 80 percent by step 1,000, but it grows ever more sure of itself, wrong answers included. After that the score stops improving, and training only tightens its grip on these forty points.

One draw is one draw

And a score from one training set is one draw. Pick five different sets of 40 training points, and the held-out score runs from 75.5 to 86.0 percent.

Even which curve looks best depends on the draw. Over two hundred draws of sixteen points, the gentle curve came out best 113 times, not every time.

The language model

The language model from the attention lesson was trained on a stream of freshly made sentences, a new batch every step. Its little grammar does repeat itself: 507 of its 32,000 training rows were exact copies of earlier ones. But no row came round more than 5 times, so it had little chance to learn its training rows by heart.

Score the finished model on rows it trained on, and its loss is about 0.63. On the next stretch of the same stream, which it didn't train on, 0.67. A small gap, not a chasm.

And its own held-out set turns out to be a little hard: 0.69, higher than five fresh draws of the same size, which range from 0.65 to 0.67.

Keep it held out

One rule makes all of this work. Once you use the held-out points to choose anything, how flexible the curve is, how long to train, they stop being held out.

So careful work keeps a third set locked away until the very end, and looks at it once.

Goodnight

Getting the practice right is not the test. Keep points back, and check on those.

What this lesson leaves out

The curve and the spirals are toy problems with known answers, so overfitting is easy to see; in real work the truth is unknown and the held-out set is the only window onto it.

Some choices in the film (the best degree, the best leash) were picked on the same held-out points they are scored on, which flatters them a little; the film says so, and the closing rule (a third set, looked at once) is the fix.
