Aliocha's reading technique for dense philosophy

Post by Aliocha (@4liocha_), 2026-10-24

What it is, and who made it

This is a reading method for dense philosophical texts, shown on Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I, question 2, articles 1 to 3. Aliocha learned it in a course she names, "Leitura e interpretação de textos clássicos". She uses it only for reading that will be assessed academically. In her words, without it she would read "2 livros por ano".

The post is the method, and the four images show it in use:

  1. a notebook page that rewrites two objections as numbered premises and conclusions;
  2. 2. to 4. the printed page, annotated with circled conjunctions, colour highlights, cut marks and margin brackets.

Parts, layer by layer

The method, as stated (observed in the text).

  1. Structure. Split each argument into premises and conclusions, and often presuppositions.
  2. Movements. Decide whether arguments form a chain, a list (number them), or a turn, where the author moves from affirmative to negative.
  3. Conjunctions. They "indicate reversal of movement or continuity": they deny or confirm what came before.
  4. Active reading. Underline the main argument and the links between ideas. The aim is to reproduce the author's whole movement from conclusion to conclusion.

The argument under the notation. Each objection in article 2 has a fixed shape: a major premise, then Ora (Latin Sed) introducing the minor premise, then Logo (Latin Ergo) introducing the conclusion. Objection 3 nests: its minor premise is itself argued ("finite and infinite have no proportion") before the conclusion.

The framework behind it

An argument has a grammar, and connectives are its function words. The method makes three structures visible:

  • Segments: premise, presupposition, conclusion.
  • Joints: the connective that opens a segment, with its role. The roles are conclusion (portanto, logo, ergo), minor (ora, scholastic sed), reversal (mas, sed, autem), continuation (além disso, praeterea) and cause (pois, quia, enim).
  • Movements: how segments link, as a chain, a list or a turn.

The colour, the cuts and the brackets are three encodings of the same tree, one per layer of attention. Reading actively means rebuilding that tree.

The same idea in distant fields

  • Logic and law: argument mapping (Toulmin's claim, data, warrant and rebuttal) and legal briefs that number premises. The scholastic article form (objections, sed contra, respondeo, replies) is the original template.
  • Linguistics: Rhetorical Structure Theory and the Penn Discourse Treebank. Both annotate discourse relations at connectives, which is the computational version of her circles.
  • Music: cadences. A conclusion connective works like a cadence, and mas works like a deceptive cadence that turns the line.
  • Code: control flow. Se is if, mas is else, logo is return. A reader's bracket is a block, and a movement is a call graph.
  • Our own work: Daylight's reading lens names techniques at spans. Articulate flags stock connectives as style. Neither yet reads the logic the connectives carry.

Dual edge

  • The lens can make weak arguments look rigorous. Marking portanto as a "conclusion" looks like validation, but a connective asserts a link; it does not prove one. The margin must say "the author marks this as following", never "this follows". That is our does-not-prove rule at the span.
  • It can be gamed. Persuasive writing borrows logical connectives to borrow authority ("therefore, clearly ..."). In Daylight the same spans that teach reading can flag connective inflation: many conclusion markers, few premises. That is a manipulation cue, and it stays behind the same gate.
  • It helps the readers the author named (2026-10-25: people who find communicating hard). It also risks replacing their reading with ours. The lens shows the joints and leaves the judgement to the reader, consistent with the canon: reveal the process, support the decision, don't make it.
