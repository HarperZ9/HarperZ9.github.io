Piers Cowburn, liquid cities from Google Earth data

Post by Piers Cowburn (@pierscowburn), 2026-10-24

What it is, and who made it

Renders of London (Westminster and the Thames) and Shibuya in which streets and buildings bend along smooth curves, as if the city were a liquid surface. Three stills and a 24 s clip.

Parts, layer by layer

  • Source layer: Google Earth photogrammetry meshes with baked facade textures (O, from the post).
  • Deformation field: smooth, large-scale; rows of buildings follow it like streamlines (O). In the clip the camera drifts with mean speed 0.092 widths per second, coherence 0.41 (M).
  • Texture: where the field shears, facade pixels stretch into streaks (O). Measured stretch share (structure-tensor coherence over 0.6) 0.16 to 0.29 across the stills (M).
  • Bend: 3.5 to 4.1 degrees per 1% of width (M); controls 2.7 to 6.6 (hand-drawn shelves, Bayer ribbons, Elettrosumma blocks, Moran canyon). The measure does not separate this work: a reported null.
  • Light: photoreal daylight render; shadows follow the bent geometry (O).

The framework behind it

Advection applied to solid geometry: positions move through a velocity field while topology is kept. The look comes from two rules: deform vertices smoothly, and do not resample textures, so they stretch where the field stretches.

The same idea in distant fields

  • Geology: folded strata; rock flows over long times and the layers record the strain.
  • Glaciology: flow bands in glaciers.
  • Cartography: cartograms and fisheye maps bend a city for a reason (population, attention).
  • Film: the city-folding scene in Inception (from memory, a reference to check, not this work's source).

Dual edge

Bending a real city is a strong image of instability. Used on a news surface it could imply damage that did not happen. The data has a licence (Google Earth terms) that limits reuse; our version should use open map data.
