Otto Piene, Red Eye Red (1977), with three colour studies

Post by Meditations in Color (@MeditateColor), 2026-10-25

What it is, and who made it

The post has two layers of authorship:

  • Otto Piene, Red Eye Red, 1977, serigraph. These details come from the post caption. Piene (1928 to 2014) co-founded the ZERO group, and he made light ballets as well as smoke and fire paintings. Those biographical facts are from memory, high confidence.
  • Three colour studies by Meditations in Color, credited "by Pixel Symphony". Each study takes the print's palette through a historical colour system:
    • Josef Albers, Homage to the Square;
    • Wada Sanzō, A Dictionary of Color Combinations;
    • the Heian court's kasane no irome, colours of layered robes.

The framework behind it

One palette, three operators. The post is a decomposition engine made by hand:

  • The source artwork supplies a palette, here 12 colours.
  • Each historical colour system acts as an operator on that palette, and each tests a different claim:
    • Relational (Albers): a colour's appearance depends on its neighbour, so hold one colour and vary its ground.
    • Combinatorial (Wada): harmony lives in small indexed sets, so enumerate pairs, triples and quadruples.
    • Layered (kasane): colour is a stack seen at its edges, and the stack is named for a time of year.

The outputs are plates, not paintings. Each makes one claim checkable. That matches the canon's method: break a reference into parts, then recombine.

The same idea in distant fields

  • Music: a scale (the palette) run through harmonic systems. The same twelve tones serve as chords (Wada), as voicings over a pedal (Albers, one note against changing bass) and as layered instrumentation (kasane).
  • Chemistry: one compound's spectrum under different solvents. The solvent shifts what the "same" colour looks like, as the ground does in Albers.
  • Typography: one glyph set at many sizes and on many grounds in a specimen sheet. Our feed sets ask for this repeatedly (GAPS item 24, the specimen sheet).
  • Data visualisation: colour-blind and contrast audits put one palette through several perceptual models, and each plate is a test.
  • Textile and lacquer: kasane is a cousin of laminated glass and of lacquer built up in coats. The colour is the sum of the layers, and the edge is where the sum shows. That links to our glass and intaglio work, where the cut edge reveals the layers.

Dual edge

  • Reused palettes fake provenance. Extracting a master's palette and running it through prestige systems lends authority to anything painted in it. The plates must say whose palette, extracted how. Our records do; a published plate set must too.
  • Simultaneous contrast is a persuasion tool. The same effect that teaches Albers can make a chart's bar look larger or a warning look milder.
  • Seasonal naming is a cultural system. Kasane names carry court meaning. Using them as decoration without that context is a thin borrowing. The study pages credit the source; our plates should cite it too.
