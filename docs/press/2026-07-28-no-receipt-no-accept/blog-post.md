# Blog post (ready to publish or adapt)

Suggested title: **No Receipt, No Accept: an argument for machines that prove themselves**

---

There is one rule under everything I build: a system is not trustworthy because the person who built it says it works.

That sounds obvious until you look at how anything actually operates. The auditor is hired by the audited. The benchmark is run by the vendor. The safety evaluation is performed by the lab whose launch depends on passing it. The proposer grades its own paper, and the paper always passes.

AI just made this old problem urgent. Machines got very good at producing expert-shaped output, and here is the asymmetry nobody prices in: generating a plausible claim collapsed to nearly free, and checking a claim stayed exactly as expensive as it always was. I call the gap review debt, and somebody always pays it, usually the person with the least power to refuse it.

I spent the last years building the answer I could build alone: a verification loop where nothing warrants itself. Every claim faces a criterion its author did not write. The check is kept as a receipt a stranger can re-run. The failed branches are kept next to the wins. And "I cannot check this" survives as a first-class verdict instead of being quietly rounded up to "probably fine," because that rounding is where most of the lying in the world actually happens.

The essay walks that machinery end to end, then follows the argument outward: who gets the other nine when the machine makes work ten times more productive, how safety cases keep landing on the exact conclusion the business model wanted, why the record is starting to eat itself, and what it would mean to open the box instead of metering it.

It is about 19,400 words, in 25 movements, with the process disclosed inside it: AI-assisted, author-directed, and checkable against the public record it came from.
