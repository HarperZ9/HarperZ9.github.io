Everything the film says

Search and sorting: the cost of looking

Search and sorting: the cost of looking.

Looking things up and putting them in order are two of the commonest jobs a computer does. Tonight Rerun counts the cost, one knock at a time.

One shell at a time

Rerun is looking for one shell in a row of 65,536. The plain way is to knock on each in turn until he finds it.

On average that took 32,921 knocks, about half the row. If the shell is not there, he knocks on all 65,536.

Halve the beach

Now suppose the shells are in order, smallest to largest. Knock on the middle one. If it is too big, the shell must be in the left half; too small, the right. Half the beach is gone in one knock.

Keep halving. For 65,536 shells it takes at most 17 knocks, and about 15 on average.

Sixty-five thousand shells, in order. How many knocks at most? (the film pauses here)

Only if they are in order

Halving only works because the shells are in order. Try it on a jumbled row of the same size and it still stops within 17 knocks, but even in its best run it missed 65,515 of the 65,536 shells that are there.

Fewer knocks is no good if the answer is wrong. So first, the shells have to be put in order.

Sorting by insertion

The simplest way: pick up each shell in turn and slide it left past every bigger one, the way many people sort a hand of cards.

On a row that is already in order, that is 65,535 comparisons for 65,536 shells, one for each shell after the first. On a row in reverse order it is 2,147,450,880. On a jumbled row, about 1,074,190,182.

Sorting by merging

A better way: split the row in half, sort each half, then merge the two sorted halves by walking along both and always taking the smaller front shell.

Each half is sorted the same way, down to single shells. On jumbled rows of 65,536, that took about 965,755 comparisons on average. Insertion took more than a thousand times as many.

How the cost grows

Double the row and see what happens. Knocking along it doubles. Halving adds one knock. Insertion sort on a jumbled row about quadruples. Merge sort a little more than doubles.

On this computer, sorting 65,536 jumbled shells took 1,151 milliseconds by insertion and 7.34 by merging.

What the count leaves out

These are counts of comparisons, not times. A real computer also pays for moving things and for memory, and the built-in sort that comes with the JavaScript engine, which counts nothing, took 2.99 milliseconds on the same row and the same computer.

But the counts tell you which way it grows, and that matters more as the row gets longer.
