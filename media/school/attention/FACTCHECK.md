## A. Bahdanau, Cho, Bengio (arXiv:1409.0473)

- First posting: v1 submitted 1 Sep 2014 (arXiv listing). The film's "2014" is correct for the preprint.
- Venue: arXiv comment reads "Accepted at ICLR 2015 as oral presentation." The PDF header reads "Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2015." Evidence.json matches.
- Fixed-length bottleneck, abstract: "we conjecture that the use of a fixed-length vector is a bottleneck." The paper presents this as a conjecture and cites Cho et al. 2014b for evidence that the basic encoder-decoder "deteriorates rapidly as the length of an input sentence increases" (sec 1).
- Does the paper use the word "attention"? Yes, once, in sec 3.1 (the same sentence is in v1 and v7): "Intuitively, this implements a mechanism of attention in the decoder. The decoder decides parts of the source sentence to pay attention to. By letting the decoder have an attention mechanism, we relieve the encoder from the burden of having to encode all information..." The title, abstract and method name use "align", "(soft-)search" and "alignment model". So "They called it attention" is partly true and mostly not: the authors used the word once as an intuition, and the field adopted it as the name.
- "look back at every word directly": the decoder computes a weighted sum of annotations for all source positions (sec 3.1: "a weighted sum of all the annotations"). Two limits. The words are source-sentence words (the sentence being translated), not words the model already wrote. The annotations are bidirectional RNN states, which carry neighbor context, not raw word vectors (sec 3.2). "Directly" is fair for the access path, loose for the content.
- Evidence.json `bahdanau2015`: claims are accurate. `does_not_prove` is accurate and consistent with the above; it could say "once, in section 3.1".

## B. Olsson 2022 and Elhage 2021

- Olsson 2022 (arXiv:2209.11895, submitted 24 Sep 2022), intro: "Mechanically, induction heads in our models are implemented by a circuit of two attention heads: the first head is a 'previous token head' which copies information from the previous token into the next token, while the second head (the actual 'induction head') uses that information to find tokens preceded by the present token."
- Olsson, section "Induction Heads": "a pair of attention heads in different layers that work together to copy or complete patterns." and "The first attention head copies information from the previous token into each token." So "one notes what came before each word" matches in meaning: the information about the previous token is written into each position, which then lets the second head match on "what happened before them." The phrase "notes" is fine for a lay film; I propose "writes into each word a note about the word before it" to make the direction of the information explicit.
- Definition (Olsson, same section): formal definition is behavioral, on a repeated random token sequence: prefix matching (attends to a token whose earlier context matched the current token) plus copying (output raises that token's logit). The pattern is [A][B] ... [A] -> [B]. The film's "finds an earlier copy of the current word and reads what followed it" is the right description.
- First found in small attention-only models: yes. Olsson: "we discovered a special kind of attention head, which we named induction heads, in two layer attention-only models" and notes they occur "in 2-layer attention-only models (but not 1-layer models)". Elhage 2021 summary: "two layer models use attention head composition to create 'induction heads'", and "In small two-layer attention-only transformers, composition seems to be primarily used for one purpose: the creation of what we call induction heads."
- Previous token head and K-composition (Elhage 2021): "The minimal way to create an induction head is to use K-composition with a previous token head to shift the key vector forward one token." Matches evidence.json claim 2.
- Olsson also states the original 2021 definition was "a bit vague" and that the 2022 paper uses a behavioral definition. Evidence.json claim 1 follows the 2022 definition; claim 2 correctly credits 2021 for the mechanism.
- Attribution "researchers at Anthropic": both papers carry Anthropic affiliation (Olsson PDF lists Anthropic; Elhage is on the Transformer Circuits Thread). I did not separately fetch Elhage's author list.

## C. Jain and Wallace 2019, Wiegreffe and Pinter 2019

- Jain and Wallace (arXiv:1902.10186, "Accepted as NAACL 2019 Long Paper"). Abstract: "learned attention weights are frequently uncorrelated with gradient-based measures of feature importance, and one can identify very different attention distributions that nonetheless yield equivalent predictions." Conclusion: "that attention consistently correlates poorly with multiple such measures ought to give pause." Scope: BiLSTM encoders mainly; the paper also tests "average" (non-recurrent) encoders and states that "attention weights in simple, feedforward (weighted average) encoders enjoy better behaviors" (sec 1). The film's "cases where very different attention gives almost the same answer" is a fair summary.
- Wiegreffe and Pinter (arXiv:1908.04626, "Accepted to EMNLP 2019"). Abstract: such a claim "depends on one's definition of explanation," and they "propose four alternative tests." They also state "prior work does not disprove the usefulness of attention mechanisms for explainability." A sentence in sec 5 says the question of whether high attention weights lead to the prediction is "ultimately left largely unanswered by prior work." Film's "others have argued about when attention does explain" is a fair summary.
- Scope: both papers study RNN-based NLP models, not transformer language models. The film does not say so; row 18 adds it.
- Evidence.json `jain2019`: the claim "in the recurrent text-classification models studied" is slightly narrow. They also studied average encoders and found them better behaved, and they covered question answering and inference tasks (the `n` field says so). Suggest: "In the recurrent (BiLSTM) models studied, attention weights are often only weakly correlated with gradient-based importance, and very different attention distributions can give equivalent predictions; simple weighted-average encoders behave better." `does_not_prove` is accurate.
- Evidence.json `wiegreffe2019`: the claim is accurate. The `does_not_prove` line "it shows the question is open" is the film's reading; the paper's own claim is stronger than "open" on one side: that the earlier experiments do not disprove attention as explanation and that the answer depends on the definition. Suggest: "That attention is an explanation; it shows prior experiments do not settle the question."
